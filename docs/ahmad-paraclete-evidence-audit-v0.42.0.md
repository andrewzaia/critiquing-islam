# CI-001 - Quran 61:6, the Johannine Paraclete and Ahmad

Source-critical evidence audit for v0.42.0, 7 November 2026.

## Research question

The exact issue is whether the *existing* Gospel of John identifies the promised Paraclete as Muhammad, or whether an earlier identifiable Gospel witness supports Jesus predicting a messenger by the proper name Ahmad. This is separate from the theological question of whether the Quran is divine revelation, and separate again from a speculative possibility that Jesus said something now unrecorded.

### Quran-first text

Quran 61:6 attributes to Jesus the words "whose name is Ahmad", using Arabic `اسْمُهُ أَحْمَدُ`. The Quranic Arabic Corpus parses Ahmad as a proper noun. This is a named assertion, not simply a generic future teacher. Quran 7:157 and 5:46-48 contain related Quranic claims about the earlier Torah and Gospel. Repeated Quranic assertions do not count as independent pre-Islamic documentary witnesses.

### The original Greek in context

| Passage | Explicit Greek or contextual feature | Interpretive constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| John 14:16 | `ἄλλον παράκλητον` - another Paraclete | A title and a promise to the disciples |
| John 14:17 | `τὸ πνεῦμα τῆς ἀληθείας` - Spirit of truth | The world cannot receive or see him; he dwells with or in the disciples |
| John 14:26 | `ὁ δὲ παράκλητος, τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον` - the Paraclete, the Holy Spirit | Explicit identification; teaching and recalling what Jesus said to his addressees |
| John 15:26-27 | Spirit from the Father testifies; disciples also testify | Distinction between Spirit and first-generation human witnesses |
| John 16:7-15 | Speaks what is heard, guides, glorifies Jesus | Generic received-speech similarity must be weighed against whole discourse |
| 1 John 2:1 | `παράκλητον` of Jesus as advocate | The noun functions as a role designation rather than a unique proper name |

The Liddell-Scott-Jones Greek dictionary distinguishes *parakletos* (legal assistant, advocate, one called to help) from *periklutos* (famous, renowned). The lexical existence of the second word is not evidence of its presence in an original manuscript of John. No pre-Islamic Johannine witness documented in this study substitutes it for the Paraclete designation.

The masculine demonstrative used in John 16 does not identify a biological male. Its grammar and the nearby masculine title do not overcome John 14:26's explicit identification of the same referent as the Holy Spirit.

### Pre-Islamic material witnesses

P66 (P. Bodmer II) is an early fragmentary papyrus of John's Gospel with a scholarly transcription from the International Greek New Testament Project. CSNTM assigns it broadly to the second or third century; manuscript dating remains a scholarly assessment, not the original composition date of John. P75 is a separate manuscript of Luke and John, dated by the Vatican Library to the early third century and now substantially incomplete; the IGNTP supplies a transcription. Passage-level claims must be checked against surviving folios and editorial lacunae.

Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus preserve a Greek Gospel tradition centuries before the Quran. A public manuscript comparison of John 14:26 retains the Paraclete and Holy Spirit identification in pre-Islamic codices. These witnesses attest text, not the original circumstances of every utterance attributed to Jesus.

John 14:17 has a documented variant `ἐστίν` versus `ἔσται`, present versus future tense of the Spirit's presence. Neither reading names Ahmad. Real textual variation is acknowledged without converting it into an unattested hypothetical personal name.

### Earliest documented Muslim reception

Sean W. Anthony, *Muhammad, Menahem, and the Paraclete* (2016), explores Ibn Ishaq's Arabic adaptation of John and the influence of Christian Palestinian Aramaic transmission and late-antique expectation. This is a substantial historical study of how a Christian scriptural motif was adapted in early Islamic argument. It is not an eyewitness report from Jesus' lifetime and does not establish a Greek Gospel autograph containing Ahmad.

The Christian narratives in Acts 2:1-4 and John 20:22 link a gift of the Holy Spirit to Jesus' immediate disciples. Those are earlier literary traditions, not independent proof that supernatural occurrences happened exactly as described. They are relevant to how the authors understood fulfilment.

### Burdens of proof and decisive verdict

**[Verified]** Quran 61:6 attributes a named Ahmad prediction to Jesus.

**[Verified]** The original Greek text of John 14:26 identifies the Paraclete as the Holy Spirit, and the broader farewell discourse characterises him as the Spirit of truth dwelling with or in the disciples.

**[Verified]** Pre-Islamic Greek witnesses preserve this Gospel tradition, with passage-specific gaps and minor variants; the conjecture *periklutos* in place of *parakletos* is not established by the cited physical record.

**[Verified]** Early Islamic adaptation of the Paraclete discourse is documented and studied, but belongs to later reception history.

**Not demonstrated:** An actual first-century Jesus prophecy naming Ahmad, a Greek original replaced by an otherwise unattested synonym, or a deliberate documented change of John 14:26 from a human messenger to the Holy Spirit.

**Text-first verdict:** The claim that John's existing Paraclete passages identify Muhammad fails. John names the Holy Spirit and provides descriptions that do not fit a later human messenger.

**Historical verdict, on the balance of probabilities:** The surviving documentary evidence strongly favours the Johannine Holy Spirit interpretation. The separate claim that Jesus delivered another unrecorded prophecy remains unverified; its logical possibility is not positive historical evidence.

**Falsification criteria:** An independently dateable early manuscript or pre-Islamic quotation naming Ahmad in this context, a genuine earlier Greek variant or a credible textual stemma demonstrating a specific replacement could alter the conclusion. A later interpretive paraphrase or linguistic guess would not.
