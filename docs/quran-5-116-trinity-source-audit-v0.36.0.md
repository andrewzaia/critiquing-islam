# CI-007 - Quran 5:116, the Trinity and Marian devotion

Evidence audit for v0.36.0, November 2026.

## Proposition and original-language observations

The question is whether Quran 5:116 and the nearby condemnation of three correctly portray the pre-Islamic Christian Trinity, or instead express a distinct polemic against Marian ritual devotion.

- Quran 5:116 names Jesus and his mother as two deities *ilahayni* besides God, using a masculine dual. The verse does not contain a formal list of the three Christian persons.
- Quran 5:73 contains *thalithu thalathatin*, 'third of three', without naming the three. Quran 4:171 forbids saying three and calls Christ God's messenger, word and a spirit from him. The three Quranic passages are related in theme but not identical in grammatical content.
- Quran 5:72-77 establishes the surrounding polemic against identifying God with Christ, insists Jesus and Mary ate food and condemns religious worship besides God.
- Quran 5:112-120 places 5:116 after a table-from-heaven scene and presents Jesus as denying that he instructed deification. A relation to Christian ritual practice is a hypothesis requiring evidence, not an explicitly named audience.
- Quran 9:31 is relevant because it accuses religious leaders of being taken as lords without itself proving those leaders had been formally inserted into a doctrine of God.

Original Arabic morphology was checked against Quranic Arabic Corpus entries for 5:116, 5:73, 4:171 and 5:75. The Arabic wording is preserved as actual script in CI-007 rather than using transliteration as a substitute for an Arabic edition.

## Earlier Christian creedal witnesses

- Nicaea (325): the creed expressly professes one God, the Son begotten and not made and the Holy Spirit, without defining Mary as divine.
- Constantinople (381): the Greek creed names the Father, Son and Holy Spirit, including the Spirit's joint worship with Father and Son. Mary is mentioned in the incarnation account as the Virgin, not as a person of the Trinity.
- The two creeds are not textually identical. The fuller 381 creed is commonly called Nicene in modern liturgical use, which should not erase the 325/381 distinction.
- Matthew 28:19 and 2 Corinthians 13:14 provide earlier triadic Christian formulas, without setting out all later fourth-century metaphysical vocabulary.
- John 1:14 asserts that the Word became flesh; consequently the fact that Jesus ate food cannot by itself disprove a Christology explicitly affirming his humanity.
- The Council of Ephesus (431) debates the title Theotokos in connection with Christ's identity. Richard Price (2019) cautions against claiming a separate Ephesus decree directly defining this title. The title means God-bearer, not that Mary herself is divine.

A creed documents a central public doctrine, not every act of Christian worship in all communities. Creed chronology and doctrinal truth are separate assertions.

## The strongest alternative: devotion as a cultic target

Epiphanius of Salamis, *Panarion* 79, provides a fourth-century Christian polemic about women making ritual offerings associated with Mary. His report and its Greek-language transmission are primary textual evidence of an accusation about practice. He was not an independent member of the alleged group, and his report does not furnish a seventh-century group confession identifying Mary as divine.

Mohsen Goudarzi, 'The Qur'an's Cultic Trinity: Marian Piety in Late Antiquity and the Qur'an', *Journal of Late Antiquity* 18 (2025), DOI 10.1353/jla.2025.a969359, argues that the Quran's charge probably concerns lived Marian devotion rather than a technical creed. The currently consulted evidence was the published abstract, not an independent page-by-page inspection of the full journal article. The argument is relevant and substantive, but the ancient target still needs identification.

Zahra Mohagheghian, 'Mariolatry in the Qur'an', *Studies in Interreligious Dialogue* 34 (2024), DOI 10.2143/SID.34.2.3293939, critiques a proposed historical identification of the group accused in Quran 5:116. This too is cited through its published abstract.

The strongest practice-focused reading is historically plausible. It neither proves an officially Marian Trinity nor independently establishes the specific belief of a named Christian community in the Quran's time. Christian distinctions between honouring a holy person and worshipping God are attested before Islam; an analyst may criticise an actual practice without changing the terms of the original creed.

## Comparative verdicts under the balance of probabilities

**Text-first verdict:** Quran 5:116 accuses people of treating Jesus and Mary as deities. It does not explicitly define the Trinity's three persons. A direct assertion that the Quran literally enumerates a Mary-inclusive Trinity is not substantiated by the verse's Arabic.

**Historical verdict:** The normative Christian Trinity demonstrably excludes Mary. If the Quran's accusation is presented as a description of the orthodox creed, that claim fails. Historically attested disputes about Marian devotion provide a credible alternative explanation of the polemic, but do not demonstrate that Mary was a member of the orthodox Trinity or that a specific surviving sect confessed her full deity in Muhammad's environment.

**Theological scope:** Showing a mismatch between the Quranic criticism and the orthodox creed does not establish the supernatural truth of Trinitarian theology. Conversely, showing some practical devotion does not itself verify divine authorship of the Quranic accusation.

**What could change the verdict:** A securely pre-Islamic first-person confession treating Mary as God, evidence of a continuing sect relevant to the Quran's audience, or unambiguous wording enumerating a Mary-inclusive Trinity could change the relevant factual claims. Neither a later exegetical assertion nor an allegation by an opposing writer alone provides that chain.
