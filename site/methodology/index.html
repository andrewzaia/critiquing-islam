EDITORIAL PRINCIPLES

How we test a claim.

A credible critique must be more than a confident statement. It must show its work, delimit its conclusions and remain open to correction.

01

State the claim precisely

Identify the claim's exact wording and distinguish the source's own position from interpretations attributed to it.

02

Begin with the Quranic wording

For claims about the Quran, start with its actual Arabic wording, relevant translations, surrounding verses and cross-references. Quote or paraphrase it accurately. Later hadith and tafsir cannot add words to a verse or prove that a disputed reading is historically correct.

03

Check context

Read surrounding passages, identify translation uncertainty and consider relevant historical circumstances.

04

Test explanations, not authority

When an interpreter proposes a reconciliation, identify the extra premise and ask where the text itself establishes it. Record a relevant defence accurately, but neither reputation, tradition nor later agreement counts as independent proof that the explanation is true.

05

Calibrate the conclusion

Separate direct textual findings from historical inference and matters that the evidence cannot settle.

06

Maintain a correction trail

Correct factual errors transparently and preserve a readable record of meaningful editorial changes.

07

Assign each source its proper weight

A Quranic verse establishes the Quranic assertion under examination. Hadith documents attributed traditions, not independent authentication of miracles, revelation or unstated Quranic exceptions. Tafsir records interpretations, not missing original wording. Manuscripts establish readings they physically preserve.

08

Separate sources from independent witnesses

Several quotations from one author or copied tradition do not count as several independent witnesses. Assess textual dependence, date, provenance and whether the source directly observes the event. Repeating the same reference must not inflate the evidence base.

09

Audit every reference

A numbered source must have an identifiable destination and an in-text citation supporting a relevant statement. Published articles are automatically checked for broken citation anchors, sequential IDs and duplicated sources. These checks validate structural integrity, not the historical truth of a source's contents or the availability of every external website.

Quran-first research standard

For a critique of the Quran, the text itself is the subject, not later attempts to reconcile it. A finding must distinguish (1) what the Quran expressly says; (2) what follows from those words and their context; (3) what independent historical or linguistic evidence establishes; and (4) what an interpreter proposes but the Quran does not itself specify. A possible reconciliation is not an evidenced resolution merely because an exegete wrote it down. When the text leaves an issue open, record that limitation instead of claiming the Quran says something it does not.

Hadith and commentary: limited evidentiary roles

Hadith may be cited where a question concerns the actual contents of Islamic tradition, such as reports of a miracle, recitation, compilation or later belief. Its authenticity must then be assessed consistently, including where a report complicates a criticism. Neither accepting a useful report uncritically nor rejecting a contrary report solely for its conclusion is a valid method. Later scholars' explanations may be noted briefly to define a claim or expose its assumptions; they do not enjoy interpretive immunity and are not independent proof of a divine event, lost manuscript, abrogation, genealogy or historical fact. Contemporary independent witnesses, manuscripts and verifiable language evidence take precedence when the issue is historical.

Distortion claims and an equal standard

Quran 3:78 condemns presenting words as scripture when they are not, and 2:79 criticises falsely attributing writings to God. These accusations make fidelity to the stated words especially important when examining Quranic arguments about earlier revelation. They do not establish that every later religious interpreter is intentionally deceptive. The test is narrower and verifiable: does the added explanation actually follow from the text, and is there evidence for its additional claims? Quran 3:78 and Quran 2:79.

Manuscript and translation evidence

When a historical argument depends on the wording of earlier biblical scripture, check surviving manuscripts, their dates, catalogue identifiers, transcription status and known variants. Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus are datable fourth-century Greek manuscript witnesses; the Septuagint is an earlier Greek translation tradition, not a single codex. Cite a manuscript only where the relevant book and passage survive. Do not confuse proof that a reading existed before Islam with proof of complete textual invariance or theological truth. View the manuscript source register.

Three separate historical tests

A datable biblical manuscript can establish that a text or reading existed before Islam, independently of any later religious claim. That does not, by itself, demonstrate that the manuscript preserves the exact original wording, that its account of events is historically accurate, or that its theological assertions are true. Arguments alleging textual corruption must identify the disputed passage, a plausible chronology of change and relevant evidence. Likewise, an argument about the Quran's instruction to consult earlier scripture must distinguish the scripture that physically circulated from the original Injil understood in Islamic theology.

Testing claims of comprehensive explanation

Evaluate Quranic statements describing the revelation as detailed or explanatory (12:111, 16:89 and 6:114) in their full textual context. Later commentators may offer proposed limits on their scope, but those limits need support from the Quranic wording rather than deference to the commentator. It is legitimate to ask why a specific purported alteration to an earlier scripture is not identified and evidenced. Do not misrepresent the verses as an explicit promise to document every manuscript variant, or assume that the later Quranic disagreement is itself independent historical evidence of textual corruption.

Conclusive findings require a defined claim

Every study should give a direct verdict about the proposition tested. When direct texts contradict an identification, state that the proposed identification fails. When a historical assertion lacks corroboration, state that it has not met its burden of evidence. When a matter remains genuinely disputed, state precisely what is and is not established. Distinguish legal interpretation, moral judgement, historical reconstruction and theological commitments rather than imposing the same standard on each.

Source-level meaning and transmission chains

Do not assume that two hadith records constitute two independent eyewitnesses. Identify the earliest shared companion and any common intermediaries, and distinguish clauses found in one version but absent from another. Similarly, cite scholars for their actual arguments rather than for convenient conclusions that their publications do not establish. The published source-content review records representative claim-by-claim checks. Automated attribution checks guard against citation drift but cannot certify historical events or theological propositions.

Classify alleged contradictions precisely

The research library distinguishes historical non-corroboration, textual tension, legal qualification, literary dependence and scientific non-verification. A strict contradiction requires mutually exclusive claims with matching scope and conditions. An absence of independent evidence does not logically prove that a small group never existed, nor does a supernatural assertion become scientific simply because a natural phenomenon is observed.

Literary parallels and chronology

When alleging that one text depends on another, document the actual passages, the source language and edition, meaningful similarities and differences, material manuscript dates, proposed composition dates and the possible transmission routes. A later dated recension cannot automatically be cited as the direct source of a traditionally earlier passage. Shared oral or written traditions may explain parallels without direct manuscript copying. Distinguish a published original-script excerpt from a new translation or independent manuscript collation.

Our scope

Critiquing Islam examines religions, doctrines, texts, historical claims and arguments. It does not advocate harassment, discrimination or hostility toward Muslims as people. Evidence and reasoning, not personal identity, are the object of critique.
