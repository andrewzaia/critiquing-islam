05 / PRIMARY MANUSCRIPT WITNESSES

Codex Vaticanus

Fourth century CE (Vatican Library, Vat. gr. 1209). A surviving Greek Bible with substantial portions of the Greek Old Testament and New Testament. Its original surviving New Testament contains the Gospels and major Pauline letters, important for studying early Christian portrayals of Jesus. Some books and leaves are missing or represented by later replacement material. In particular, the original surviving manuscript must not be cited as a witness for Revelation.

The Vatican Library's catalogue establishes manuscript provenance, date and scope. The digital viewer allows inspection of the surviving pages; individual readings require page-level examination.

06 / PRIMARY MANUSCRIPT WITNESSES

Codex Sinaiticus

Mid-fourth century CE. A surviving Greek Bible whose complete New Testament can be examined in the international Codex Sinaiticus Project. Its Greek text and corrections can be studied through linked manuscript photographs and scholarly transcription. It provides a material witness to Christian writings centuries before the Quran, including the Gospels and Revelation.

For a claim concerning a specific verse, the manuscript image and transcription should be consulted directly, including any corrections or variants.

07 / ANCIENT TRANSLATION TRADITIONS

Septuagint (Greek Jewish scriptures)

Beginning in the third century BCE. The Septuagint began as a Greek translation of the Jewish Torah, with other books translated over time. It is a textual and translation tradition, not one surviving volume comparable to Vaticanus or Sinaiticus. Later Christian Greek Bibles preserve forms of that tradition, while variations among Greek and Hebrew witnesses must be assessed passage by passage.

This evidence is relevant when a New Testament writer interprets Greek Jewish scripture, such as the use of Isaiah's language in discussions of Christ. It is evidence about the textual background, not by itself proof of any theological claim.

09 / TEXTUAL CONTINUITY AND VARIANTS

Earlier papyri and specific readings

Late second- and early third-century papyri, including P66 for John, extend the manuscript record before the great fourth-century codices. These witnesses allow comparisons of whether particular passages were in circulation long before Islam. The principal Johannine claims about the Word and Jesus' identity persist in modern New Testaments, although spelling, wording and larger textual variants are documented across the tradition.

The longer Trinitarian formula associated with 1 John 5:7-8 is an example of a later textual addition in some received translations. Its disputed textual history shows why the argument must be made passage by passage rather than declaring all copies absolutely identical or all scriptures wholly corrupt.

08 / EVIDENCE LIMITATIONS

What the witnesses establish

These materials can establish that particular biblical texts and readings existed in a datable form before Islam, and can illuminate earlier textual transmission. They do not prove uniform wording across every copy, recover the original autograph with certainty, or demonstrate the supernatural truth of statements recorded in a manuscript.

Our method is to distinguish manuscript dating, direct inspection of an individual reading, historical inference and theological interpretation. The manuscripts are cited only for content they actually preserve.
