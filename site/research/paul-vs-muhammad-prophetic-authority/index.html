BIBLICAL TEST OF AUTHORITY

Paul claims to be commissioned by the risen Jesus. Muhammad claims a later revelation that rejects essential claims of the apostolic gospel. The Biblical test of a contrary gospel and the historical evidence for each claim are examined separately.

01 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

A Biblical verdict requires clearly stated theological premises

The first question is not whether Paul and Muhammad both used the same title. Paul identifies himself as an apostle commissioned through Jesus Christ, whereas Quran 33:40 calls Muhammad the messenger of God and seal of the prophets. Their asserted offices and the centuries in which they lived differ. The actual contest concerns their incompatible claims to convey God's authoritative teaching about Jesus. [1] [19]

Critiquing Islam adopts an explicitly Biblical-Christian doctrinal framework. If Jesus is God and genuinely commissioned Paul, that commission is divine. But the historical fact that Paul claimed such a revelation and the theological claim that the risen divine Jesus actually appeared are separate propositions. Stating the distinction improves the critique without withholding the Christian conclusion. [3] [18]

The evidence test asks which propositions have early source support and what each revelation says. A later claim cannot authenticate itself by announcing that earlier scriptures were wrong. A private experience of either man cannot be established as supernatural merely because its narrator was convinced. [21] [24]

02 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Which standards does the Bible itself prescribe?

Deuteronomy 13:1-5 warns that even an impressive sign cannot justify allegiance contrary to the God of Israel. Deuteronomy 18:18-22 tests a person speaking presumptuously in God's name. These texts originally address ancient Israel, not Muhammad by name, so their Christian application here is explicitly theological and interpretive rather than an invented direct prophecy about Islam. [6] [7]

Galatians 1:6-9 warns that even an angel from heaven must not be accepted if proclaiming a different gospel. First John 4:1-3 commands believers to test spiritual claims by their confession concerning Jesus; 2 Corinthians 11 warns against another Jesus and a different gospel. These are concrete earlier Christian criteria against which the Quranic message can be scrutinised. [5] [8] [9]

The essential work is showing a genuine contradiction in the contents of those messages. Merely asserting that a later claimant had an angelic experience does not, by itself, prove deceit or an evil spirit. [5] [23]

03 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Distinguish encounters, compositions and physical witnesses

Paul's generally accepted letters are dated to the first-century generation after Jesus' death. The Quran originated in the seventh-century Islamic movement. Papyrus 46 is a much later physical copy of several Pauline letters, while early Quranic manuscripts physically attest Quranic material before the later hadith collections. A manuscript date is not the date of the alleged supernatural encounter. [33] [34]

The Dome of the Rock inscriptions dated 72 AH, about 691-692 CE, show developed Islamic teaching about Muhammad and Jesus in monumental form. This is strong evidence against the assertion that Islamic confession only appeared in late medieval literature, but it does not authenticate Gabriel as a historical heavenly visitor. [35] [36]

Chronology matters most when later revelation contradicts a particular earlier event. The first-century death tradition and later Roman testimony weigh heavily against a seventh-century literal denial of Jesus' execution, although earlier evidence cannot by itself prove the supernatural resurrection. [4] [26]

04 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Paul describes an apostolic commission from Jesus

Galatians 1:1 identifies Paul as an apostle whose commission is through Jesus Christ and God, not appointed by other humans. In 1 Corinthians 9:1 he asks whether he has seen Jesus. These are direct literary claims within generally accepted Pauline letters. They attest what Paul asserted but do not independently certify that the risen Jesus appeared. [1] [3]

Paul says he had persecuted the early church before a revelatory change and subsequent mission. That conversion is substantial biographical testimony in his own voice. The Christian theological interpretation is that Christ called him; a historian can identify the claim and its effects without adjudicating its supernatural cause. [1] [18]

It would therefore be inaccurate to call Paul simply a competing final prophet. His self-described task was to proclaim the gospel of Jesus and to take that message to Gentiles. [1]

05 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Muhammad's messenger status is asserted by the Quran

Quran 33:40 names Muhammad God's messenger and seal of the prophets, and 3:144 speaks of him as a messenger among earlier messengers. These statements give Muhammad a broader prophetic succession and finality claim than Paul's specific assertion of apostleship under Jesus. [19] [20]

The Quran's assertion is a fact about its text. It is not yet independent evidence that God issued the mission. The investigation must ask whether the message is consistent with earlier revelation under the Biblical standard and whether claims about historical events match material witnesses. [25] [27]

The Quran also honours earlier revelation. But naming previous prophets does not demonstrate that a later text accurately preserves the earlier teaching when the writings themselves disagree. [27] [28]

06 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Paul's first-person claimed revelation is early but not self-authenticating

Galatians 1:11-17 says Paul received the gospel through revelation of Jesus Christ and describes his former hostility to the church. First Corinthians 15:8 claims an appearance of Jesus to Paul after earlier appearances. These are early written statements from the claimant, not first-century audio or independent verification of the heavenly encounter. [1] [4]

Second Corinthians 12:1-7 reports visionary experience while admitting uncertainty about being in or out of the body. That text should not automatically be merged with the original conversion claim to manufacture a single fully described event. [18]

A Christian may affirm Paul's divine commission because of the identity of the risen Jesus and the continuity of his gospel. Historical analysis separately evaluates Paul's earliest writing, conversion and contacts rather than making the experience indisputable from self-testimony alone. [1] [18]

07 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The Quran and later biographies describe different layers of Muhammad's revelation

Quran 53:1-18 and 81:19-25 speak of a powerful visionary messenger and defend the trustworthiness of revelation; Quran 2:97 names Gabriel as the one who brought revelation. It is an interpretive overreach to combine all the images into one fully documented encounter at Hira without distinguishing the texts' contexts. [21] [22] [23]

Sahih al-Bukhari 3 preserves a later account of Muhammad's fear, Khadija's reassurance and Waraqa's identification of the visitor by comparison with Moses. Even if this happened as narrated, Waraqa would have inferred the visitor's identity from Muhammad's story; he was not independently observing the heavenly messenger. [24]

Neither an assertion that an angel spoke nor a later interpreter's identification proves divine commissioning. The Biblical gospel test asks whether the content conforms to earlier revelation, and the historical test asks what independently dated evidence corroborates the event. [5] [24]

08 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The source record for Paul is early, with a real authorship debate

Most historical-critical scholarship distinguishes seven letters commonly accepted as Paul's authentic writings, including Galatians, Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians and Philippians. Other letters attributed to Paul are debated. This study accordingly gives greatest weight to his generally accepted letters rather than treating every later epistle and Acts narrative as Paul's own eyewitness testimony. [1] [15]

A 2026 study by Nina Livesey challenges the usual confidence in the seven-letter collection and argues for a possible later pseudonymous origin. It is a minority scholarly proposal whose existence deserves disclosure; it cannot be promoted into a settled consensus or dismissed without examining evidence. [38]

The dates of surviving manuscript copies must also be distinguished from composition dates. Papyrus 46 is an early copy, not a first-century autograph, yet its existence independently constrains any claim that the Pauline tradition first appeared in response to Islam. [33]

09 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Paul reports meeting leaders of Jesus' original movement

In Galatians 1:18-19 Paul says he visited Cephas and met James, called the Lord's brother. In Galatians 2:1-10 he reports a later encounter with recognised Jerusalem leaders who accepted his Gentile mission. These details directly challenge the allegation that Paul operated without contact with the original Jesus movement. [1] [2]

Paul is arguing his own case, so this is still an interested first-person report. It does not prove that every Jerusalem leader signed off on every theological argument in Paul's later letters. Nevertheless, a specific claimed contact is stronger evidence than simply assuming complete separation between Paul and the earliest followers. [2]

Historical continuity is not identity in every doctrinal detail, and the source should neither be dismissed because it helps Paul nor treated as an infallible institutional endorsement. [1]

10 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The disagreement with Cephas is not optional evidence

Galatians 2:11-14 portrays Paul opposing Cephas at Antioch after Cephas withdrew from eating with Gentile believers. Paul interprets the withdrawal as inconsistent with their gospel. This is positive evidence of an early conflict over religious practice and community boundaries. A Biblical critique must report it, rather than claiming Paul and Peter never disagreed. [2]

The passage's immediate concern is Gentile table fellowship, not an alternative account in which Cephas denied Jesus' crucifixion or foretold Muhammad. Those broader claims require additional early witnesses. A disagreement on one issue does not establish that the two men preached completely different saviours. [2]

Acts 15 offers a later narrative of Jerusalem deliberations about Gentiles. It must be weighed against Paul's earlier letter instead of automatically harmonised or assumed worthless. [10]

11 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Acts reports Paul's calling but remains a later narrative witness

Acts 9, 22 and 26 narrate Paul's Damascus encounter in different settings and with details that need close comparison. These are narrative speeches in a later Christian book, not three independent first-person letters from Paul or three eyewitness depositions by his companions. [11]

The Christian theological reading accepts that the risen Christ commissioned Paul. A historian, however, should place greater initial weight on what Paul wrote in Galatians and Corinthians concerning his own experience, while investigating whether Acts accurately preserves his memory. [1] [3]

A critique of Islam does not benefit from exaggerating these accounts as identical, independent witnesses to a miracle. The more defensible claim is that Paul's own early writing and later Christian narrative both attest a commission tradition, while its supernatural cause is a separate proposition. [11]

12 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Early Islam is historically attested without proving divine inspiration

Early Quranic material, dated Arabic inscriptions and non-Muslim references studied in modern scholarship corroborate a seventh-century religious movement associated with Muhammad. It would be erroneous to describe the Prophet's entire historical existence as an invention of later hadith compilers. [34] [35] [37]

Marcus Milwright's study of the Dome of the Rock's dated 72 AH inscriptions supplies concrete early material culture, including Islamic claims about Jesus and messengerhood. The inscriptions prove early public profession of such beliefs; they are neither a transcript of Muhammad's private visions nor eyewitness evidence that an angel selected him. [36]

Sean Anthony's research distinguishes recoverable early history from later biographical narration. A strong Christian critique concedes the historical movement and concentrates its rejection on the truth of its conflicting religious and historical claims. [37]

13 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

An exalted understanding of Jesus is found in Paul's earliest material

First Corinthians 8:6 confesses one God, the Father, and one Lord Jesus Christ, linking creation and worship to both. Philippians 2:5-11 describes Jesus' humiliation, death by crucifixion and subsequent exaltation. These are first-century Christian claims predating the Quran by centuries. [12] [13]

Within Biblical Christianity these texts contribute to the conclusion that Jesus shares divine identity. The precise theological relationship between these formulas and later conciliar language is debated, and the formulas alone do not give a laboratory proof of divine nature. But the notion that a divine status for Jesus appeared only after Islam is inconsistent with the textual chronology. [12] [13]

A claim that Paul invented all exalted Christology must provide earlier evidence of a completely different Jesus movement, not merely point to a later Quranic denial and call it older. [1] [27]

14 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The Quran explicitly rejects the Christian divine-Christ confession

Quran 4:171 describes Jesus as Messiah and messenger but rejects "Three" and divine sonship language. Quran 5:72-75 rejects saying the Messiah is God, while surah 112 insists that God does not beget. The doctrinal difference from Christian confession is substantive and comes from the Quran's own text, not solely later Muslim commentary. [27] [28] [29]

The Quran's argument against divine begetting must not be misrepresented as if every Christian believes God biologically fathered Jesus. The historical Christian doctrine and the Quranic polemic should be compared in their strongest actual forms. Nonetheless, the Quran rejects the central Christian identification of Jesus as divine. [12] [27]

Under the project's Biblical standard, that rejection directly conflicts with the prior apostolic gospel. A later scripture cannot establish that earlier writings were altered merely by disagreeing with them. A historical corruption hypothesis requires dated textual witnesses and a plausible mechanism of change. [5] [33]

15 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Jesus' crucifixion is a decisive historical comparison

First Corinthians 15:3-4 says Paul received and handed on the tradition that Christ died and was buried. Tacitus, a later non-Christian Roman source, reports Christus executed under Pontius Pilate. The Gospel Passion narratives also identify Jesus. These sources vary in dependence and purpose but materially predate Islam. [4] [26]

Quran 4:157 denies killing and crucifixion by the speakers under condemnation. Its dominant traditional interpretation rejects the historical crucifixion; a narrower agency-only reading can avoid directly denying execution by the Romans but needs its own contextual defence. The Quran does not name a substitute. [25]

The early death tradition strongly supports Jesus' execution under Pilate and weighs against a literal substitution narrative. This confirms a central historical premise of Paul's proclamation without independently proving a supernatural resurrection. [4] [26]

16 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The charge that Paul invented the crucifixion ignores what he says he received

In 1 Corinthians 15 Paul uses language of receiving and passing on a tradition that Jesus died, was buried and was reportedly seen after death. He identifies Cephas and James among appearance claimants, but the list is Paul's account of their claims, not several independently preserved witness statements. [4]

The historical combination of early death proclamation and Paul's reported contact with Cephas and James undermines a sweeping assertion that Paul alone concocted the whole story centuries later. The exact origin and age of every part of the received formula remain legitimate areas for study. [1] [4]

The resurrection is a separate religious claim. A Christian may affirm it; a historian can verify that it was proclaimed very early without deriving a supernatural conclusion solely from the proclamation. [4]

17 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Paul and James discuss faith and works in different settings

Romans 3:21-31 discusses justification by faith apart from works of law yet says faith does not abolish the law. James 2:14-26 challenges professed belief without deeds. These texts are in tension as phrased, but they address different questions and contexts. Their exact definitions of justification, works and faith must be compared before concluding strict logical contradiction or effortless harmony. [15] [16]

Paul himself distinguishes in 1 Corinthians 7:10-12 between a command he attributes to the Lord and guidance he gives to a different situation. This undermines a claim that every instruction in his letters is presented as a word-for-word quotation spoken by Jesus before his death. [17]

A Biblical critique should engage Pauline arguments rather than caricature him as rejecting every moral obligation. Likewise, later Islamic law should not be confused automatically with the wording of the Quran. [15]

18 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Was Paul departing from the historical Jesus?

Mark 12:28-34 presents Jesus affirming one God and the commandments to love God and neighbour. Paul's confession in 1 Corinthians 8:6 also affirms one God while incorporating the Lord Jesus into its formula. This is evidence for development of Christological language within a Jewish monotheistic setting, not straightforward proof that Paul abandoned monotheism. [12] [14]

Paul's Gentile mission and conflict with Cephas show that early Christianity debated practical consequences of Jesus' message. A claim that Paul created a different religion must specify a teaching from the historical Jesus that Paul demonstrably replaced, and show its early independent source. [2] [14]

The seventh-century Quran can be evaluated as a competing theological assertion, but it cannot independently serve as an eyewitness transcript correcting Paul's first-century account. [27] [28]

19 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The Biblical angel warning applies to contradictory content

Galatians 1:8-9 warns Christians not to accept a contrary gospel even if an angel claims to deliver it. A common Christian critique applies this warning to Muhammad's claimed Gabriel revelation, but the argument only works after establishing a real doctrinal contradiction. An angelic claim alone does not prove deception. [5]

The Quran rejects the divine Christ confessed in early Christian literature and is traditionally read as denying his crucifixion. Those are substantive conflicting propositions, rather than differences of ritual custom or Arabic vocabulary. Thus Muhammad's later message fails the test of the earlier Christian gospel under the expressly Biblical premise of this website. [27] [28] [25]

Historically, neither Paul's claimed risen-Jesus encounter nor Muhammad's claimed Gabriel encounter can be independently certified as supernatural solely from the narrators' assertions. Biblical rejection of the later gospel is a theological verdict supported by documentary comparison, not a claimed forensic detection of a fallen angel. [5] [18]

20 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The supposed Gospel announcement of Ahmad lacks an earlier witness

Quran 61:6 attributes to Jesus a prophecy naming Ahmad, and 7:157 claims the unlettered prophet is found written in earlier scriptures. These are Quranic assertions rather than independent first-century records. [30] [32]

John 14:16-26 explicitly identifies the promised Paraclete as the Holy Spirit and describes the Spirit's work among Jesus' disciples. That pre-Islamic Johannine language does not name Muhammad. The claim that an original text read as a personal name rather than the surviving Greek title requires a dated variant or other concrete witness. [31]

CI-001 separately examines the Greek and manuscripts. For Paul versus Muhammad, the narrow result is that this alleged pre-Islamic Gospel endorsement of Muhammad does not satisfy the historical evidentiary burden. The logical possibility of a completely unrecorded prophecy is not affirmative evidence. [30] [31]

22 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

The strongest objections deserve direct answers

Paul never travelled with Jesus before the crucifixion. This is correct; his claimed authority depends on the risen-Jesus commission, not a claim to have been part of Jesus' original travelling disciples. Paul's direct testimony about his own experience is early, but is not independent proof of its supernatural origin. [1] [3]

Paul and Cephas disagreed over Gentile table fellowship. This is also correct; it cannot be suppressed to present the earliest church as uniformly agreed on every practice, but it is not positive evidence that Cephas supported an Islamic non-crucifixion account. [2]

Muhammad's movement has early historical witnesses and inscriptions. Correct again; those corroborate the existence of the religious movement and its public proclamations, not a divine commission. [35] [36]

The Quran might claim to restore a pre-Pauline Jesus. That explanation would need earlier independent testimony demonstrating the alleged lost original, not only a later source announcing that it has corrected the Christians. [25] [37]

23 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

What evidence would require a revision?

A demonstrably early independent source establishing that Jesus was not crucified, or that first-century Jerusalem Christians rejected the crucifixion while Paul uniquely created it, would substantially change the historical judgement. No such evidence is supplied by a later theological assertion or a physically unattested substitution proposal. [4] [25] [26]

More secure evidence about the authorship of the Pauline letters could refine how much of Paul's record is genuinely first person. The scholarly discussion is live, including a recent minority proposal, so the study avoids a categorical claim that no authorial dispute exists. [38]

A new independent witness to either man's claimed supernatural encounter would need scrutiny of provenance and what the witness actually observed. Neither a hypothetical hidden manuscript nor a self-authenticating divine claim meets the present burden. [18] [24]

24 / CLAIM AND EVIDENCE

Biblical verdict and historical verdict must be stated separately

**Biblical theological verdict:** Given the project's stated premises that Jesus is divine and that the apostolic gospel is the relevant standard, Paul's commission is coherent with that framework, whereas Muhammad's later revelation contradicts central earlier Christian teachings about Jesus and cannot pass the Galatians test of a different gospel. This is a defined doctrinal judgement, not a purportedly neutral proof of divinity. [5] [12] [13] [27]

**Historical verdict:** Paul's testimony to Jesus' death and his claimed dealings with Cephas and James are early and fit the best-supported account of the Roman crucifixion. Muhammad and his early Islamic movement are historically attested, but the claimed divine origin of his revelations is not independently established. No historical method also directly verifies Paul's claimed risen-Jesus appearance simply by reading his letters. [1] [4] [26] [36]

**Conclusion:** A claim that Muhammad corrected Paul's alleged fabrication fails the documentary burden on its central historical test. Under the openly Biblical standard, Muhammad's competing revelation fails the earlier gospel test. Criticism remains directed at the truth of doctrines and texts, not at Muslims as people. [5] [25]
