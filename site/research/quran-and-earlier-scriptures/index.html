Does the Quran affirm the Torah and Gospel?

When scripture is described as guidance and its readers are told to consult it, what does that mean for claims of corruption?

01 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Can a book be both consulted and dismissed?

DOCUMENTED TEXTUAL TENSION

The Quran directs Jews and Christians to earlier scripture and depicts those scriptures as containing divine guidance. A blanket assertion that those communities possessed no meaningful or accessible divine revelation must explain why the Quran addresses them this way. The passages do not, by themselves, establish that every word of today's biblical canon was preserved unchanged.

The central question is whether the Quran's affirmation of earlier revelation can be reconciled with the claim that the earlier scriptures had become unreliable by Muhammad's time. The answer requires a more precise test than the simple assertion that every existing biblical text must be either wholly authentic or wholly false.

02 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

The Torah is presented as available and containing judgement

Quran 5:43 asks why some Jews seek Muhammad's judgement when they already have the Torah, within which is God's judgement. [1] The grammatical and practical premise matters: the Torah is presented as something the people possess and that contains a source of judgement.

This is stronger than a merely nostalgic reference to an unrecoverable ancient revelation. It is an argument directed to contemporaries who are depicted as having access to the Torah. The verse does not say that every passage in every manuscript is free of alteration, but neither does it say that the Torah available to these people is entirely useless.

03 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

The People of the Gospel are told to judge by it

Quran 5:46 describes the Gospel as containing guidance and light. The following verse directs the People of the Gospel to judge by what God revealed within it. [2]

That instruction raises a concrete question: what identifiable written or received revelation could those people use to obey it? A theory that the genuine Gospel had disappeared altogether must supply an account of how this command remained actionable.

It is important not to treat the existence of scriptures as an open historical question. By the time Muhammad preached, the canonical Gospels and other Christian writings had circulated for centuries. Their pre-Islamic existence is established independently by datable manuscripts, including Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus. [8] [9] What remains open is whether the Quran endorses those extant writings in whole, in part, or a conceptually distinct original revelation.

However, identifying the Quranic Injil with the whole later Christian biblical canon, or even automatically with all four canonical Gospels, requires an additional argument. The text's affirmation of a Gospel does not settle that identity by definition.

04 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Quran 5:48 says more than confirmation

Quran 5:48 describes the Quran as confirming earlier scripture and also as muhaymin over it, variously rendered as guardian, overseer or supreme authority. [3] Classical commentators, including Ibn Kathir, understand this as permitting the Quran to adjudicate which portions of previous scripture are accepted as true. [7]

A later interpretation may distinguish the original revelation from surviving Christian books, but it cannot supply the missing historical evidence. The Quran does not identify the supposed changed manuscript or produce an earlier reading merely by claiming authority over prior texts. The testable question remains: which specific passages were altered, when, and on what evidence?

Textual standard: Quran 5:43 and 5:46-48 describe existing Torah and Gospel material in terms of divine judgement or guidance. Quran 2:79 and 3:78 accuse certain people of falsification or misrepresentation. Those Quranic claims must be compared directly; the opinion of a later commentator cannot erase one set of verses or authenticate a specific proposed biblical alteration. [1] [2] [3] [5] [6]

For the word-by-word Arabic argument, the root and morphology, and a review of al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and Ibn Manzur, see Al-Muhaymin: guardian, witness or judge?. That study evaluates what the claimed authority can and cannot independently prove.

05 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

What do the often-cited corruption verses actually condemn?

Quran 2:79 condemns people who write material with their own hands and falsely present it as from God. Quran 3:78 condemns a group that distorts scripture with its tongues so that listeners mistake what it says. [5] [6]

Those passages are real evidence for accusations of falsification or misrepresentation. Their wording, however, does not amount to an explicit assertion that the entire Torah and Gospel possessed by Jews and Christians had already been completely rewritten. The specific misconduct described should not be silently enlarged into a universal statement.

A crucial distinction

Corruption of a specific passage, misrepresentation of a text and total loss of an original revelation are different propositions. An argument proving one does not automatically prove all three.

06 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

What Christian and Jewish texts existed before the Quran?

Written manuscript evidence matters because a corruption claim about a particular passage is a historical claim. Codex Vaticanus (Vat. gr. 1209) is catalogued by the Vatican Library as a fourth-century Greek biblical manuscript, containing substantial Old and New Testament material. Its surviving original contents are incomplete, but it is a direct witness to Christian biblical books circulating centuries before Islam. [8]

Codex Sinaiticus, also fourth century, preserves the complete New Testament in Greek, accessible through digitised manuscripts and scholarly transcriptions. It demonstrates the existence of a substantial Christian biblical collection before Muhammad. Neither codex is a first-century autograph, and readings should be verified in the relevant surviving pages before alleging that a particular sentence was identical across all copies. [9]

The Septuagint began as a pre-Christian Jewish translation of the Torah into Greek, later extending to other scriptural books. It establishes a much earlier Greek textual tradition for Israel's scriptures, with individual differences from Hebrew and among Greek copies. It is not itself a manuscript of the New Testament and therefore cannot independently prove the wording of Jesus' words in John. [10]

Historical propositionWhat the manuscripts establish
Did substantial biblical writings exist before Islam?Yes. Fourth-century Greek codices preserve major parts of the Old and New Testaments, centuries before Muhammad.
Were the exact surviving codices in the hands of the Christians addressed by the Quran?Not established by their survival. Their existence witnesses a wider pre-Islamic Christian textual tradition, not an individual community's library.
Are all surviving readings necessarily identical to their first composition?No. Manuscript age alone does not eliminate textual variants, earlier changes, or interpretive differences.
Does the Quran refer to the same four canonical Gospel books?This must be established by interpreting the Quranic Injil alongside the historical evidence; the manuscripts do not decide it.

This establishes a significant constraint on the chronology of any proposed corruption. A claim that John's divine language was added after Muhammad, for example, cannot be defended by ignoring the earlier Greek Gospel manuscript tradition. A claim that the language arose before those codices remains a different historical hypothesis, requiring its own evidence. [8] [9]

What these witnesses can and cannot resolve

They establish that biblical texts existed long before the Quran and provide testable evidence against claims of an exclusively post-Islamic fabrication. They cannot by themselves decide whether the Quranic Injil refers to the entire fourfold Gospel collection, nor prove that every earlier reading was preserved unchanged. A proposed corruption of John or another text requires precise manuscript comparisons.

07 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Do the same central claims appear before Islam and in modern Bibles?

STRONG DOCUMENTARY FINDING

The central Christian passages at issue were not invented after Muhammad. The Gospel of John, including the narrative identifying Jesus with divine status, is attested in Greek manuscripts centuries before Islam. Its principal claims can still be read in modern New Testaments. This does not mean every ancient and modern biblical manuscript has identical wording.

Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus are fourth-century witnesses to John's Gospel. The earlier papyrus P66 provides an additional witness to substantial parts of John, including its opening and the concluding Thomas episode, commonly dated around 200 CE with scholarly uncertainty about the exact date. [8] [9] [11]

John 1:1-14 presents the Word as divine and as having become flesh. John 20:24-29 depicts Thomas addressing the risen Jesus as Lord and God. These passages remain in the modern canonical Gospel of John. [17] [18] For this specific line of Christian teaching, the documented witnesses make a proposed post-Islamic invention untenable. This is a statement about the survival of particular readings, not a claim of verbatim agreement in every line of every copy.

The broader textual tradition spread among Christian communities using Greek and other languages. A hypothesis of a late coordinated replacement across established textual streams requires a plausible historical mechanism and evidence from the manuscript record. Conversely, copies can share ancestors, so a change arising before a widespread copying tradition could appear in many later descendants without any worldwide conspiracy.

What a corruption claim must identify

Which biblical passage or teaching is alleged to be corrupt? Does the claim concern an alteration before the earliest surviving witnesses, a later insertion, a mistranslation or a disputed interpretation? The mechanism and date matter when testing whether documentary evidence supports the claim, even though the Quran's instruction to its seventh-century audience remains the central theological question.

A useful caution is the longer Trinitarian clause in 1 John 5:7-8 found in some older translations. It is absent from the early Greek evidence and is generally treated as a later addition. Such cases demonstrate that genuine variants exist and can be detected. They do not make John 1:1 or John 20:28 post-Islamic inventions. [16]

08 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

If the Quran explains all things, why is the alleged Gospel corruption not specified?

Quran 12:111 describes the Quran as confirming earlier revelation and giving a detailed explanation of all things. Quran 16:89 similarly calls it an explanation of all things, and 6:114 describes it as a book explained in detail. [12] [13] [14]

These statements create an appropriate question for a critique of the Quran's authority: if a teaching about Jesus as central as his divinity is being corrected, why is no named biblical manuscript, identifiable Johannine passage or traceable history of its alleged alteration supplied? The Quran's condemnation of particular forms of misrepresentation cannot substitute for external evidence that these specific pre-Islamic Christian claims were inserted into the text.

It is important to state the objection correctly. The absence of a manuscript history is not, on its own, a formal contradiction with the Quran's claim to be detailed. Ibn Kathir interprets the comprehensive language of 12:111 primarily in relation to guidance, permissible and prohibited conduct, religious obligations and theology. The text does not unambiguously promise to supply a complete history of textual criticism. [15]

The resulting criticism concerns explanatory adequacy and historical verification. A person may accept the Quran as a theological criterion over earlier writings according to Quran 5:48, but to persuade someone who does not already accept its authority, an allegation that a specific ancient reading was corrupted requires evidence beyond the later revelation's disagreement with it. [3]

Distinguish textual charges

Quran 3:78 refers to verbal misrepresentation. Quran 2:79 explicitly alleges that some people wrote material and falsely attributed it to God. The latter cannot fairly be reduced to misinterpretation alone, but neither verse identifies a wholesale, coordinated replacement of the four Christian Gospels. [5] [6]

09 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

The Arabic of Quran 5:43 describes a Torah already in their possession

Quran 5:43 uses عِندَهُمُ التَّوْرَاةُ, meaning that the Torah is with the addressees, and فِيهَا حُكْمُ اللَّهِ, that God's judgement is in it. The original-language corpus corroborates the grammar and pronouns. The verse is not merely a recollection of something God once said to Moses but an argument about the Torah a community can consult. [1] [19]

That statement is incompatible with the strongest blanket thesis that the Torah available to Muhammad's contemporaries possessed no accessible divine judgement whatsoever. It does not certify every line or every copy. A coherent partial-corruption account remains possible, but must explain which material was genuine and how the addressees could recognise it. [1]

A claim that the Torah's wording was comprehensively replaced before Muhammad becomes a positive historical claim requiring a proposed date, readings and documentary evidence. The Quran's own appeal to contemporary possession weighs against treating total loss as an established fact. [1] [8]

10 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Quran 5:47 instructs the People of the Gospel to judge by it

In Quran 5:47 the phrase wal-yahkum ahlu l-injili directs the People of the Gospel to judge by what God has revealed in that Gospel. The Quranic Arabic Corpus identifies the imperative particle and jussive verb, and the direct object of reference to what was revealed within it. This is an operative religious command rather than merely praise for a vanished historical document. [2] [20]

The important qualification is bima anzala Allahu fihi, what God revealed therein. It does not assert that the entire later Christian canon is automatically identical to the original revelation. Nevertheless, a theory that no usable Gospel survived has to explain how the addressed Christians were expected to obey the command. [2]

The canonical four Gospels demonstrably predate Islam as physical Greek manuscript collections. This establishes a historically available Christian scriptural tradition without resolving whether the Quran's singular Injil means the fourfold corpus, a message preached by Jesus, or some narrower original revelation. Any proposed lost original remains a claim requiring independent substantiation. [8] [9] [31]

11 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Quran 5:68 and 3:93 assume usable earlier scripture

Quran 5:68 tells the People of the Book that they must uphold the Torah, Gospel and what has been revealed to them. An instruction to uphold texts is difficult to reconcile with an assertion that no usable earlier revelation existed among the audience. A narrow appeal to surviving genuine material is possible; a categorical disappearance thesis lacks the necessary practical explanation. [24]

Quran 3:93 goes further in a particular legal dispute: bring the Torah and recite it. This is an invitation to produce a text publicly, not a proposal to reconstruct an inaccessible autograph. It presupposes an identifiable Torah against which an assertion can be tested. [25]

Neither verse states that the Torah and Gospel existed in one standardised modern edition or that every transmission was perfect. But both strengthen the cumulative evidence that the Quran views earlier scripture as something held, used or read by contemporaries rather than as merely hypothetical lost sources. [1] [2] [24] [25]

12 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

The original 5:48 claims both confirmation and authority

Quran 5:48 joins مُصَدِّقًا, confirming, with مُهَيْمِنًا عَلَيْهِ, guardian or authority over earlier scripture. The Arabic Corpus's grammatical annotation identifies both as active participles. Ignoring confirmation or ignoring oversight produces a selective reading, not a full reading of the verse. [3] [21]

An authority may in principle correct genuinely altered texts while confirming authentic contents, so these concepts do not create a formal contradiction just by occurring together. But asserting a superior theological criterion is different from demonstrating the earlier readings were historically rewritten. Manuscripts and independent earlier sources remain necessary to test the latter. [3]

If the Quran's own assertion of authority is treated as the only proof that a contradicting earlier Gospel passage was corrupted, the argument assumes the conclusion being investigated. Our separate study of al-Muhaymin examines its lexical and interpretive issues; CI-005 examines its practical interaction with physical manuscripts. [3] [21]

13 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Quran 2:79 genuinely describes written forgery

Quran 2:79 condemns people who write a document with their hands and then claim it comes from God. It is not defensible to insist that every Quranic charge of corruption refers only to mistaken interpretation: this one explicitly alleges an act of writing and false attribution. The Arabic Corpus confirms the relevant verbal forms. [5] [22]

Its scope nevertheless matters. The verse does not identify which extant biblical book was rewritten, specify an altered reading, date the alleged action, describe its spread, or state that every Torah and Gospel manuscript was replaced. A written fraud by some people is not automatically a claim about every historical copy. [5]

The strongest case would identify the alleged forged content and an earlier independent witness disproving it. In the absence of such a comparison, 2:79 demonstrates a Quranic accusation of textual forgery but does not establish the much larger historical thesis of complete biblical corruption. [5] [27] [28]

14 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Quran 3:78 differentiates misleading speech from the actual Book

Quran 3:78 alleges that a group twists its tongues concerning scripture so listeners will think words are from the Book when they are not. The verse distinguishes material genuinely in the Book from material falsely made to sound scriptural. It is therefore an error to convert its specific spoken distortion into proof of altered ancient physical codices. [6] [23]

Other verses, including Quran 5:13-15, accuse people of moving words from their proper places, forgetting a portion and concealing material. They introduce related but different allegations. A precise critique does not merge every act of concealment, speech or writing into one undocumented wholesale replacement operation. [26]

The fact that 2:79 describes written fraud should also not be hidden: the Quran can refer to distinct behaviours in different passages. Fair source analysis names the behaviour, the group and the scale that the original wording supports before asking whether an external witness verifies it. [5] [6] [26]

15 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Ancient Torah witnesses exist and differ in real ways

Hila Dayfani's peer-reviewed analysis of 4Q11 (4QpaleoGen-Exod) examines a fragmentary Genesis-Exodus manuscript from Qumran dated on palaeographic grounds to the second or first century BCE. It witnesses a version of Exodus long before Islam, while its manuscript evidence includes identifiable variants. [27]

Lawrence Schiffman's analysis of 4Q22 (4QpaleoExodm) describes a pre-Samaritan Exodus textual form with expansions drawn from parallel Torah passages. Such expansions are important evidence against simplistic declarations that every Hebrew Torah copy always used exactly identical words. Their chronology also refutes claims that the Torah books themselves were first composed by later Islamic-era Jewish communities. [28]

The two scrolls do not prove that the Torah goes back word-for-word to Moses' actual lifetime. They also cannot establish a coordinated post-Islamic corruption of a Torah already witnessed many centuries earlier. Claims about manuscript dating, textual history and actual ancient events must remain separate. [27] [28]

16 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

The Great Isaiah Scroll is pre-Christian Hebrew evidence, not the Torah

The Israel Museum's Great Isaiah Scroll, 1QIsaa, is a substantial Hebrew manuscript copied around the second century BCE and commonly dated approximately 125 BCE. The museum's digital collection provides access to the surviving primary witness. Isaiah belongs to the Prophets, not to the five books of the Torah, so this scroll should not be misrepresented as a Torah manuscript. [29]

Its importance is comparative: a scriptural text that later Christians interpreted in connection with Jesus was transmitted before Christianity and many centuries before Islam. Variants in spelling or particular readings can be assessed rather than assumed away. That is evidence of a real earlier Jewish textual tradition. [29]

The manuscript does not authenticate every historical prediction or prove that the complete modern Bible was already assembled in the first century BCE. It does impose a firm chronological limit on claims that Isaiah as a book was fabricated only after Muhammad. [29]

17 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Codex Alexandrinus adds a fifth-century Greek witness

Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus are fourth-century Greek Christian biblical codices. Both preserve substantial Gospel material before Islam. Codex Sinaiticus contains the entire New Testament but has surviving Old Testament losses and extensive annotations or corrections. None is an apostolic autograph, and Codex Vaticanus lacks portions of the later New Testament canon. [8] [9]

Codex Alexandrinus, Royal MS 1 D VIII, is independently catalogued at the British Library as a fifth-century Greek New Testament witness. These physically datable codices set a strong outer chronological limit against a proposed post-Islamic invention of the Gospel books they contain. [30]

The witnesses are not equivalent to independent eyewitness accounts of Jesus' life: they attest earlier wording and distribution, not event historicity. Their shared ancestry and corrections must be evaluated where a proposed alteration concerns an exact verse. Nevertheless, copies made centuries before Muhammad cannot be explained away as a subsequent invention. [8] [9] [30]

18 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Papyrus 66 supplies an earlier Johannine Gospel witness

Papyrus 66, P. Bodmer II, is a substantial Greek manuscript of John's Gospel from approximately the late second or third century. Its survival extends the physical evidence for Johannine material back before the great fourth-century codices. This is a manuscript date, not the demonstrated date of John's composition or of the narrated events. [31]

The Johannine prologue presents the Word in divine terms and says it became flesh, while John 20:28 records Thomas addressing Jesus as his Lord and his God. A fourth-century Codex Sinaiticus page containing John 20:28 can be inspected in the official manuscript edition. These are not theological sentences first inserted after the rise of Islam. [17] [18] [32]

The manuscript evidence does not establish that John supplies historically true theology, and an earlier alteration before P66 remains logically conceivable. Such an earlier corruption proposal requires positive evidence of an earlier alternative reading, not simply the later Quran's disagreement with John's testimony. [31] [32]

19 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Documented variants matter, but require passage-level analysis

The longer Trinitarian wording of 1 John 5:7-8 in certain later translations is a recognised textual-critical problem. Galiza and Reeve's analysis supplies a comparison of the history of that wording. Other well-known cases include the longer ending of Mark and John 7:53-8:11. The presence of genuine variants prevents any claim of perfect identity among all Christian manuscripts. [16]

These cases show the proper historical procedure: cite the exact line, examine older and later witnesses, identify the change and date its appearance where possible. Merely repeating that the Bible has variants does not establish deliberate corruption of unrelated passages such as John 1:1 or John 20:28. [16] [17] [18] [32]

A critic of Islam should apply the same test to the Quran's own text and historical transmission. Rejecting every variation as a change of doctrine, or treating every later copy as an independent witness, would undermine the rigorous standards used on this site. [27] [31]

20 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

Predictions about Muhammad demand an actual earlier source

Quran 7:157 describes a prophet whose characteristics are found with earlier communities in the Torah and Gospel. Quran 61:6 claims Jesus predicted a messenger named Ahmad. These are direct Quranic assertions that can be compared with dated Jewish and Christian witnesses. [33] [34]

Neither assertion by itself identifies a surviving pre-Islamic Gospel manuscript containing the Arabic name Ahmad or establishes that such a sentence was deliberately removed from copies around the world. The relevant historical burden is to identify a prior text, its date and wording, and the mechanism by which it disappeared if forgery is alleged. [33] [34]

The specific arguments for Muhammad in earlier scriptures belong to CI-011 and the separate Paraclete investigation. Here the relevant inference is that a claimed lost prophecy cannot simultaneously function as independently verified evidence of biblical corruption. [27] [30]

22 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

What a historical corruption hypothesis would need to show

A testable corruption allegation must specify the altered passage, the original reading, the period of change, independent earlier witnesses or citations, the plausible method of transmission and the source's connection to the actual Torah or Gospel communities addressed. Without those details, a hypothesis of universal rewriting can fit virtually any evidence and therefore explains very little. [1] [2] [27]

For a claim that John 20:28 was created after Islam, the fourth-century Codex Sinaiticus manuscript directly contradicts the proposed late date. For a proposed change before the earliest surviving copies, the burden shifts: identify evidence of an earlier form. P66 and other codices constrain chronology but cannot logically rule out all earlier alterations. [31] [32]

A proposed original Gospel entirely distinct from every known pre-Islamic Gospel book may be possible at the level of abstract belief; without dated testimony to that specific book, it is not an equally supported historical alternative. Quran 5:47's command intensifies the question of what people were meant to read. [2] [20]

23 / PRIMARY TEXT AND MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE

What follows from the evidence?

The Quran treats the Torah and Gospel as significant sources of divine guidance for communities in its own setting. This directly challenges a categorical claim that the scriptures available to those communities could contain no accessible divine judgement or guidance.

The surviving fourth-century codices decisively establish that much of the biblical textual tradition predates Islam. Thus a proposed post-Islamic invention of those books is contradicted by manuscript evidence. But the historical existence of a written text and the absence of every earlier textual change are not the same claim. [8] [9]

This creates a focused dilemma for particular corruption arguments. If the Gospel that the Quran praises in 5:46-47 was substantially available to its audience, an Islamic account must explain which teachings were genuinely revealed and how Christian readers could distinguish them from alleged corruption. If instead the authentic Injil had been lost or was not equivalent to the Christian writings then in use, the account must explain how the command to judge by the revelation in that Gospel remained meaningful. [2]

Quran 5:48 supplies an internal theological response by making the Quran an authority over previous scripture, so the dilemma is not a self-proving logical contradiction. However, that claim of authority cannot serve as independent historical evidence that a particular pre-Islamic sentence was fabricated. Specific assertions of corruption still require textual or historical support. [3] [7]

The surviving pre-Islamic Greek witnesses contain the relevant Christian writings, and the principal passages remain in today's New Testament. Therefore an assertion that these teachings were introduced only after Muhammad is inconsistent with the manuscript chronology. The Quran's claims to detailed explanation heighten the question of what specific evidence supports its correction, although they do not logically require a complete manuscript catalogue. [8] [9] [12] [13]

Verdict: A blanket corruption allegation fails without evidence of the proposed change. Pre-Islamic manuscripts establish the written Christian tradition centuries before Muhammad, and Quran 5:46-48 addresses an earlier Gospel as guidance. Calling specific surviving readings corrupt requires identifying and evidencing a change, not simply citing the Quran's later disagreement.

Historical verdict, on the balance of probabilities: Ancient Torah scrolls and Christian Gospel manuscripts establish scriptural traditions that predate the Quran by centuries. The surviving copies show real textual variation, but no source examined here demonstrates a comprehensive replacement of the Torah and Gospel texts held by the Quran's intended audience. Hypothetical unknown earlier readings are not equivalent to independently documented variants. The burden remains with any allegation identifying an actual corrupted passage, its earlier reading and the historical mechanism of alteration. [27] [28] [31]

EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATIONVerified scriptural affirmation; interpretation disputedThe affirmation is explicit. The degree of textual preservation it implies is a separate question.

Quran 10:94 makes a conditional appeal to people who had been reading earlier scripture. This is evidence that the Quran uses earlier scriptural readers as possible witnesses to knowledge, although classical interpreters differ over the address and its rhetorical function. The appeal is difficult to reconcile with a claim that every accessible earlier textual witness had become wholly useless, but it does not alone certify every surviving biblical reading. [4]

For the historical question of whether Jesus was executed, and the evidence predating Quran 4:157, see CI-002, the crucifixion in competing accounts. The mere fact of a later disagreement does not establish that a pre-Islamic Gospel manuscript was rewritten.
