RESEARCH / CI-007

Does the Quran describe the Trinity accurately?

The Quran rebukes deifying Jesus and Mary. How closely does that criticism correspond to the central Christian creed?

01 / DEFINE THE QUESTION

Mary appears in a condemnation of deification

TEXTUAL FINDING

Quran 5:116 describes Jesus and Mary being taken as deities besides God. That is not the Trinity defined by the Nicene Creed. The verse does not explicitly say that Mary is a member of the Christian Trinity, so this mismatch must not be inflated into a claim that the Quran formally defines the Trinity as God, Jesus and Mary.

A fair test separates two propositions: that the Quran condemns the deification of Mary and Jesus, and that it accurately represents orthodox Christianity's account of the Trinity. Those propositions are not the same.

02 / QURANIC PASSAGES

What do 5:116, 5:73 and 4:171 actually say?

In Quran 5:116, God asks Jesus whether he instructed people to take him and his mother as deities besides God. The verse presents Jesus rejecting such a claim. [1] Quran 5:73 condemns those who describe God as a third of three, and 4:171 warns the People of the Book against saying three. [2] [3]

Quran 5:75 identifies Jesus as a messenger and points out that Jesus and Mary ate food. [4] This fits the polemical emphasis on their humanity. These passages together show a sustained rejection of divine status being accorded to Jesus, and 5:116 extends that rejection to Mary.

None of the cited Quranic sentences explicitly provides the standard creedal list Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Equally, 5:116 does not state, in those words, that the Trinity consists of Mary, Jesus and God.

03 / THE PRIMARY CHRISTIAN CREED

What does Trinitarian Christianity profess?

The Nicene Creed professes one God, identifies Jesus Christ as the Son and confesses the Holy Spirit as Lord. The three persons named in traditional Trinitarian doctrine are the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Mary is described as the woman from whom Christ was born, not as a divine person. [5]

In Luke 1:46-47, Mary directs praise to God rather than describing herself as a deity. [6] Catholic and Orthodox traditions give Mary exceptional honour, but honouring or seeking the intercession of a saint is not identical to declaring that saint to be God. The distinction between veneration and worship matters for evaluating a doctrinal representation.

What the Creed actually establishes

It establishes the normative Trinitarian formulation, not that every historical Christian behaved consistently with it. An accusation against a particular form of practice therefore needs evidence of that practice.

04 / INTERPRETIVE OPTIONS

A criticism of practice, or a misunderstanding of doctrine?

One reading takes 5:116 as targeting people who treated Jesus and Mary as divine in practice, regardless of their formal creed. Under that reading it need not be a technical definition of the Trinity at all. The narrower interpretation is textually possible because the verse asks about making them deities; it does not use the word Trinity. [1]

A different reading connects 5:116 with the adjacent Quranic criticism of those who say three, arguing that the text misunderstands the people it is addressing. The juxtaposition is relevant, but the passages do not explicitly equate Mary's deification with the Christian three persons. That stronger identification requires evidence from interpretation and historical context, not just repetition of 5:116.

The resulting question is evidentiary: if the criticism aims at Christianity's central creed, it misses that creed. If it targets a particular Marian devotion thought to cross into worship, the historical target must be identified and demonstrated.

There is a further distinction within the Bible itself. Matthew 28:19 gives the baptismal formula invoking Father, Son and Holy Spirit, while Quran 112 emphasises God's uniqueness and rejection of divine offspring. Those passages represent substantively different theological formulations; this conflict should be assessed directly rather than implying the Nicene Creed names Mary. [7] [8]

The precise textual failure occurs when someone presents Quran 5:116 as an accurate formal definition of the orthodox Trinity. That claim does not meet the Christian primary texts. A different charge, that particular people gave Mary excessive reverence, requires actual historical evidence for their practices and cannot be decided simply by restating a formal creed.

05 / THE ARABIC DOES NOT ACTUALLY DEFINE THE TRINITY

The Arabic does not actually define the Trinity

Quran 5:116 uses the dual expression ilahayn, speaking of Jesus and his mother as two deities besides God. Quran 5:73 and 4:171 use expressions concerning three, but these are different passages without an explicit statement that Mary is one of the three persons. This distinction is decisive for evaluating what the Quran does and does not claim. [1] [2] [3]

It is legitimate to criticise a modern assertion that 5:116 accurately summarises Nicene Christianity, because Nicene Christianity does not deify Mary. It is not legitimate to assert that 5:116 literally reads "the Christian Trinity consists of God, Mary and Jesus." The wording does not say that. The stronger inquiry asks what practice the verse targets and whether identifiable contemporaneous Christians actually practised it.

This prevents a weak criticism from obscuring a stronger one: why does an alleged correction of Christian theology concentrate on a form of deification that the ordinary Christian creed rejects, while challenging the actual Father-Son-Spirit formulation elsewhere without naming its three persons?

06 / THE MOST SERIOUS ISLAMIC COUNTER-READING IS ABOUT PRACTICE

The most serious Islamic counter-reading is about practice

Quran 9:31 accuses some People of the Book of treating their religious leaders as lords apart from God. That language demonstrates that Quranic polemic can describe what it considers excessive deference as a form of taking someone as a lord, without alleging that adherents formally wrote that person into a creedal definition of God. The same possibility must be considered for Quran 5:116. [9]

An Islamic explanatory article published with Quran 5:116 explicitly distinguishes the verse from a technical description of the Trinity and proposes that it criticises extravagant Marian veneration. This is a substantive counterargument, although the existence of a modern defence does not independently identify a seventh-century target or establish that a particular group worshipped Mary as a goddess. [10]

The criticism then becomes historically testable: which sect, region, form of devotion, language and date are being described? How is worship as a deity distinguished from petitioning a saint for intercession? A polemical allegation cannot be validated merely by defining every practice the critic dislikes as literal deity-worship.

07 / AN ANCIENT SECT REPORT IS NOT PROOF OF A WIDESPREAD CHRISTIAN DOCTRINE

An ancient sect report is not proof of a widespread Christian doctrine

A common Muslim defence appeals to Epiphanius's report of a group sometimes called Collyridians who allegedly offered cakes to Mary. This is potentially relevant evidence that an ancient Christian controversialist accused a particular group of an unacceptable Marian rite. It is not a surviving confession of faith issued by the accused group, nor is the alleged practice equivalent without further argument to worship of Mary as a member of the Trinity. The Islamic explanatory article cites this historical proposal. [10]

Even if the report is accepted, several further questions remain: was the group present among the Christians actually addressed by the Quran, did its members treat Mary as ontologically divine, how extensive was it, and was the criticism meant for such a local practice rather than mainstream Christianity? The evidence presently cited does not settle all of these questions.

This is the proper burden of proof for a narrower historical defence. It is more careful than declaring that no pre-Islamic Christian ever excessively venerated Mary, but it also prevents a disputed and geographically limited report from being used as proof that Christianity's creed named Mary as God.

08 / THE DOCTRINAL COMPARISON MUST REMAIN ANCHORED IN EARLIER EVIDENCE

The doctrinal comparison must remain anchored in earlier evidence

The Nicene Creed explicitly identifies Father, Son and Holy Spirit and speaks of Mary's role in the incarnation without describing her as a divine person. Matthew 28:19 likewise presents a baptismal invocation of Father, Son and Holy Spirit. These are identifiable Christian witnesses to the principal doctrine being challenged. [5] [7]

Quran 5:75 reasons from the fact that Jesus and Mary ate food. This may serve as a criticism of attributing divine status to human beings, but it does not specifically establish that Christian theology believed Mary was divine. Nor does an argument from Jesus' human characteristics, by itself, refute the Christian doctrine that the incarnation includes a real human nature. [4]

Evidence assessment: there is a verified mismatch between the Quran's Mary-and-Jesus deity accusation and the Nicene creed; it is undemonstrated that Quran 5:116 is intended as a literal listing of the Trinity. The historically meaningful challenge is whether the allegedly condemned form of belief can be independently identified and shown to be relevant to the Quran's audience.

09 / CONCLUSION

The mismatch is real; the identification must be precise

The Christian Trinity does not consist of God, Jesus and Mary. The Nicene Creed clearly identifies Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Quran 5:116 instead condemns the taking of Jesus and Mary as deities, an accusation that does not describe the orthodox creed. [1] [5]

It is therefore incorrect to claim that the deification of Mary is a feature of standard Trinitarian teaching. It would also be incorrect to assert, solely from 5:116, that the Quran explicitly defines the Trinity as a three-person combination including Mary. The argument's strength lies in identifying exactly what the Quran criticises and testing whether the claimed Christian target fits.

Verdict: The claimed portrayal fails as a description of Nicene Christianity. If Quran 5:116 is offered as an account of orthodox Trinitarian doctrine, it does not match the Creed, which identifies Father, Son and Holy Spirit, not Mary. Because 5:116 does not explicitly define the Trinity, the broader claim that the Quran states Mary belongs to it remains unproven.
