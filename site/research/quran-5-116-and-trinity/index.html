RESEARCH / CI-007

Does the Quran describe the Trinity accurately?

The Quran rebukes deifying Jesus and Mary. How closely does that criticism correspond to the central Christian creed?

01 / DEFINE THE QUESTION

Mary appears in a condemnation of deification

TEXTUAL FINDING

Quran 5:116 describes Jesus and Mary being taken as deities besides God. That is not the Trinity defined by the Nicene Creed. The verse does not explicitly say that Mary is a member of the Christian Trinity, so this mismatch must not be inflated into a claim that the Quran formally defines the Trinity as God, Jesus and Mary.

A fair test separates two propositions: that the Quran condemns the deification of Mary and Jesus, and that it accurately represents orthodox Christianity's account of the Trinity. Those propositions are not the same.

02 / QURANIC PASSAGES

What do 5:116, 5:73 and 4:171 actually say?

In Quran 5:116, God asks Jesus whether he instructed people to take him and his mother as deities besides God. The verse presents Jesus rejecting such a claim. [1] Quran 5:73 condemns those who describe God as a third of three, and 4:171 warns the People of the Book against saying three. [2] [3]

Quran 5:75 identifies Jesus as a messenger and points out that Jesus and Mary ate food. [4] This fits the polemical emphasis on their humanity. These passages together show a sustained rejection of divine status being accorded to Jesus, and 5:116 extends that rejection to Mary.

None of the cited Quranic sentences explicitly provides the standard creedal list Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Equally, 5:116 does not state, in those words, that the Trinity consists of Mary, Jesus and God.

03 / THE PRIMARY CHRISTIAN CREED

What does Trinitarian Christianity profess?

The Nicene Creed professes one God, identifies Jesus Christ as the Son and confesses the Holy Spirit as Lord. The three persons named in traditional Trinitarian doctrine are the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Mary is described as the woman from whom Christ was born, not as a divine person. [5]

In Luke 1:46-47, Mary directs praise to God rather than describing herself as a deity. [6] Catholic and Orthodox traditions give Mary exceptional honour, but honouring or seeking the intercession of a saint is not identical to declaring that saint to be God. The distinction between veneration and worship matters for evaluating a doctrinal representation.

What the Creed actually establishes

It establishes the normative Trinitarian formulation, not that every historical Christian behaved consistently with it. An accusation against a particular form of practice therefore needs evidence of that practice.

04 / INTERPRETIVE OPTIONS

A criticism of practice, or a misunderstanding of doctrine?

The wording of Quran 5:116 allows a separate question about excessive devotion to Mary because it does not name the Trinity. But the verse itself identifies no specific group, date or rite. Treating an unnamed minority practice as the intended target requires outside historical evidence; a later interpretation alone cannot establish that premise. [1]

A different reading connects 5:116 with the adjacent Quranic criticism of those who say three, arguing that the text misunderstands the people it is addressing. The juxtaposition is relevant, but the passages do not explicitly equate Mary's deification with the Christian three persons. That stronger identification requires evidence from interpretation and historical context, not just repetition of 5:116.

The resulting question is evidentiary: if the criticism aims at Christianity's central creed, it misses that creed. If it targets a particular Marian devotion thought to cross into worship, the historical target must be identified and demonstrated.

There is a further distinction within the Bible itself. Matthew 28:19 gives the baptismal formula invoking Father, Son and Holy Spirit, while Quran 112 emphasises God's uniqueness and rejection of divine offspring. Those passages represent substantively different theological formulations; this conflict should be assessed directly rather than implying the Nicene Creed names Mary. [7] [8]

The precise textual failure occurs when someone presents Quran 5:116 as an accurate formal definition of the orthodox Trinity. That claim does not meet the Christian primary texts. A different charge, that particular people gave Mary excessive reverence, requires actual historical evidence for their practices and cannot be decided simply by restating a formal creed.

05 / THE ARABIC DOES NOT ACTUALLY DEFINE THE TRINITY

The Arabic does not actually define the Trinity

Quran 5:116 uses the dual expression ilahayn, speaking of Jesus and his mother as two deities besides God. Quran 5:73 and 4:171 use expressions concerning three, but these are different passages without an explicit statement that Mary is one of the three persons. This distinction is decisive for evaluating what the Quran does and does not claim. [1] [2] [3]

It is legitimate to criticise a modern assertion that 5:116 accurately summarises Nicene Christianity, because Nicene Christianity does not deify Mary. It is not legitimate to assert that 5:116 literally reads "the Christian Trinity consists of God, Mary and Jesus." The wording does not say that. The stronger inquiry asks what practice the verse targets and whether identifiable contemporaneous Christians actually practised it.

This prevents a weak criticism from obscuring a stronger one: why does an alleged correction of Christian theology concentrate on a form of deification that the ordinary Christian creed rejects, while challenging the actual Father-Son-Spirit formulation elsewhere without naming its three persons?

06 / THE MOST SERIOUS ISLAMIC COUNTER-READING IS ABOUT PRACTICE

The most serious Islamic counter-reading is about practice

Quran 9:31 accuses some People of the Book of treating their religious leaders as lords apart from God. That language demonstrates that Quranic polemic can describe what it considers excessive deference as a form of taking someone as a lord, without alleging that adherents formally wrote that person into a creedal definition of God. The same possibility must be considered for Quran 5:116. [9]

Epiphanius's pre-Islamic Panarion 79 accuses some women of making offerings in Mary's name. It documents an ancient accusation about a practice, not a confession of faith by those accused, and it does not establish that they considered Mary a divine person in the Trinity or that they were the people addressed by Quran 5:116. [10]

The criticism then becomes historically testable: which sect, region, form of devotion, language and date are being described? How is worship as a deity distinguished from petitioning a saint for intercession? A polemical allegation cannot be validated merely by defining every practice the critic dislikes as literal deity-worship.

07 / AN ANCIENT SECT REPORT IS NOT PROOF OF A WIDESPREAD CHRISTIAN DOCTRINE

An ancient sect report is not proof of a widespread Christian doctrine

The historical allegation should be assessed from the earlier source rather than through a later defence of the Quran. Epiphanius condemns offerings to Mary and explicitly distinguishes honouring her from worshipping God. A contested rite does not by itself show that Mary was included in mainstream Christian doctrine. [10]

Even if the report is accepted, several further questions remain: was the group present among the Christians actually addressed by the Quran, did its members treat Mary as ontologically divine, how extensive was it, and was the criticism meant for such a local practice rather than mainstream Christianity? The evidence presently cited does not settle all of these questions.

This is the proper burden of proof for a narrower historical defence. It is more careful than declaring that no pre-Islamic Christian ever excessively venerated Mary, but it also prevents a disputed and geographically limited report from being used as proof that Christianity's creed named Mary as God.

08 / THE DOCTRINAL COMPARISON MUST REMAIN ANCHORED IN EARLIER EVIDENCE

The doctrinal comparison must remain anchored in earlier evidence

The Nicene Creed explicitly identifies Father, Son and Holy Spirit and speaks of Mary's role in the incarnation without describing her as a divine person. Matthew 28:19 likewise presents a baptismal invocation of Father, Son and Holy Spirit. These are identifiable Christian witnesses to the principal doctrine being challenged. [5] [7]

Quran 5:75 reasons from the fact that Jesus and Mary ate food. This may serve as a criticism of attributing divine status to human beings, but it does not specifically establish that Christian theology believed Mary was divine. Nor does an argument from Jesus' human characteristics, by itself, refute the Christian doctrine that the incarnation includes a real human nature. [4]

Evidence assessment: there is a verified mismatch between the Quran's Mary-and-Jesus deity accusation and the Nicene creed; it is undemonstrated that Quran 5:116 is intended as a literal listing of the Trinity. The historically meaningful challenge is whether the allegedly condemned form of belief can be independently identified and shown to be relevant to the Quran's audience.

09 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

What the Arabic of 5:116 actually asserts

The original Arabic says إِلَٰهَيْنِ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ, ilahayni min duni Allah. The dual noun means two deities, and the context identifies Jesus and his mother Mary. This is a specific accusation of deity-language, not a neutral description of admiration. Replacing the dual with a phrase such as 'respected figures' would weaken the source before testing it. [1] [24]

Yet the Arabic does not contain the Greek term Trinity, nor list Father, Son and Spirit. 'Jesus and Mary as deities besides God' is not identical to 'Mary is the third person of the Christian Trinity'. The latter would require a separate inference connecting verses and an actual doctrinal target. [1] [2] [24]

This difference matters to fair criticism: the valid objection is that the named Marian-deity accusation does not reflect Nicene Christianity; the invalid shortcut is claiming the Quran has literally provided a formal three-person definition including Mary. A strong critical site should state both conclusions plainly. [11] [13]

10 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

The Arabic phrase 'third of three' does not name a creedal triad

Quran 5:73 uses ثَالِثُ ثَلَاثَةٍ, thalithu thalathatin, meaning third of three. The grammatical wording is a count and an ordinal; the three figures are not explicitly listed. The verse then states that God is one. Thus the Quran unmistakably disputes a claim involving three, but the text alone does not tell the reader which doctrinal statement is being paraphrased. [2] [25]

The previous verse condemns people who say God is Christ, while verse 75 points to the human behaviour of Jesus and Mary. The placement makes a related Christological polemic clear. Nevertheless, placing verses side by side does not authorise putting an unspoken sentence into them. [2] [4]

The burden of a claimed contradiction is therefore exact: show that the Quran misstates a specific Christian creed, not merely that it uses categories that are objectionable to the critic. In the case of Mary's deity, the contrast is direct; in the case of the unnamed three, a complete identification requires more work. [13]

11 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

Quran 4:171 rejects three without enumerating Mary

Quran 4:171 calls Jesus the Messiah, the messenger and a word from God, and refers to a spirit from him. It then directs its audience not to say three. This is a clear rejection of Trinitarian language while retaining certain elevated Quranic descriptions of Jesus. [3] [26]

It is not evidence that the Quran has enumerated three persons or explicitly equated its own 'spirit' terminology with the fourth-century Christian Holy Spirit. The two traditions' words may sound similar without sharing identical metaphysical definitions. [3] [13]

Earlier Christian disputes about the Holy Spirit were already documented before Islam. Athanasius's letters to Serapion, associated with the late 350s, defended the Spirit's divine status against Christians who treated the Spirit as created. The 381 creed then openly identifies the Spirit as Lord and jointly worshipped with Father and Son. A theory that Christian theology first inserted the Spirit into a three-person creed after Islam is therefore chronologically untenable. [13] [28]

12 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

Nicaea 325 and Constantinople 381 are different documents

The Creed of Nicaea issued in 325 professes one God, describes the Son as begotten rather than made and of one substance with the Father, and adds a brief confession of the Holy Spirit. This wording is not identical to the much longer creed later associated with Constantinople in 381. [12] [14]

The 381 Greek creed expands the pneumatological formulation and describes the Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life, worshipped and glorified alongside the Father and Son. It calls Mary the Virgin through whom the Son became incarnate, not an object of divine worship. Both creeds are pre-Islamic, leaving no plausible dispute over the general chronology of their doctrine. [13] [14]

Credal history does not prove the Trinity metaphysically true. It establishes a direct documentary comparison: before the Quran, normative Christianity explicitly placed Mary outside the triune identity. A later statement depicting her as an object of deity-worship cannot be defended as an accurate quotation of those creeds. [12] [13]

13 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

Earlier Christian books name Father, Son and Spirit without Mary

Matthew 28:19 contains a baptismal formula naming Father, Son and Holy Spirit. In the earlier Pauline material, 2 Corinthians 13:14 brings together Jesus Christ, God and Holy Spirit in a benediction. Neither lists Mary. The exact later language of one divine essence and three persons is not already formulated fully in these biblical lines. [7] [15]

Nevertheless the identity of the three named figures is not an invention of the medieval or Islamic periods. The Christian written tradition had a recognisable triadic pattern long before Quran 5:116 and before the fourth-century creeds formalised it. [7] [13] [15]

This does not require treating every scriptural detail as historically infallible. We are testing what identifiable earlier documents actually claim, not asking whether quoting a verse magically proves its doctrine. Where the accusation is that Christians considered Mary divine, the earlier triadic texts and creeds supply contrary positive textual evidence. [12] [13]

14 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

The Council of Ephesus did not deify Mary

The early fifth-century dispute about the Greek title Theotokos, meaning God-bearer, centred on whether the person born of Mary was truly the incarnate divine Word. It did not propose that Mary became an additional divine person. The written acts and associated letters of Ephesus (431) are sources for the Christological controversy, not a confession of Mary's independent deity. [17]

Richard Price's specialist discussion cautions that the council did not issue the simple standalone decree defining Theotokos sometimes attributed to it. The exact conciliar procedure matters, but the central historical point survives: earlier Christians disputed how to speak of Christ and his mother while retaining a distinction between the human mother and divine Son. [17] [18]

Calling Mary God-bearer does not logically entail that Mary is God any more than describing a human birth automatically makes the mother share every attribute of her child. A critic who argues otherwise must establish that philosophical inference. An actual Christian creed listing Mary as divine would be different evidence; the council material does not provide one. [13] [17]

15 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

Epiphanius supplies an earlier accusation, not a confession

In Panarion 79, Epiphanius reports women making ritual bread offerings in Mary's honour, associated by him with Arabia and places further north. This fourth-century testimony is an important counterweight to an assertion that no Marian practice was ever controversial before Islam. The report is earlier than the Quran by centuries. [10] [19]

However Epiphanius was the critic of these women, not an independently verified spokesman for their doctrines. His account does not supply a creed declaring Mary God, nor is it a surviving membership record of a seventh-century sect that can be shown to have existed in Muhammad's immediate milieu. Repeating a sect's later nickname cannot fill that evidence gap. [10] [19]

Epiphanius also distinguished honouring Mary from worshipping God. Therefore the older Christian record includes both excessive-devotion accusations and an explicit objection to treating Mary as divine. The existence of controversy is established; the precise beliefs and transmission routes require further evidence. [10] [19]

16 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

The strongest practice-focused explanation deserves an actual test

Mohsen Goudarzi's 2025 peer-reviewed article, The Qur'an's Cultic Trinity, argues that the fifth surah's language is better explained by devotional acts than by a formal alternative trinitarian creed. Its published abstract draws on Marian feasts, intercessory practices and offerings. That provides a substantive historical hypothesis rather than the claim that an otherwise unknown sect must simply have existed. [20]

The same surah includes a table-from-heaven narrative before the interrogation in 5:116. This sequence can motivate a cultic reading, but it does not explicitly name a Christian community or state that the reported Eucharistic-like actions were addressed specifically by the later accusation about Mary. The connection remains an argued inference, not an observed link. [22] [20]

The principal unresolved step is from a documented act of honour or religious intercession to a historically demonstrated belief that the recipient is a goddess. A polemic may classify worship differently from the worshipper's own creed. The article should assess those rival classifications, rather than silently convert one into a claimed historical confession. [6] [20]

17 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

Modern scholarly disagreement does not settle the ancient target

Goudarzi's analysis supports reading the Quran as an internal critique of lived Christian Marian devotion; Zahra Mohagheghian's 2024 review challenges a different historical attempt to identify the precise group accused, drawing attention to problems created by a narrowly chosen political target. Both contributions demonstrate that the historical referent remains debated. [20] [21]

These are modern interpretations, not new contemporaneous witnesses to the actions of a named seventh-century group. Their value is in specifying rival explanations and highlighting what those explanations must establish. It would be unsound to cite either as if it directly reported Muhammad's conversation with an identifiable worshipping community. [20] [21]

On a balance-of-probabilities standard, an argument anchored in existing Marian practice deserves greater consideration than one relying on an entirely unattested Trinity with Mary. But better contextual plausibility does not validate the exact accusation of deification; that remains a separate proposition requiring independent support. [13] [20]

18 / SOURCE AND EVIDENCE TEST

Eating food is compatible with the Nicene claim of human nature

Quran 5:75 points out that Jesus and Mary ate food, demonstrating ordinary human dependency. The point is clear against a claim that Mary is a nonhuman divine being; the Christian creeds, however, already treat Mary as human. The polemic does not expose an internal Nicene contradiction concerning her. [4] [27]

For Jesus, the objection likewise does not directly refute the actual doctrine of incarnation. John 1:14 explicitly says the Word became flesh, and the 381 creed describes the Son as becoming human. In that theological system eating is not an unexpected admission but part of the very human nature the creed professes. [13] [16]

This does not establish that incarnation is true. It establishes a requirement for a coherent criticism: address the two-nature claim as actually stated, rather than assuming the Christian position denies that Jesus had a physical human body. Similarly, Quran 6:101's argument from God's lack of a consort cannot simply be equated to a refutation of the non-sexual generation described by the early creed. [23] [12]

To change the result, a defender of the exact historical deification accusation should produce an independently dated Christian witness to Mary being understood as a deity by the identifiable Quran-relevant group. To prove the Quran formally defines the Trinity with Mary, a critic must supply a passage explicitly connecting those names to the three rather than inferring an unquoted creed. [1] [20]

These burdens are symmetrical: an unsupported reconciliation does not defeat a demonstrated doctrinal mismatch, and an unquoted assertion should not be placed in the Quran merely because it makes a critique easier. [13] [24]

20 / CONCLUSION

Mary is not part of the Nicene Trinity; identify the real historical target

The Christian Trinity does not consist of God, Jesus and Mary. The Nicene Creed clearly identifies Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Quran 5:116 instead condemns the taking of Jesus and Mary as deities, an accusation that does not describe the orthodox creed. [1] [5]

It is therefore incorrect to claim that the deification of Mary is a feature of standard Trinitarian teaching. It would also be incorrect to assert, solely from 5:116, that the Quran explicitly defines the Trinity as a three-person combination including Mary. The argument's strength lies in identifying exactly what the Quran criticises and testing whether the claimed Christian target fits.

Verdict: The claimed portrayal fails as a description of Nicene Christianity. If Quran 5:116 is offered as an account of orthodox Trinitarian doctrine, it does not match the Creed, which identifies Father, Son and Holy Spirit, not Mary. Because 5:116 does not explicitly define the Trinity, the broader claim that the Quran states Mary belongs to it remains unproven.

EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATIONVerified doctrinal mismatch; intended target disputedThe written Christian creed does not identify Mary as divine

Historical verdict, on the balance of probabilities: The earlier creeds decisively exclude Mary from the orthodox Trinity. A practice-based polemic is a serious explanation of the Quranic accusation given documented late antique Marian offerings and veneration, but neither a named continuing sect nor a seventh-century confession of Mary's actual deity has been independently demonstrated here. The stronger historical outcome is a mismatch with orthodox doctrine and an unresolved specific target, not a proven quotation of a three-person Mary-Trinity. [13] [19] [20] [21]

Direct textual finding: Quran 5:116 accuses people of taking Jesus and Mary as deities, while the pre-Islamic Christian creed names Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The verse does not explicitly say Mary is part of a Trinity. The proposed identification of a particular Marian sect is not made by the Quran and is not independently verified merely by citing a later explanation. [1] [11]
