When inheritance shares exceed the estate.

A worked case shows why the raw fractions in Quran 4:11-12 cannot always be distributed without an additional rule.

01 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Can every stated fraction be paid in full?

VERIFIED ARITHMETICAL RESULT

In a recognised inheritance case, the shares named in Quran 4:11-12 total 27/24, or 112.5% of the estate. Those original fractions cannot all be paid in full from one estate. Islamic jurists use a proportional reduction called awl to resolve such cases. The Quranic verses themselves do not explicitly set out that reduction.

Consider a man who dies leaving a wife, his father, his mother and three daughters, with no sons. Assume that debts and any permissible bequests have already been dealt with and that these are the relevant heirs. The rule for three daughters avoids the separate translation dispute over whether the phrase in 4:11 includes exactly two daughters. [1] [2]

02 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

What fractions do the verses assign?

Quran 4:11 assigns two-thirds of the estate collectively to three daughters. It separately assigns one-sixth to each parent when the deceased has children. Quran 4:12 assigns one-eighth to a widow when her deceased husband has children. [1] [2]

The total is not an allegation about a mistranslated word. It is the direct sum of the displayed fractions: 16 + 4 + 4 + 3 = 27 parts when the estate contains 24 parts. In a hypothetical estate worth $24,000, the nominal entitlements sum to $27,000.

03 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

How does Islamic law make the shares fit?

Sunni jurists have long applied awl, which proportionately reduces the prescribed shares if their total exceeds the distributable estate. A written Islamic legal explanation applies the method to precisely this family configuration. [3] The general rule is also described in legal responses to the apparent excess. [4]

Rather than award 16/24, 4/24, 4/24 and 3/24 of the estate, the method distributes 16/27, 4/27, 4/27 and 3/27. The new fractions sum to one and can all be paid.

The awl adjustment makes the estate distributable, but it is not a reduction formula written in Quran 4:11-12. Changing the denominator from 24 to 27 is an additional rule applied to the listed fractions, not proof that the Quran supplies that rule. This distinction holds regardless of whether the resulting legal calculation is workable.

04 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Does the Quran explicitly provide the reduction formula?

The cited verses specify shares and make distribution subject to debts and bequests. They do not state an explicit instruction such as: when the listed shares exceed the whole estate, reduce every listed entitlement in proportion to the excess. [1] [2]

That omission does not mean Muslims lack a working inheritance system. It means the practical system relies on juristic interpretation beyond the fractions stated in these two verses. Describing that distinction as a mathematical failure in the operational law would be inaccurate; describing the textual fractions as universally distributable without adjustment would also be inaccurate.

What is actually established

A later interpretive rule resolves the over-allocation. Whether law is expected to require post-scriptural elaboration is a theological and jurisprudential question, not a calculation that can settle itself.

In the worked example, the nominal shares are 16/24 for the three daughters, 4/24 for each parent, and 3/24 for the widow. They total 27/24, so even if the estate were free from debts and other complications, not all entitlements can be paid in the stated proportions. The awl remedy makes the denominator 27, reducing every claimant's effective fraction. [1] [2] [3]

This matters for evaluating the claim of an exhaustive, directly usable divine allocation formula: the original fractions and the enforceable fractions are not identical in this situation. An Islamic jurisprudential response that law may legitimately be completed through interpretation is coherent, but it must acknowledge that additional interpretive work is necessary. The criticism is not that jurists cannot distribute an estate; it is that these verses alone do not provide a self-contained solution to every permitted set of heirs.

05 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

A second inheritance configuration confirms the over-allocation

The problem is not confined to one artificially arranged family. Consider a deceased woman who leaves a husband, mother and two full sisters, with no children or father and with the applicable inheritance conditions satisfied. Quran 4:12 gives a childless widower one-half; Quran 4:11 gives the mother one-sixth where siblings are present; Quran 4:176 assigns two sisters two-thirds in a kalalah case. The face-value shares total 1/2 + 1/6 + 2/3 = 4/3 of the estate. [5] [1] [2]

The legal definitions of kalalah, sibling classes and exclusion rules must be established before applying the figures; a simple list of relatives is not always sufficient. Under the stated assumptions, however, this is another instance in which paying the unadjusted fractions would require more money than exists. The fact that a juristic procedure can resolve the case is relevant, but it does not erase the excess in the initial face-value allocation.

06 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

The historical disagreement about awl matters

An Islamic legal response preserves a significant early disagreement: an account attributes proportional awl to Umar, whereas Ibn Abbas is reported to have favoured an ordering in which some specified shares take priority over others rather than all being proportionately reduced. The legal explanation at Islam Question and Answer sets out this disagreement while defending the predominant Sunni method. This is evidence that interpreters recognised a real allocation problem and debated its legal remedy. [4]

The difference is substantive. With a proportional remedy, all affected face-value fractions decline. With a priority remedy, some are protected and the remainder is reduced more heavily or excluded. Neither mechanism is the literal instruction "pay each listed percentage in full" when the percentages exceed 100%. Historical disagreement over the remedy therefore strengthens the narrow criticism that these verses alone do not give a unique complete executable algorithm.

It would be inaccurate to infer from disagreement that all Islamic inheritance practice is arbitrary. Legal systems routinely use interpretive principles. The claim under scrutiny is the stronger assertion that the relevant Quranic sentences already specify every required allocation step without recourse to interpretation.

07 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Unallocated residue is a separate problem from over-allocation

The inverse situation also matters. A deceased person may leave fixed-share heirs whose stated fractions sum to less than the estate. Islamic jurisprudence has rules for residual heirs and for return of a remainder, known as radd, when relevant; these are different problems from awl. The existence of a remainder does not produce the same mathematical impossibility as an excess, and no general critique should confuse them. Quran 4:11-12 and 4:176 specify a range of fixed shares and classes, not a self-contained algorithm for all possible family structures. [5]

The hidden dependency is legal: before anyone can run the fractions, they must identify qualified heirs, exclusions, debts, applicable bequests, residual priority and which jurisprudential school governs the distribution. A statement that a fraction is divinely assigned is not sufficient to calculate the full estate unless those surrounding conditions have already been settled.

The test should therefore be framed as a documentation issue, not the claim that Muslim jurists cannot perform elementary arithmetic. The mathematics of the demonstrated shortfall is indisputable; its implications for the completeness of revelation depend on what the Quran promises to give its readers.

08 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

What would count as a genuine defence of completeness?

A successful argument that the Quran is an exhaustive inheritance allocation code would have to identify a Quranic instruction that selects the priority or proportionality rule for every permitted excess, prove that this principle follows from the wording rather than merely from later practice, and account for historical disagreements over the remedy. An argument that the Quran intentionally supplies general duties while legal experts legitimately develop implementing rules is different and more plausible; it concedes that the verses alone are incomplete as a numerical programme.

This distinction matters to Quranic claims that guidance is explained in detail. Quran 16:89 describes the Book as an explanation of all things in a broader religious context. It is not sound to pretend this is an explicit promise to include every legal formula. Nevertheless, a defender asserting that no extra interpretive machinery is necessary for these specific shares faces an unambiguous practical counterexample. [6]

Evidence assessment: the numerical excess is verified from the primary text; awl is a real juristic correction; the question of the Quran's claimed comprehensiveness is a theological and interpretive dispute, not a reason to deny the fractions.

09 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

The Arabic states exact fractions, not the awl formula

Quran 4:11 assigns ثُلُثَا, two-thirds, to the specified daughters and السُّدُسُ, one-sixth, to each parent if the deceased has children. Quran 4:12 assigns a surviving wife الثُّمُنُ, one-eighth, when the deceased leaves a child. The Quranic Arabic Corpus identifies each as a fractional allocation: al-thumun names the eighth, while fawqa ithnatayn in 4:11 literally refers to more than two daughters. [1] [2] [7] [8]

These are parts of what the deceased leaves. The verses describe debts and bequests but do not specify proportional reduction, priority protection for a particular heir, or any common-denominator correction if their stated fractions exceed one. The operational rule must be supplied separately. [1] [2]

Calling the fractions prescribed by God establishes their claimed status inside the text; it does not provide an unstated instruction for determining which heir must receive less when their simultaneous nominal entitlements exceed the estate.

10 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Three daughters avoid the two-daughter interpretive controversy

The Arabic of 4:11 includes فَوْقَ اثْنَتَيْنِ, literally 'above two', although many Islamic translations and jurists read the two-thirds provision as including exactly two daughters. The main worked cases use three daughters, which satisfy either reading. [7] [16]

Quran 4:176 separately assigns two-thirds to two sisters in a specified sibling setting. Jami at-Tirmidhi 2092 transmits a ruling giving two daughters two-thirds, but its chain and differing displayed grades need independent historical scrutiny. It illustrates a traditional legal interpretation rather than providing a missing Quranic sentence. [3] [15]

The 112.5 percent deficit does not depend on accepting a disputed reading for two daughters, making the numerical objection stronger than a criticism relying on later transmission alone.

11 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Case A requires 27 twenty-fourths from an estate of 24

A deceased man leaves his wife, father, mother and three daughters but no son. The daughters collectively receive 16/24; father 4/24; mother 4/24; wife 3/24. The total is 27/24, or 112.5 percent. IslamWeb independently documents precisely this combination as an awl case. [1] [2] [9]

For a net estate worth AUD 24,000, paying the face-value fractions would require AUD 16,000 to the daughters, AUD 4,000 to each parent and AUD 3,000 to the wife, a total of AUD 27,000. The deficit is AUD 3,000. This is exact arithmetic, not a disagreement over the monetary unit. [9]

The correct Sunni repair makes the daughter's claim 16/27, each parent 4/27 and the wife 3/27, totaling one. The repair succeeds because it reduces the effective fractional awards, not because the original fractions themselves summed to one. [11] [12]

12 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Imami priority reduction is not proportional Sunni awl

Agostino Cilardo's comparative survey and Muhammad Jawad Mughniyya's legal treatise record different approaches. Sunni schools generally proportionately reduce the eligible fixed shares when the total exceeds the estate. Imami jurisprudence protects the minimum shares of parents and spouse and takes the shortfall from daughters or sisters. [11] [12]

For case A, Imami priority preserves wife 3/24, father 4/24 and mother 4/24, leaving daughters 13/24 instead of the nominal 16/24. The Sunni result is wife 3/27, father and mother 4/27 each and daughters 16/27. [11] [12]

Both results are arithmetically payable, but are not identical outcomes. Therefore the text alone does not identify a uniquely applied adjustment across major legal schools, and the assertion that all jurists use the same Quranic rule would be factually misleading.

Both schools preserve the estate by changing an entitlement, but they change different entitlements. This difference is legally significant for the family receiving the property; it is not a mere choice of denominator notation. [11] [12]

A completed inheritance law can adopt either distribution in its own jurisprudential framework. That does not prove either method is expressly written into Quran 4:11-12, or that the original two-thirds, sixths and eighth can all be paid simultaneously in case A.

14 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Debts and bequests cannot cure this ratio

Quran 4:11-12 directs that recognised debts and bequests are dealt with before distribution. The worked example concerns only the net estate after those matters are resolved. If E represents that estate, the four nominal fractions claim 9E/8. Reducing E to pay a creditor changes the fund but cannot change 9/8 into 1. [1] [2]

It would be a defective criticism to add shares against the wrong gross estate or ignore creditor rights. Here the same remaining fund is the base for every fractional entitlement. A reduction of the fund leaves each claimant's fractional percentage unchanged and hence preserves the over-allocation. [8]

The relevant question is which share must change when the fund is inadequate, not whether the deceased could have owed money or made a will.

15 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Case B requires 125 percent when the surviving spouse is male

For a deceased woman leaving a husband, both parents and three daughters with no son, 4:12 specifies husband 1/4; 4:11 specifies the parents 1/6 each and daughters collectively 2/3. The total is 1/4 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 2/3 = 15/12, or 125 percent. [1] [2]

Sunni awl makes the husband 3/15, each parent 2/15 and daughters 8/15. Imami priority preserves the husband at 3/12 and each parent at 2/12, leaving daughters 5/12. Either method produces a payable total by lowering at least one specified claim. [11] [12]

IslamWeb gives a separate recognised husband-parent-daughter case using the fifteen-part Sunni calculation. The presence of a surviving husband does not rescue the unadjusted face-value fractions. [10]

16 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

What reported early disagreement actually establishes

Later transmitted legal accounts attribute a proportional awl judgement to Umar ibn al-Khattab and a priority approach to Ibn Abbas. A modern Sunni legal defence documents the traditional attribution, and Mughniyya explains its relevance to Imami jurisprudence. These are transmitted legal positions, not verified contemporary minutes of the first historical inheritance dispute. [12] [13]

It is improper to assert as a proven historical fact that no over-allocation ever occurred during Muhammad's lifetime merely because later sources reported the first notable dispute under Umar. That would give a partisan transmission more authority than its actual evidentiary role warrants. [11]

What is established for the textual issue is that competing legal methods were developed or transmitted to resolve a mathematically real shortfall. Neither historical lineage of a method inserts an explicit correction formula into the Quranic fractions themselves.

17 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

Under-allocation and radd must not be conflated with awl

Other estates have fixed shares totaling less than the available property. Residuary inheritance and return of a remainder, often called radd, concern those different cases. The deficit called awl arises only when the eligible specified claims collectively exceed the fund. Cilardo describes the differing school structures and residue treatment. [11]

Quran 4:7 gives both sexes rights to inheritance, while 8:75, 33:6, 4:33 and 4:8 address kinship or division in related settings. These passages may inform broader law, but none explicitly gives the over-allocation denominator correction in the four-heir example. [14] [17] [18] [19] [20]

A legal case with both sons and daughters distributes the remaining estate differently after spouse and parent shares. That is not a counterexample to a case with three daughters and no sons; adding sons changes the operative class of children's entitlement. [21]

18 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

The self-contained allocation claim is the one that fails

Quran 4:13-14 describes nearby inheritance directions as divine limits, but does not specify how to resolve an excess. Quran 16:89 discusses the scripture's explanatory role, and it would be a mistaken shortcut to turn that broad language into an unqualified promise of a detailed computer-like inheritance algorithm. [4] [6]

A serious defence may argue that revelation intentionally sets entitlements and authorises jurists to develop fair implementation rules. That can provide a coherent theory of legal development, but concedes the point that the original face-value shares need additional rules to operate in every valid estate. [11] [12]

The test should therefore reject universal unadjusted distributability without pretending that the numerical problem alone disproves all religious authority. The observed sum of 112.5 percent is beyond dispute; broader theological implications depend on other premises.

19 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

The evidence that would actually overturn the conclusion

To rebut the numerical example, one must show that the quoted fractions do not apply simultaneously to the named eligible relatives, that they concern different net estates, or that a Quranic text expressly supplies the adjustment that makes the claims compatible. The actual Sunni worked-case sources accept the family structure and the need for awl. [9] [10]

A valid Quranic instruction establishing proportional reduction or protecting certain heirs would weaken the textual-completeness criticism. The fact that later jurists devised two functioning but different rules cannot demonstrate that such an instruction already appears in 4:11-12. [11] [12]

One verified case totaling more than 100 percent is sufficient to reject the assertion that all nominal fractions are always payable in full. The second example and legal-school comparison explain the scale and significance of the required correction.

20 / ORIGINAL TEXT AND LEGAL TEST

A real over-allocation, and a real legal remedy

The stated fractions in Quran 4:11-12 exceed 100% for this family configuration. The figures cannot simultaneously operate as unreduced percentages of the same estate. The traditional awl procedure corrects the allocation by proportionally reducing the shares.

The defensible criticism is therefore precise: the Quranic fractions are not a self-sufficient executable allocation rule for every case. A claim that the verses alone always prescribe distributable, unadjusted percentages is false. The stronger claim that Islamic law cannot calculate or distribute such an estate is also false.

Verdict: The Quranic fractions fail as a complete unadjusted allocation rule. In the demonstrated valid family configuration, the prescribed shares total 112.5 percent. Sunni jurisprudence resolves the problem through awl, but that is an additional allocation rule rather than a direct instruction in 4:11-12.

Comparative legal verdict: Sunni proportional awl and Imami priority reduction both resolve the deficit but alter different nominal shares. Quran 4:11-12 does not expressly provide either adjustment as a complete algorithm. [11] [12]

Direct textual finding: the Quranic shares in the two worked examples exceed the distributable estate; Quran 4:11-12 and 4:176 do not prescribe the proportional awl correction. Later legal explanations show how practitioners address the shortfall, but cannot establish that the missing instruction was already present in the verses. [1] [2] [5]
