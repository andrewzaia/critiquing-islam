Abrogated recitation and the Quran's preservation claim.

Aisha, Umar, Ubayy, omitted recited passages and the burden of establishing intentional abrogation.

Distinguishing textual preservation, recitation withdrawal and unverified revelation claims.

01 / THE PRESERVATION CLAIM NEEDS A MEASURABLE OBJECT

The preservation claim needs a measurable object

The Quran promises that God will guard the Reminder (15:9) and portrays the revelation as resistant to falsehood (41:41-42). These are clear claims of divine protection. To test their historical fulfilment, however, we must identify what is protected: every utterance originally given to Muhammad, only verses intended to remain liturgically recited, a stable consonantal skeleton, an approved family of readings, or a particular printed edition. Those are not interchangeable propositions. [1] [2]

An early manuscript closely resembling today's standard Quran strongly supports the existence of an early textual tradition. It does not by itself recover every recitation attributed to Muhammad in later reports or prove that all forms described as revealed were included in the official collection. A defence that moves between broad and narrow meanings of preservation without declaring the shift is not a successful historical explanation.

This investigation asks whether reports of recited material absent from the standard codex are best explained as intentional abrogation, lost transmission, non-standard recitation or a problem with the reports themselves. It does not begin by assuming that absence automatically establishes corruption.

02 / AISHA'S REPORT NAMES TWO SUCCESSIVE RECITATION RULES

Aisha's report names two successive recitation rules

Sahih Muslim 1452a attributes to Aisha a report that a rule involving ten clear breastfeedings was revealed, later replaced by five, and that the five-breastfeeding wording was still among Quranic material recited when Muhammad died. The Arabic report includes the claim that the Prophet died while the relevant material was still being recited. Neither version is present as an independent verse in the standard Quranic text. [3]

Muslim 1452b contains another wording through Aisha describing the transition from ten to five. This is corroboration that the same companion report circulated in related forms, not an independent eyewitness from another community. Counting the two hadith entries as two independent memories of the original revelation would overstate the evidence. [4]

The question is not whether the reported nursing rule is morally acceptable; it is a narrower text-history question. If the tradition is accurate, what accounts for an allegedly revealed, still-recited expression not being included in the stable written standard? If the report is inaccurate, what grounds justify accepting the hadith corpus as authentic on comparable questions while rejecting this particular inconvenient narration?

Chain-level qualification: Muslim 1452a passes from Aisha through Amra bint Abd al-Rahman and Abdullah ibn Abi Bakr before Malik. Muslim 1452b also passes from Aisha through Amra, then follows Yahya ibn Said and later transmitters. The two reports therefore share the named early transmitter Amra and the ultimate companion Aisha; they are not two independent companion attestations. Crucially, 1452b does not repeat the statement that the five-breastfeeding wording was still being recited when Muhammad died. That timing claim is specifically present in 1452a and must not be inflated by counting 1452b as a second explicit witness to it. [3] [4]

03 / THE PHRASE RECITED AT MUHAMMAD'S DEATH MATTERS

The phrase recited at Muhammad's death matters

A common Sunni explanation distinguishes a text whose legal ruling is abrogated from a text whose recitation is no longer part of the final Quran. That is a coherent possible category. The most demanding feature of Muslim 1452a is its reported timing: recitation at the death of Muhammad. It requires explanation of how and when the community learned that the words were no longer to be recited, especially if the withdrawal is said to have occurred before his death. [3]

One response is that some companions continued to recite an abrogated form temporarily because the abrogation had not reached them. Another is that the reported phrase should be interpreted as continuing until close to his death rather than after the text's final settlement. These answers may reconcile the report with a final authorised canon, but they need identifiable evidence for their chronology, not merely an assertion that whatever the standard codex lacks must have been abrogated.

Nor can the modern reader simply prove the absent wording was once divine revelation from the report itself. The report is a later transmission of a remembered claim. Its historical credibility and its theological classification are distinct stages of the inquiry.

04 / UMAR DESCRIBES A RECITED STONING VERSE

Umar describes a recited stoning verse

In Sahih Muslim 1691a, Umar, as reported through Ibn Abbas, declares that a verse about stoning had been among the revelation, that the community recited and understood it, and that he fears later generations will reject the rule because it is not found in the Book. Sahih al-Bukhari 6829 preserves a related account. These are significant primary Islamic textual reports of an absent recited passage, but the related accounts trace back to Umar rather than representing independent direct witnesses to the revelation itself. [5] [6]

The standard Quran contains a corporal punishment for unlawful sexual intercourse in 24:2 but not the specific recited verse that Umar describes. Classical jurists may distinguish the circumstances covered by the Quranic rule from the reported rule concerning married offenders. Thus absence from the codex is a real textual question, not a complete reconstruction of Islamic criminal law from one verse. [7]

A Muslim response commonly describes this as abrogation of recitation with a continuing legal ruling. That response removes a superficial contradiction only if the distinction is granted and substantiated. It cannot serve as independent evidence of divine authorisation unless there is a non-circular reason to know the wording was intentionally withdrawn while its legal force remained.

The Umar accounts are not independent companion corroboration: Muslim 1691a includes the positive wording that the alleged verse was revealed, recited, memorised and understood. Bukhari 6829 reports Umar's warning that the stoning ruling would be rejected because people could not find its verse in the Book, but does not reproduce the full recitation statement found in Muslim's version. The reported routes both run through Ibn Abbas, Ubayd Allah ibn Abd Allah and Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri before diverging. Their agreement supports the circulation of an attributed Umar tradition; the longer Muslim wording should not be silently assigned to the shorter Bukhari version. [5] [6]

05 / UBAYY'S RECITATION AND THE PROBLEM OF SELECTIVE RETENTION

Ubayy's recitation and the problem of selective retention

Sahih al-Bukhari 5005 reports Umar regarding Ubayy as a leading Quran reciter while stating that some of his recitation was left aside. Ubayy is portrayed as insisting that he learned it directly from Muhammad. Umar's reply invokes Quran 2:106, concerning abrogation or causing a verse to be forgotten. This is evidence that the transmission tradition itself remembered disputes about what should continue to be recited. [8] [9]

The report does not present a full transcript of the disputed wording, or an independently surviving document identifying all such readings. It therefore cannot by itself quantify a number of missing verses or demonstrate that Ubayy's version was more authentic than the standard text. A claim that it proves an entire second Quran existed would exceed what the report says.

It does impose a proof requirement: when a highly regarded reciter attributes wording to Muhammad but an authority decides not to retain it, what standard distinguishes a correct omission under abrogation from a mistaken exclusion? Simply referring to the final editorial choice does not supply an independent answer.

06 / QURANIC ABROGATION IS AN ESTABLISHED INTERPRETIVE RESPONSE

Quranic abrogation is an established interpretive response

Quran 2:106 speaks of replacing or causing a verse to be forgotten; Quran 16:101 describes substitution of one verse for another. These passages give Islamic interpreters a textual basis for abrogation. Ibn Kathir's commentary on 2:106 discusses both abrogation and differences in how the relevant Arabic expressions are read and explained, and transmits reports about a verse being made to be forgotten. The doctrine is therefore not a modern explanation invented to evade manuscript evidence. [9] [10] [11]

But three separate propositions still require evaluation: that God may abrogate a verse, that God actually abrogated a particular reported recitation, and that the later written collection reliably distinguishes all abrogated material from intended permanent revelation. The first is a textual principle in the Quran; the second and third are particular historical and theological claims.

If the only reason offered for identifying a verse as abrogated is that it does not appear in the current codex, the explanation risks becoming unfalsifiable. Every absence is automatically reclassified as intentional and thus incapable of counting against preservation. A persuasive account should offer positive evidence of withdrawal, dating and transmission, or candidly acknowledge what is not recoverable.

07 / UTHMAN'S COMMITTEE DID NOT BEGIN WITH A BLANK PAGE

Uthman's committee did not begin with a blank page

Sahih al-Bukhari 4986 describes an earlier collection under Abu Bakr, undertaken by Zayd ibn Thabit after concerns that reciters were dying. Bukhari 4987 later describes Uthman obtaining Hafsa's pages, directing a four-person copying committee, specifying use of the Quraysh dialect when disputes arose, distributing authorised codices and ordering other written Quranic material burned. These reports directly contradict the unsupported idea that Uthman invented the Quran without companions or earlier reference material. [12] [13]

At the same time, the tradition describes a standard being chosen through human decisions. Bukhari 4988 adds that Zayd remembered a verse from al-Ahzab which he did not initially find in the copying material, searched for it and incorporated it after locating it with Khuzaima ibn Thabit. This report may support an argument about the committee's diligence; it does not show a process that required no recollection or comparison of variants. [14]

The genuine historical gap is the inability to inspect the full contents of the materials ordered destroyed. It is improper to assert that those materials definitely contained lost divine revelation. It is equally improper to claim that surviving uniformity among official descendants independently proves the content of every eliminated alternative.

08 / AN EARLY WRITTEN ARCHETYPE IS POSITIVE EVIDENCE, NOT A MIRACLE PROOF

An early written archetype is positive evidence, not a miracle proof

Marijn van Putten's 2019 study compared distinctive spelling patterns across fourteen early Quranic manuscripts and argued that their agreement supports a common written Uthmanic archetype. This is substantive manuscript evidence favouring early codification, beyond oral-tradition claims. An argument that all early Quran copies were entirely fluid or that no written model existed ignores this result. [15]

Sadeghi and Goudarzi's analysis of the Sanaa palimpsest identifies its erased lower text as belonging to a different early Quranic textual tradition, while its upper writing conforms to the standard type. This places observable plurality alongside evidence of early standardisation. The palimpsest is not a preserved codex of Aisha's breastfeeding wording or Umar's stoning verse, and it should not be cited as if it independently confirms those reported passages. [16]

Both findings can be true: an official early written tradition existed and at least one significant early non-standard tradition was present. The resulting question is about the scope and selectivity of preservation, not whether an early Quranic text existed at all. Textual survival does not in itself establish divine authorship.

09 / AUTHORISED RECITATION DIFFERS FROM LATER STANDARD READINGS

Authorised recitation differs from later standard readings

Sahih al-Bukhari 4991 describes permission to recite in seven ahruf, while 4992 records Umar objecting to Hisham's different recitation and Muhammad reportedly accepting both. These are reports that differences in recitation were recognised and explained in early Muslim memory. They cannot automatically be identified one-for-one with the later canonical qiraat, and their transmission still requires historical analysis. [17] [18]

An Islamic preservation claim could coherently mean a protected range of authorised spoken readings rather than one identical vocalised sentence everywhere. But that model must explain which variations were authorised, how their boundaries were fixed and whether an omitted report such as Aisha's was intentionally excluded. Mere existence of multiple sanctioned readings does not resolve every disagreement about an absent verse.

A critic should not treat every pronunciation difference as corruption of the consonantal text, and a defender should not claim one unchanged pronunciation if recognised authoritative readings differ. Accurate categories strengthen both the historical description and the central test.

10 / WHAT WOULD INDEPENDENTLY IDENTIFY INTENTIONAL ABROGATION?

What would independently identify intentional abrogation?

A positive case for a particular passage's withdrawal would ideally show an early, dateable attribution identifying the original wording, a separate report that recitation was deliberately ended while Muhammad was alive, and a consistent account of when transmitters were informed. For a rule said to remain legally operative, the evidence should also explain why legal force survived the text's removal. These requirements can be evaluated individually even when no perfect ancient archive survives.

The available hadith may supply some of these elements and not others. A statement such as "the verse was abrogated" is evidence that a transmitter or interpreter understood the situation that way; it does not automatically recover a lost public announcement of the withdrawal. Conversely, the absence of such an announcement in extant sources is not proof it never occurred.

The decisive distinction is between a reading's present absence, the positive historical reasons for that absence and a theological explanation assigning divine intention. Each additional step needs evidence; none is justified by a claim of preservation whose fulfilment is what the inquiry was supposed to test.

A stronger historical test is a source-family comparison, not a count of numbered hadith entries. Record which transmitter reports each clause, where the chains converge, which versions omit the relevant clause, and which interpretation of abrogation is later imposed. For the breastfeeding report, the death-time wording is not repeated by Muslim 1452b. For the stoning account, the full memory-of-recitation wording is preserved in Muslim 1691a, while Bukhari 6829 has a shorter formulation. A reviewer can compare those facts without first accepting or rejecting divine abrogation. [3] [4] [5] [6]

11 / THE SAME RULE MUST GOVERN ALLEGATIONS ABOUT THE BIBLE

The same rule must govern allegations about the Bible

Quran 5:48 calls the Quran a confirmer and an overseer or judge over earlier scripture. This is an intelligible Islamic account of doctrinal authority. But if an earlier biblical text is called corrupt merely because it differs from the Quran, the later scripture is being treated as its own historical authentication. A demonstrable alteration still needs manuscript witnesses, an identifiable earlier reading and a plausible history of change. [19]

The same caution applies to the Quran. That a Muslim narrator refers to revelation not found in the standard text does not automatically prove intentional falsification; a specific omission needs an argument. But accepting an uncorroborated claim of intentional Quranic abrogation while demanding physical manuscript proof of every suggested biblical textual change is an asymmetric standard.

Both Jewish and Christian communities used writing and oral teaching; neither method is exclusive to Islam. A historical test should distinguish what surviving documents show, what later communities remember, and what additional theological claims are being made about those documents.

12 / EVIDENCE VERDICT: WHICH PROPOSITION SURVIVES THE TESTS?

Evidence verdict: which proposition survives the tests?

Documented in the sources: a tradition of early Quranic collection and standardisation; reports attributed to Aisha, Umar and Ubayy about earlier or competing recitations; the development of a classical explanation involving abrogation; material evidence of an early shared written archetype alongside a non-standard early undertext. Those facts should be neither concealed nor inflated.

Strongly supported historical inference: the familiar Uthmanic written text type was fixed relatively early and reproduced with substantial fidelity. Not independently established by that inference: identity between the standard canon and every utterance ever described as revealed, or a fully recoverable mechanism proving that all excluded material was intentionally abrogated rather than mistakenly omitted.

Conclusion: the strongest criticism does not assert that Uthman maliciously fabricated the Quran. It challenges the move from an early stable canonical text to absolute claims about divine preservation when the tradition itself remembers recited material outside that canon. Muslim explanations may produce internal consistency, but their applicability to each disputed passage requires evidence separate from the current text's existence.
