01 / THE ISSUE

The official codex is early; its exact correspondence to all original recitations is another question

CENTRAL RESEARCH QUESTION

The sources establish that a standard Quranic written tradition emerged very early, but they do not independently demonstrate that Uthman's selected recension exhaustively reproduces every form of the revelation originally delivered to Muhammad. [2] [9] [10]

Uthman ibn Affan was the third Rashidun caliph and a companion who knew Muhammad. The objection is not that an uninformed outsider wrote the Quran from scratch. It is that a political and editorial process chose a normative written form, eliminated competing written materials and thereby limited later comparison of what was standardised against what was excluded.

02 / RECONSTRUCT THE CHRONOLOGY

Abu Bakr's collection and Uthman's standardisation are not the same event

Sahih al-Bukhari 4986 portrays Abu Bakr, Umar and Zayd as collecting Quranic material after the deaths of reciters in battle. Bukhari 4987 describes a later Uthmanic committee copying Hafsa's collected sheets into official codices. [1] [2]

These reports put Muhammad's companions and a reported former revelation scribe close to the process. This is genuine evidence of a claimed chain of custody, not proof of a complete, universally distributed autograph written and verified by Muhammad himself.

It is also important to recognise that these are reports preserved in later hadith collections. Historical assessment must examine their transmission and compare their account with surviving early manuscripts, rather than treating the collection narratives as contemporary meeting minutes.

03 / UTHMAN'S COMMITTEE

Four men were appointed to produce the official copies

Uthman instructed Zayd ibn Thabit, Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr, Said ibn al-As and Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Harith ibn Hisham to produce standard copies, according to Bukhari 4987. The committee used material held by Hafsa, and the report explicitly contemplates disagreement between Zayd and the three Qurayshi members. [2]

This was a rule-governed editorial project, not a random individual improvisation. Yet the narrative leaves a real historical question: what objective evidence establishes that the chosen written outcome preserved every relevant original form, including variations that were later excluded? Authority to decide and proof of textual completeness are not interchangeable.

04 / A REAL DECISION RULE

Uthman's instruction made a preferred dialect authoritative

Bukhari 4987 and 3506 say that when the Qurayshi men disagreed with Zayd, they should write according to the language of Quraysh. The rule was justified by the assertion that the Quran was revealed in that language. [2] [4]

The committee's rule could be consistent with faithful preservation of an original primary dialect. But it is an explicit decision procedure, not neutral proof that no other originally authorised forms existed. The distinct question is whether a standard textual form is historically identical to the only original reading or is one selection from a range accepted in early practice.

05 / THE CRITICAL EVIDENCE PROBLEM

Burning the other written materials removed opportunities for comparison

Bukhari 4987 says Uthman sent official codices to the provinces and ordered other Quranic materials, whether fragments or complete copies, to be burned. The report describes an effort to reduce regional recitational division. [2]

This action is historically significant even if its motive was unity rather than deception. Destroying alternate written exemplars narrows the surviving comparative record. Today, the degree of agreement among descendants of the official text is not equivalent to agreement among all the earlier forms if competing evidence was deliberately eliminated.

The report does not tell us the full contents of the destroyed items. It cannot be used to prove that they necessarily contained lost divine verses, nor can the official codices be used to show what was in materials that no longer survive.

06 / INDIVIDUAL VERIFICATION

The reports describe searches for verses, not an effortless mechanical copying

In Bukhari 4986, Zayd reports finding the final verses of Surat al-Tawba with one named companion when collecting the text. In Bukhari 4988, during Uthman's copying, he says a verse of al-Ahzab he remembered hearing was missing from the available material and was found with Khuzayma. [1] [3]

These episodes support a story of diligence and verification. They also show that the process, as remembered in Islamic tradition, involved searching and checking evidence rather than opening a single indisputable complete master document. A report that a verse was recovered is not proof that the final text omitted that verse; indeed the tradition says it was included.

07 / WHAT DOES MEMORY ESTABLISH?

Oral transmission is evidence, but it cannot independently certify itself

Bukhari 4992 reports two recitations that Muhammad was said to accept. Bukhari 5003 names people who gathered or recited the Quran during his lifetime. These reports attest the importance placed on recitation and memorisation. [5] [17]

Named reciters with a plausible chain can support historical confidence. Yet to prove that all competing original forms were faithfully transmitted, one must assess which transmitters are genuinely independent, when their traditions are first recoverable and whether their wording agrees with contemporaneous manuscripts. A tradition cannot validate its own completeness simply by asserting that its transmitters remembered everything.

Orality was not unique to Arabia or Islam. Jewish and Christian communities also transmitted scripture and teachings orally alongside writing. A standard that accepts Quranic oral continuity but dismisses all Christian oral continuity as inherently unreliable is inconsistent.

Sahih al-Bukhari 5005 attributes to Umar the statement that some of Ubayy's recitation was set aside, despite Ubayy's insistence that Muhammad taught it to him. The report invokes Quran 2:106. That is evidence of a debate within the later transmission tradition, not proof that a particular alternative reading was divinely authoritative. It still requires an explanation for how an officially retained text can be independently checked against previously taught but excluded material. [19]

Sahih Muslim 1452a likewise attributes to Aisha material absent from the standard codex which she described as still recited at Muhammad's death. An abrogation account may resolve this within classical theology; an external proof of the historical withdrawal needs positive corroboration rather than an inference from present absence alone. [20]

08 / MANUSCRIPT COUNTERCHECK

The Sanaa lower text documents a different early written text type

Sadeghi and Goudarzi identify the lower text of the Sanaa palimpsest as a distinctive early Quranic text tradition, separate from the standard Uthmanic type. This is physical evidence that alternative Quranic written forms were not merely hypothetical products of much later debate. [10]

That finding should be used precisely. The existence of a different text type does not prove that the Uthmanic codex is generally corrupt or that the lower text contains a complete and superior original. It defeats the absolute assertion that no early written alternatives existed, and requires an explanation of their relationship to the standard text.

09 / THE STRONGEST COUNTEREVIDENCE

Early manuscript agreement corroborates a written archetype

Marijn van Putten's comparison of unusual orthographic patterns across fourteen early Quranic manuscripts found strong evidence for a shared written ancestor, plausibly very early and consistent with Uthmanic codification. The pattern is evidence of copying from a written archetype, not merely reconstructing a book through oral dictation generations later. [9]

The Birmingham parchment likewise provides early material evidence for parts of the Quranic text, although it does not preserve an entire codex and its radiocarbon date applies to the parchment rather than directly to the writing. [11]

This evidence weakens a claim that the Quran was assembled from scratch centuries after Muhammad. It does not, however, compare every lost pre-standardisation version with the official archetype or prove supernatural preservation.

10 / WHAT WAS REVEALED?

A report about a revealed stoning verse raises a further scope question

In Bukhari 6829, Umar warns that people may later deny an obligation because they do not find the verse of stoning in the Book; he states that such material was revealed and known. [6]

Classical Islamic theology can distinguish revealed material whose recitation was abrogated from recited verses preserved in the final codex; Quran 2:106 forms part of that broader interpretive discussion. [16]

The report therefore cannot simply be cited as proof of an accidental missing verse. It does, however, require a claim of perfect preservation to define whether it concerns every revelation ever delivered or only the final text intended for ongoing recitation.

The related report in Sahih Muslim 1691a is explicit that Umar believed the stoning verse had been revealed, recited and memorised. The strong historical conclusion is that a tradition preserving this attribution existed; the report alone does not demonstrate whether the missing recitation was intentionally abrogated or excluded in error. The distinction between the text's recitation and a legal ruling that persists must be established, not presumed. [18]

The detailed passage-level challenge, including Aisha's separate report about breastfeeding recitations and the classical response of abrogation, is treated in CI-032: Abrogated recitation and Quranic preservation.

11 / A CONSISTENT COMPARISON

The Bible cannot be rejected solely because its transmission used oral tradition

Christian transmission includes early oral proclamation, written accounts, manuscript copying and later ecclesiastical decisions about canonical books. Paul identifies a tradition he received in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, while Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus materially attest substantial New Testament text before Islam. [12] [13] [14]

The two histories are not identical. Uthman's reported attempt to eliminate competing written Quranic material is not directly equivalent to every stage of Christian canon formation, and Biblical manuscripts preserve numerous textual variants. Nonetheless, both sets of evidence should be evaluated for date, diversity, source relationships, variant readings and corroboration.

The claim that oral transmission proves the Quran reliable while oral transmission inherently discredits the Bible fails a consistent evidentiary standard. Oral and written continuity require independent assessment in each tradition, not a special exemption for one.

12 / A CROSS-SCRIPTURAL TENSION

A corruption allegation about Christian scripture needs the same proof

Quran 5:46-48 describes earlier Gospel material as guidance, while claiming the Quran confirms and exercises authority over prior revelation. If Christian passages already present before Islam are labelled corrupt, the allegation must identify the passage, proposed time of change and supporting transmission evidence. [15]

A Muslim account may distinguish the revealed Injil from the later four written Gospels. That is a coherent theological distinction, but it is not a historical demonstration that a particular verse was added. Conversely, early Quranic standardisation does not itself show that its written archetype is fabricated.

13 / FOUR DISTINCT PRESERVATION CLAIMS REQUIRE FOUR DISTINCT TESTS

Four distinct preservation claims require four distinct tests

Preservation is often presented as a single undivided proposition, but Quranic transmission has several distinct layers. A consonantal skeleton, or rasm, is a written pattern; spelling conventions concern how that pattern is represented; vocalisation and consonant pointing supply information absent from many early copies; and qira'at are reading traditions which may differ in pronunciation, grammar or the consonantal form itself. A claim that all four were identical at every stage requires evidence about each layer, not just an early manuscript with a familiar word sequence. [9] [21] [22]

Other distinctions concern the object being preserved. One proposition asserts that the text used today has remained materially stable since an early written archetype. A stronger proposition asserts it exactly reproduces one authoritative seventh-century recension. The strongest asserts it reproduces every revealed word and divinely authorised reading taught by Muhammad, including those lost before our first surviving witnesses. Each additional clause creates a further historical burden.

Modern printed editions preserve editorial conventions too, including pointing, vowel signs and verse divisions which are not identical to the sparse appearance of many early witnesses. Those conventions are not automatically errors, and a manuscript's lack of dots does not prove it was meaningless or read without established pronunciation. Nonetheless, an undotted consonantal witness cannot by itself directly certify every later recited phoneme or the exclusion of a competing reading. Preservation claims should state what evidence they actually address.

14 / REGIONAL RASM VARIANTS STRENGTHEN THE HISTORICAL CASE FOR UTHMANIC DISTRIBUTION

Regional rasm variants strengthen the historical case for Uthmanic distribution

Hythem Sidky's investigation of regional Quranic codices tests the reported distribution of exemplars to centres such as Medina, Kufa, Basra and Syria against orthographic differences in early manuscripts. Using philology and a reconstructed manuscript family tree, Sidky identifies four ancestral manuscript branches among the sources analysed. This lends independent material support to a regional written codex system consistent with the standardisation narrative, rather than assuming the narrative was invented wholesale centuries later. [21]

The same study warns that later lists of regional variants are not necessarily direct records created when the alleged exemplars were first dispatched. Some knowledge of their differences appears to have been collected through later comparison of manuscripts. Sidky also identifies a previously unrecognised neo-Basran subgroup. These findings show that even within the official tradition a history of local copying and orthographic variation must be reconstructed, not asserted from one retrospective report. [21]

The strongest outcome favours a very early canonical consonantal network. This is inconvenient for a theory of a Quran wholly invented during the later Abbasid period, and it should be acknowledged. But evidence that several manuscript families descend from a structured official lineage cannot demonstrate the exact content of lost materials outside that lineage. Genealogy shows how surviving descendants are related; it does not independently attest the ancestry of every destroyed or superseded competitor.

15 / SHARED UNUSUAL SPELLINGS ESTABLISH WRITTEN COPYING RATHER THAN IDENTICAL ORAL RECALL

Shared unusual spellings establish written copying rather than identical oral recall

Marijn van Putten's study, "The Grace of God as Evidence for a Written Uthmanic Archetype", compares unusual spellings across fourteen early manuscripts and finds that shared orthographic idiosyncrasies strongly favour copying from a common written model. Such shared quirks are especially probative because independent reciters who remembered the same spoken words would not necessarily generate the same unusual spellings. The analysis supplies a concrete test of textual genealogy rather than a broad assertion that all copies look alike. [9]

This is positive evidence against the claim that the familiar written Quran arose only as a late reconstruction from separate oral memorisations. The relationship between manuscripts is not merely one of overlapping themes or conventional formulas: the same peculiar graphic choices can indicate copying dependence. The finding does not rule out oral recitation within that community, but the convergence of surviving written copies is better explained in important respects by a shared written ancestor. [9] [22]

There is a converse limit. Strong agreement among descendants of a common exemplar is less independent evidence than agreement among unrelated textual branches. A hundred copies reproducing one ancestor can show that ancestor's stability with great confidence, yet cannot recover alternative books never copied into that family. The paper therefore strengthens one component of preservation while leaving the more expansive originality claim unverified.

16 / BIRMINGHAM'S DATED PARCHMENT IS IMPORTANT BUT NOT A DATED AUTOGRAPH

Birmingham's dated parchment is important but not a dated autograph

The University of Birmingham reports that the parchment of its early Quran manuscript was radiocarbon dated to 568-645 CE at 95.4 per cent probability. The manuscript preserves portions of surahs 18-20, not a complete Quran, and is written in an early Hijazi style with limited diacritical marking and no full vocalisation. These facts anchor the physical witness close to Islam's earliest period and should be treated as important positive evidence. [11] [24] [25]

The university explicitly distinguishes the death date of the animal whose skin became parchment from the later moment when ink was placed upon it. Scientific examination has characterised the inks, but no reliable direct test has assigned a precise calendar date to the writing. The date window cannot therefore be presented as proof that every visible letter was written while Muhammad was alive, or that a complete master codex existed before Uthman. At the other extreme, its early script and material context do not justify casually dismissing it as a much later fabrication. [24] [25]

Nor is this manuscript a palimpsest. The university's multispectral study found no hidden underlying writing, differentiating it physically from the Sanaa lower-text material. This illustrates why manuscript witnesses must be identified individually before assigning a variant, a dating method or a physical feature to them. It also underscores a recurring limitation: a manuscript may securely show what some people copied while remaining silent about lost competitors and all unattested passages. [25]

17 / THE SANAA PALIMPSEST CONTAINS TWO DISTINGUISHABLE TEXTUAL LAYERS

The Sanaa palimpsest contains two distinguishable textual layers

The Sanaa 1 palimpsest was written on parchment previously used for an earlier Quranic text. The lower writing was erased or washed and later overwritten. Behnam Sadeghi and Mohsen Goudarzi analyse the lower text as a distinct early textual tradition rather than a mere unpointed copy of the standard Uthmanic consonantal text. This is a material witness to textual plurality, independently of later reports describing alternative Companion codices. [10] [26]

The distinction between the erased lower layer and the upper layer matters: it is the lower text which carries the strongest evidence for an alternative text type. To infer that the entire early Quran circulated in indefinitely many incompatible versions would overstate one surviving witness. Sadeghi and Goudarzi also argue that surah units took form before the reported Uthmanic standardisation, which cuts against an argument that the committee created the contents of all surahs from scratch. [10]

Neither a photograph of overlapping ink nor a modern reconstructed transcription should be mistaken for a complete untouched earlier Quran. Some letters are damaged or must be recovered through imaging and palaeographic reasoning. The evidence supports the existence of textual variation beyond conventional late vocalisation. Establishing whether a particular lower-text reading was original, erroneous, deliberately edited or otherwise authorised requires comparisons which cannot be settled simply by preferring whichever witness supports a theological conclusion.

For located comparisons of Quran 2:194, 2:196 and 2:201 in Stanford 2007 and Birmingham variants, see CI-003: folio-level manuscript variation.

18 / NOT EVERY TEXTUAL VARIANT CHANGES THE SAME KIND OF INFORMATION

Not every textual variant changes the same kind of information

Historical analysis must sort differences into categories before drawing conclusions about preservation. Spelling variations can represent the same pronounced word. Pointing and vowels may produce grammatical or phonetic variants from a compatible skeleton. A different consonantal pattern may create a different word, and changes involving word order, additions or omissions can be significant to the reconstruction of an early text. The distinction between a variant and a meaningful doctrinal contradiction is a further independent question. [9] [10] [21]

The Sanaa lower-text material documents differences not reducible to the simple claim that one early manuscript had no vowels. Within the standard tradition, regional orthographic patterns may still reflect an early shared programme of copying. These findings can coexist: a stable dominant written archetype does not erase evidence of another textual type, while an alternative type does not show that every surviving Uthmanic passage is wrong. [10] [21]

A responsible edition should provide the actual Arabic reading, its witness, the editor's confidence and an explanation of whether the difference affects meaning. It should not present a transliteration reconstructed from damaged traces as a complete independently preserved Quran. Equally, a preservation argument cannot dismiss a genuinely different word as insignificant merely because its theological implications are not immediately dramatic.

19 / THE LATER CANONICAL QIRA'AT ARE NOT IDENTICAL TO THE EARLIEST RASM

The later canonical qira'at are not identical to the earliest rasm

Shady Hekmat Nasser's research traces the historical transmission and canonisation of the Quran's variant reading traditions. These traditions involve chains of reciters and systems of reading, while the mushaf is a written text. Many differences in canonical readings can be represented through the undotted or unvocalised skeleton of a manuscript, but not every transmitted reading can be certified by that skeleton alone. [22] [27]

A distinction must also be made between the early reports about the seven ahruf and the later formal canon of seven or ten named reading traditions. The similarity of the number seven does not establish that the two systems coincide. Named readers and their transmitters were part of a subsequent scholarly history, and Nasser's argument about the conditions of tawatur, mass transmission, is a scholarly thesis to evaluate rather than a finding encoded in a seventh-century document. [5] [27]

Consequently, a manuscript with familiar consonants does not independently prove that every later vocalised reading was delivered by Muhammad. Nor does recognition of multiple canonical readings demonstrate that they must all be inauthentic. Establishing a specific reading's historical pedigree requires dated witnesses, transmission criticism and a distinction between the earliest possible attestation and the time the tradition first claimed for itself.

20 / A PRESENT-DAY PRINTED READING IS NOT A DIRECT PHOTOGRAPH OF THE FIRST CODEX

A present-day printed reading is not a direct photograph of the first codex

The familiar modern Quran is usually encountered as a printed and vocalised text representing a particular reading tradition, often Hafs from Asim. It uses consonant points, vowel signs and editorial layout that support public recitation. An early consonantal manuscript may share its core textual structure while lacking these aids. The absence of later graphic features from an early copy is not itself proof that its readers lacked any recitation practice. [21] [27]

This provides a useful falsification framework. A historical claim about the early consonantal text should be tested against early written codices. A claim about a specific oral pronunciation should be tested against evidence for that reading tradition. A claim about every word and recitation alleged to have been taught by Muhammad additionally requires a way to evaluate alternative reports and witnesses. A single match between an early rasm and today's printed skeleton proves only part of the strongest claim.

François Déroche's study of the early Quran examines the historical movement from flexible textual practice towards an authoritative and materially transformed book. The monograph is a scholarly synthesis, not a previously unknown seventh-century autograph. It is useful for reconstructing what manuscript evidence can show and for identifying the limits of treating present-day typography as direct evidence of the original stage. [23]

21 / BUKHARI'S COMPILATION REPORTS AND THE LIMITS OF THEIR HISTORICAL AUTHORITY

Bukhari's compilation reports and the limits of their historical authority

The accounts of Abu Bakr's collection and Uthman's distribution are preserved in later hadith compilations. Bukhari 4986, 4987 and 4988 name officials, describe the retrieval of written material and offer explanations for selecting a written standard. These details are not empty: they provide concrete historical claims which can be tested against what is independently known about early codices. Yet the reported events occurred long before Bukhari compiled his collection, so a citation to it cannot substitute for a contemporary committee record. [1] [2] [3]

The reports also have internal implications that are often overlooked. They do not say Uthman had never met Muhammad, that nobody remembered revelation or that the committee casually invented surahs. They do say that people were concerned about divergent recitations, that a preferred linguistic rule was adopted for disputed material, and that other written copies were destroyed. Each claim could be historically significant without automatically explaining every surviving variant. [2] [4]

A rigorous reconstruction asks whether manuscript genealogy independently supports the reported broad chronology, whether the regional copies exhibit a common written source, and whether known alternative text types are compatible with the reports. Sidky's regional findings and van Putten's shared spellings strengthen parts of the narrative. The Sanaa lower text supports plurality. None makes the whole narrative a word-for-word historical transcript or proves that every written material destroyed was defective. [9] [10] [21]

22 / SEVEN AHRUF, COMPANION REPORTS AND A CLAIM OF AUTHORISED PLURALITY

Seven ahruf, companion reports and a claim of authorised plurality

Sahih al-Bukhari 4992 records a dispute over variant recitation in which Muhammad is portrayed as accepting both forms and referring to seven ahruf. If this report is historically authentic in the form presented, it creates a preservation question: what relationship did multiple authorised modes bear to the official written standard? The report's existence is clear; exactly which early pronunciations or textual forms it authenticates is not automatically recoverable. [5]

Alleged companion readings also demand differentiated treatment. The account about Ubayy in Bukhari 5005 attributes an objection to setting aside something he claimed to have learned from Muhammad. This documents a later recorded tension between authorised recitation and official practice, but cannot independently show that the particular excluded material was originally revealed. Its authenticity and original wording remain historical questions. Such testimony must not be accepted without scrutiny merely because it creates a difficulty for preservation. [19]

The reverse move is equally insufficient: declaring an earlier contested reading abrogated solely because it is absent from the eventual standard assumes the answer. The Quran explicitly contains a replacement principle in 2:106, yet the specific claim that a disputed form had been divinely withdrawn requires a basis stronger than its present exclusion. A proposed theory must explain which forms were originally authorised, which were intentionally retired, and how that distinction can be verified rather than inferred from surviving canon. [16] [28]

23 / THE CHRONOLOGY OF EVENTS, MANUSCRIPTS AND EDITED WITNESSES CANNOT BE MERGED

The chronology of events, manuscripts and edited witnesses cannot be merged

The claimed lifetime revelations are conventionally placed in Muhammad's career before his death in 632 CE. The traditional Uthmanic standardisation belongs to the middle of the seventh century, often placed around 650. The Birmingham parchment's calibrated age range is 568-645 CE for its material, not necessarily its writing. Manuscripts written in the later seventh or eighth century can attest a particular text type but are not automatically an autograph from 632. The broad synthesis of these dates is historically plausible without collapsing the uncertainties. [11] [24] [25]

The same distinction governs comparison with printed editions and transmitted hadith reports. The survival of a first-millennium physical manuscript gives earlier direct evidence of wording than a ninth-century literary report, but the report may preserve remembered claims about earlier events which a manuscript cannot narrate. Neither kind of source cancels the other. The proper question is where they agree and where an assertion lacks an independently recoverable test. [1] [2] [9] [23]

24 / FALSIFICATION TESTS AND SEPARATE TEXTUAL, HISTORICAL AND THEOLOGICAL VERDICTS

Falsification tests and separate textual, historical and theological verdicts

The strongest preservation account proposes that Uthman's standard represented a faithfully transmitted original primary form and that variants were authorised alternatives, copying errors or subsequently withdrawn readings. This is not automatically disproved by plurality; a consistently identified original primary form could coexist with secondary variation. To establish it historically, the proponent must demonstrate how those classes are distinguished without assuming that the final official text is correct simply because it became official. [2] [9] [10]

Falsification test: a securely dated pre-standardisation manuscript or independent early quotation containing a clear, meaningful, securely read difference from the standard, together with strong evidence that this difference was prophetic and not scribal, would directly weaken exact-originality claims. Conversely, a reliable complete early authoritatively checked exemplar could strengthen the primary-original claim. A damaged palimpsest fragment or a later reported reading cannot supply all of these conditions automatically.

Text-first verdict: the Quran claims protection in 15:9 and 41:41-42, while its later written transmission is a separate fact requiring documents. Historical verdict: a very early and relatively stable standard consonantal tradition is strongly supported; earlier textual plurality is also real, while exhaustively reproducing every authorised original form is not independently proved. Biblical comparison: the same source-critical tests of manuscripts, oral reports and canonical decisions must apply to Christian scripture without exempting either tradition. [7] [8] [9] [10] [12] [13]

The verdict does not prove that God could not have preserved any original utterance, nor that all versions were meaning-changing, nor that scribes deliberately falsified the Quran. It rejects a substantially stronger documentary claim: that official descendants and later recitation reports independently certify every earlier form without any missing comparative evidence. The available witnesses cannot meet that universal burden.

25 / MARCEL 2: IDENTIFY THE PHYSICAL WITNESS

NLR Marcel 2 is an early parchment witness, not an autograph of the first recension

The National Library of Russia in Saint Petersburg holds the manuscript under the shelfmark Marcel 2; the Corpus Coranicum register identifies it by that shelfmark. It must not be confused with Marcel 5, Marcel 9 or other items in the same historical collection. Daniel Brubaker describes Marcel 2 as a group of 42 parchment folios, with 20-21 lines per page, in a large format classified as early Kufic C.Ia. His suggested early eighth-century date rests on manuscript features and palaeography, not an independently cited radiocarbon result for the writing. [29] [30]

This is a post-Muhammad material witness and, on that approximate dating, likely postdates the traditional Uthmanic standardisation. It can establish that a particular correction was made to a copied Quranic page. It does not directly reveal what Uthman's committee held in its hands, what Muhammad originally recited or when the correction itself was made. Distinguish the age of the material, the production of the script, the physical alteration and the date when a modern scholar examined it. [30] [9]

Access limit: the publicly available catalogue confirms the manuscript's identity, and Brubaker publishes a photograph of the relevant area, but we have not independently inspected the original folio or obtained multispectral imaging. Any assertion about individual erased strokes below is therefore attributed to the published examination, not a new direct collation. The photograph is linked in the reference rather than republished without permission. [29] [30]

26 / A SPECIFIC ARABIC COMPARISON

Quran 30:9, folio 30v: what is legible and what was erased?

The relevant phrase in the modern standard Arabic text of Quran 30:9 is كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ, kayfa kana aqibatu alladhina min qablihim, meaning roughly, "what was the fate of those before them?" This verse warns its hearers to consider what befell earlier peoples. In this study the modern Arabic is a comparison point, not a reconstructed reading of the erased strokes. [33]

He also notes a separate marginal addition on the page and reports 26 corrections in the surviving Marcel 2 fragment overall; these are his observations and count, not an independently checked inventory reproduced here. Most importantly, Brubaker expressly states that he cannot identify the erased text. His speculative grammatical possibilities illustrate the question, but must not be quoted as if they were once legible on the parchment. [30]

The observed state and the proposed earlier state require separate descriptions: corrected witness: the text agrees locally with the standard rasm; earlier erased wording: unknown. It would be inaccurate both to proclaim an authenticated extra Quranic word and to pretend that the alteration did not occur. [30] [31]

27 / TEST THE STRONGEST ALTERNATIVE

A duplicated word is a serious alternative to deliberate removal of a variant

Brubaker considers whether the space once contained extra wording, yet identifies no actual letters. Sidky instead proposes dittography: the scribe may have accidentally written الذين twice, once at the end of folio 30v and again at the start of the next page, and then removed the first occurrence. His explanation fits the page boundary and a common Quranic formula. It is a testable scribal mechanism, not a verified reading of invisible ink. [30] [31]

Sidky also notes that some illustrative alternatives offered by Brubaker do not match the grammatical case required after the construct noun aqiba. That does not disprove the existence of the erasure. It does undermine the claim that a specific alternative can be inferred from mere physical space without checking Arabic syntax. Neither a theological commitment to preservation nor an opposing expectation of textual alteration determines the missing letters. [31] [30]

The two authors also disagree about the larger inference from corrections. Sidky warns that describing alterations as moving toward "the 1924 Cairo edition" can be anachronistic because that edition reflects earlier written traditions. Brubaker responds that the modern Cairo edition serves as a comparison standard, not an alleged goal consciously pursued by the earlier scribes. The compatible, narrower finding is that the extant correction can be compared with a modern standard; its motivation and date require separate evidence. [31] [32]

28 / DISTINCT TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL VERDICTS

Marcel 2 disproves neither early standardisation nor the need to scrutinise copying

Text-first finding: the standard Quran 30:9 has عاقبة الذين. In Brubaker's description of Marcel 2 folio 30v, the surviving text after an erasure agrees with that sequence across the page boundary. An earlier reading cannot be established from an empty area. A correction is an identifiable physical event; a confirmed distinct textual variant is an additional claim requiring a legible earlier reading. [30] [31] [33]

Historical verdict, on the balance of probabilities: the published photographs and descriptions provide credible evidence of human scribal correction in an early Quran manuscript. Sidky's duplicated-word explanation is a serious, economical alternative, but cannot be demonstrated without the underlying text. Marcel 2 therefore does not carry the evidential weight of the Sanaa palimpsest's readable non-Uthmanic undertext. The latter can establish an alternative early textual type; this erasure cannot establish what text was removed. [10] [30] [31]

Burden of proof: a claim that every material Quranic copy was always letter-for-letter identical fails in the presence of alterations, and it is unnecessary to deny such alterations. A broader claim that the early Uthmanic consonantal archetype was stable is not refuted by an undetermined local correction. The stronger religious claim that every original revealed wording survives still cannot be proved merely by checking that the later corrected text conforms to descendants of the official tradition. [9] [21] [30]

Falsification test: publication of a high-quality folio image or validated multispectral reading that reveals a different word would materially strengthen the nonstandard-reading hypothesis. A readable duplicated alladhina would instead strengthen dittography. A dateable collation of independent witnesses bearing the same extra reading would carry more weight than grammatical conjecture. Until such evidence is available, the correction should be published with its location and uncertainty, not promoted into a forged transcription. [29] [30] [31]

For the separate question of an early genuinely non-Uthmanic textual type, see CI-003 on manuscript preservation and the Sanaa comparison above. Marcel 2 is a useful, bounded case study within the wider Uthmanic authenticity investigation.

29 / PARISINO-PETROPOLITANUS

Identify the physical witness before counting variants

Corpus Coranicum catalogues BnF Arabe 328(a) as 56 parchment folios in Hijazi I script, with Fustat provenance and an estimated date of 640-680 CE. Its record supplies no radiocarbon result for this shelfmark. The estimate is not an independently measured date of ink or later corrections. The Parisino-petropolitanus includes dispersed leaves of a single manuscript; the Paris and Saint Petersburg portions are not independent copies. It must not be confused with the Birmingham manuscript associated with Arabe 328(c), or Marcel 2. [34] [35] [36]

Dutton identifies consonantal features associated with the Syrian reading of Ibn Amir. Déroche's examination distinguishes multiple copyists and subsequent interventions. Regional association is evidence of textual ancestry, not necessarily the exact place of production or identity of a reader. The normalized Arabic examples below convey the readings identified in published scholarship rather than reproducing every unpointed manuscript stroke. [34] [35]

31 / PARISINO-PETROPOLITANUS

Quran 7:43 and 3:189: the finished copy differs from earlier writing

At 7:43 (f. 31v, l. 24), Dutton identifies a conjunction above the line. He interprets the spacing as indicating an earlier ما كنا without waw, consistent with Ibn Amir; the addition gives وما كنا, consistent with Hafs. This is an inference from the placement of the ink, not an independently dated intervention. The meaning is substantially similar, but the evidence of editing is material. [34] [41]

At 3:189, f. 9r, Déroche (2014), p. 30 and n. 68, reconstructs the earlier wording والله ملك السماوات والأرض (God is king of the heavens and earth), compared with ولله ملك السماوات والأرض (to God belongs the dominion of the heavens and earth). The former vocalises the unpointed mlk as malik and the latter as mulk. A removed alif distinguishes the consonantal sequences. The grammatical construction therefore differs, though both express divine sovereignty. [35] [42]

A copying lapse is a plausible explanation. The correction alone does not identify the scribe, the original oral revelation or an intention to suppress an alternative. Conversely, the corrected appearance cannot establish that the initial writing matched the standard all along.

32 / PARISINO-PETROPOLITANUS

Quran 4:12: a partially recoverable inheritance correction

The standard passage includes وله أخ أو أخت (and he has a brother or sister). Corpus Coranicum's transcription of f. 10v, l. 18 records the corresponding current wording. Déroche (2014), p. 31 and n. 72, reports an earlier feminine ولها corrected to masculine وله. The remaining erased context is not fully recoverable. [35] [36] [43]

In a legal provision the gender of a referent can matter. Here, however, the nearby text mentions a man or a woman and an isolated pronoun does not establish the antecedent in the earlier formulation. No alternative inheritance fractions can be calculated from it. Déroche's later account (2021), pp. 164-165, likewise makes recovery of further writing necessary to establish a different legal arrangement. [23] [35]

Unlike the wholly unreadable Marcel 2 erasure, a specific earlier component is identified here. Unlike the Sanaa lower-text compensation list, the entire earlier legal expression is not established. New imaging and independently located parallels could resolve that difference.

34 / PARISINO-PETROPOLITANUS

What these comparisons establish about preservation

Text-first verdict: universal consonant-for-consonant identity across all early copies fails the documented comparisons. Canonical regional variation and distinct stages of corrected writing are observable, even when the doctrinal meaning remains broadly equivalent.

Historical verdict: evidence of a predominant early standard and its regional branches is not evidence that each earlier form was divinely authorised or that the surviving standard exhausts the original revelation. Conversely, an earlier scribal error would not establish deliberate deletion of revealed wording. Each explanation has its own burden. [2] [9] [21] [34] [35]

Falsification test: imaging could confirm or revise the proposed initial wording in 3:189 or recover the missing context of 4:12. Independent early manuscript parallels would strengthen the hypothesis of transmitted rather than accidental variation. Further Marcel loci require individually located readings.

35 / MARCEL MANUSCRIPT CORRECTIONS

Distinguish the Marcel shelfmarks from independent codices

The National Library of Russia's Marcel collection contains fragments rather than a series of uniformly dated complete Qurans. Corpus Coranicum identifies Marcel 13 as 42 parchment folios, with an estimated production range of 660-750 CE, and associates Marcel 11 and 15 with the same manuscript. The Paris fragment Arabe 330(c) is also suggested as a related part. These are not four independent early copies. [44] [30]

Marcel 5 is a separate parchment witness of 17 folios in Kufic B.Ib script, associated with Arabe 335 and other dispersed leaves. The catalogue reports a radiocarbon interval of 652-763 CE at 95.4% for the associated Leiden fragment, not a direct ink date for Marcel 5. Brubaker's description dates the first portion of composite Marcel 21 approximately to the early eighth century and Marcel 7 to the ninth or tenth century on script grounds. A correction need not date to the production of its leaf. [45] [30]

37 / MARCEL MANUSCRIPT CORRECTIONS

Marcel 13: omissions that do and do not leave coherent grammar

Three more interventions are described in Brubaker's Example 3, p. 37. At 22:40, f. 20v, the received expression يذكر فيها اسم الله كثيرا explicitly identifies whose name is mentioned. The earlier sequence without Allah, يذكر فيها اسم كثيرا, could still communicate the notion of a name being mentioned, if vocalised appropriately. Its exact earlier case endings and definiteness should not be inferred from the absent noun alone. [30] [50]

At 24:51, f. 23r, the received words دعوا إلى الله ورسوله contain a preposition followed by two coordinated expressions. Removing Allah leaves دعوا إلى ورسوله; the preposition is left without its expected first complement. At 35:11, f. 26r, the received expression إن ذلك على الله يسير becomes إن ذلك على يسير if the divine name is removed, leaving an incomplete or abnormal construction. Brubaker acknowledges the difficulties; Sidky (2019), pp. 285-286, treats these as evidence favouring accidental omission. [30] [31] [51] [52]

The stronger explanation for these two cases is an ordinary copying lapse. That conclusion is compatible with correction of the manuscript and does not restore character-for-character identity to its earlier writing. It does, however, weaken an argument that every reported insertion of Allah represents deliberate theological innovation.

38 / MARCEL MANUSCRIPT CORRECTIONS

Marcel 7: the Hour is inserted, but an earlier error is likely

Brubaker, Example 18, pp. 79-80, reports the superscript addition of الساعة at Quran 6:40, Marcel 7 f. 7r. The received clause is أو أتتكم الساعة, referring to the arrival of the Hour. Without that noun, أو أتتكم leaves the second event poorly specified. Sidky's review, p. 282, reads this as an accidental omission that makes the parallel construction defective. [30] [31] [54]

This intervention is significant as evidence that even a manuscript copied centuries after Uthman could be corrected. It does not establish that an early Quran lacked eschatological teaching: the witness is relatively late, the correction is not securely dated, and the surrounding passage points to a copying error. A copy's later correction cannot be used to date the standard reading itself.

39 / MARCEL MANUSCRIPT CORRECTIONS

Marcel 5: the original wording is not recoverable by guesswork

At Quran 34:27, Marcel 5 f. 11r, Brubaker (2019), Example 19, pp. 81-82, describes هو الله written over erased material. This matches the standard local phrase بل هو الله العزيز الحكيم. Both Brubaker and Sidky consider the possibility that the erased space held هو alone. Sidky's review relates this to a recurrent Quranic formula in which the pronoun is followed by divine attributes. An assimilation to a familiar expression is therefore a plausible copying explanation; the exact erased wording is not demonstrated. [30] [31] [45] [55]

Do not treat the proposed shorter form as an observed reading. The overwritten text, the erasure and its probable cause are three different evidential propositions. This case differs from an above-line insertion whose surrounding earlier wording is legible. It also differs from the Sanaa 2:196 legal-list variant, for which a shorter local expression is recoverable.

40 / MARCEL MANUSCRIPT CORRECTIONS

What the additional corrections establish and what would change the verdict

Text-first verdict: the reported insertions identify concrete places where the earlier writing did not contain a word now present in the received text. Several have little semantic effect because an implicit subject or context conveys the same referent; others repair defective grammar. The two overwritten examples, 33:18 and 34:27, must not be counted as certain earlier readings. [30] [31]

Historical verdict: Sidky identifies older or contemporary manuscripts already containing the standard divine name at 33:18, 33:24, 33:73, 41:21 and 22:40 (p. 286, n. 32). The existence of those parallels weighs against using the insertions as evidence that the standard wording itself arose late. Their early attestation is distinct from independent proof that Muhammad authorised each word. A shared copyist's multiple omissions cannot be treated as independent testimony, and the number of corrections selected by a researcher is not a statistically representative measure of the entire textual tradition.

Falsification test: establish when each correction was made, recover any erased letters, compare independent manuscripts with the same earlier lexical form and measure the rate of divine-name omission against the rate for other frequent words. Without these controls, a claim of deliberate late theological standardisation is not demonstrated. Conversely, the evidence of actual written intervention is not eliminated by an explanation of the scribe's motive. This revision consulted published extracts and catalogue records; the underlying manuscript photographs were not independently collated. The Sanaa legal comparison and the Parisino regional comparisons remain stronger tests of substantive transmitted alternatives.

41 / MONUMENTAL AND AMRENSIS WITNESSES

Topkapi H.S. 32: the attribution is not an authentication

The Istanbul Topkapi manuscript H.S. 32 is traditionally attributed to Uthman, who died in 656 CE. Halaseh (2024), pp. 83-84, concludes that the script and orthographic evidence instead favour early Abbasid production; Altıkulaç's introduction to the 2007 edition, cited in Brubaker (2019), favours an Umayyad origin. The proposed dates differ but neither supports an autograph or commissioned exemplar from Uthman's reign. [56] [30]

Halaseh (p. 32) distinguishes missing material and replacements from the original leaves. The 2007 facsimile index records gaps in surahs 5 and 17. These gaps are not proof that the original codex omitted the associated verses. A nearly complete surviving book is valuable evidence for textual continuity but is not a complete chain of custody to Muhammad or Uthman. [56] [57]

Samarkand or Tashkent: substantial fragments, not proof of an original

The Metropolitan Museum of Art's folio 2004.87, assigned to the same dispersed Tashkent manuscript, is dated to the late eighth or early ninth century. The museum estimates that about one third of the original is held in Tashkent. It does not identify a secure place of production. [58]

The secondary survey by Islamic Awareness reports an Oxford radiocarbon determination for a related leaf, with a broad 595-855 CE interval at 95 percent and 640-765 CE at 68 percent. This release has not obtained the lab report, so these are reported figures, not independently checked calibration data. The broad material range is not evidence that Uthman commissioned the writing. The survey also describes paper replacements and retracing associated with the 1905 facsimile, creating a separate question about whether a reading belongs to the early ink or modern reproduction. [59]

There is no authenticated chain of custody from the caliph to this codex established by these sources. Dating favours a later copy, even though it may preserve earlier text. No Samarkand word-level variant has been entered in the catalogue on the basis of these descriptions alone; direct folio collation is still required.

Three manuscript tests and their evidential limits

Text-first verdict: the reported Topkapi insertions establish local variation in writing, including movement between regional forms. Amrensis records a distinct word and a repaired lexical substitution. These are incompatible with a claim of universal letter-for-letter identity across physical copies. They do not establish that the standard consonantal text type was created in the eighth century. [56] [60] [9]

Historical verdict: the traditional identification of monumental manuscripts with Uthman is not demonstrated by the surviving provenance and script evidence. The evidence favours later production of Topkapi and Samarkand; this does not entail a late date for the underlying text type. To strengthen an autograph claim, produce authenticated contemporary provenance and dating consistent with his reign. To establish an alternative original reading, identify independently preserved comparanda and date the relevant reading and correction separately. Merely detecting a correction is insufficient.

48 / CONCLUSION

Standardisation is established; exhaustive original authenticity is not

The transmitted sources report that Uthman imposed an official Quranic textual form, made choices about disputed writing, distributed copies and ordered alternatives destroyed. Manuscripts corroborate a very early written Uthmanic archetype, while the Sanaa lower text demonstrates the existence of another early tradition. [2] [9] [10]

These findings do not establish deliberate corruption or demonstrate that Uthman lacked knowledgeable advisers and verification methods. They do establish that the survival of numerous descendants of the official text is not an independent audit of every excluded alternative.

Verdict: The claim that the Uthmanic standard text is independently proven to preserve every original revealed form fails the evidence test. A much narrower historical claim, that the dominant consonantal text was fixed early and transmitted with substantial stability, is strongly supported. Oral tradition must face the same scrutiny applied to Jewish and Christian evidence.

EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATIONEarly standardisation corroborated; exhaustive originality unverifiedOfficial copying, lost alternative material and documented variant text types limit what can be concluded.

Quran 15:9 promises preservation of the Reminder and 41:41-42 describes revelation as protected from falsehood. Those passages must be distinguished from historical evidence for the completeness of the Uthmanic collection. They attest the religious promise; they do not independently inventory earlier written witnesses, authenticate destroyed codices or resolve which details of sanctioned oral readings were included.
