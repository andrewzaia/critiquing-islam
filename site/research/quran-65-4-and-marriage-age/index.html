Quran 65:4 and the marriage-age question

The written legal category, the classical interpretation and its implications.

01 / CENTRAL TEXTUAL QUESTION

Why does a divorce law address those who have not menstruated?

DOCUMENTED TEXT

Quran 65:4 gives a divorce waiting period for those who have not menstruated. Classical interpreters explicitly include girls who have not yet reached menstruation. [1] [5] [6]

This is a serious matter because it concerns the legal treatment of marriage and divorce. The question is what the Quran regulates, what classical interpreters inferred and whether a claim that the rule only refers to adults is supported by the earlier interpretive record.

02 / QURANIC TEXT

Three waiting-period categories in the verse

Quran 65:4 prescribes a three-month period for women past menstruation and for those who have not menstruated, with a separate rule for pregnancy. Quran 2:228 sets the general menstrual-cycle framework. [1] [3] [7]

The words alone do not give a numerical age or expressly list every possible reason for an absence of menstruation. Delayed or absent menstruation can also occur in adults. But it is inaccurate to claim the classical meaning excludes young girls.

03 / PRIMARY EXEGETICAL WRITINGS

Ibn Kathir and al-Jalalayn include those below menstruating age

Ibn Kathir specifically applies the waiting period to girls too young to have begun menstruating, and al-Jalalayn also explains the category in terms of young age. [5] [6]

These are identifiable written Islamic interpretations, not translations invented by modern critics. Their historical standing does not mean that every Muslim today endorses marriage involving minors; it shows what major classical commentaries understood the legal category to include.

04 / COMBINED LEGAL READING

The no-contact exception makes the implications more significant

Quran 33:49 says that a divorce before marital contact does not require the waiting period. Ibn Kathir explains this as the case of divorce before consummation. [2] [8] [9]

Reading this exception alongside 65:4 explains why the classical inclusion of minors is not merely a discussion of marriage contracts that must end before physical relations. The combined rules give rise to a serious inference regarding marriages that fell outside the pre-consummation exception.

This is a legal inference from two texts and their interpretation, not a narrated account of a specific marriage, and the verses do not order people to enter such marriages.

05 / COUNTERARGUMENTS

Quran 4:6 does not by itself cancel the classical reading

Quran 4:6 discusses marriageable age and sound judgement when returning an orphan's property. That is relevant to maturity but its immediate legal rule concerns property management. The verse does not expressly set a single numerical marriage age, nor does it automatically negate classical readings of 65:4. [4]

An adult with absent menstruation may also fall under the broad wording of 65:4. That possible application does not erase its explicit interpretation as including minors by Ibn Kathir and al-Jalalayn. Quran 4:19's general instruction to treat women well is important, but does not rewrite either clause. [10]

The evidentiary dispute should not rest on ignoring real adult causes of absent menstruation. The worded category is broader than one physiological cause; the classical interpretation, however, expressly included prepubescent girls. Demonstrating an adult application is therefore not a rebuttal of what the classical exegetes actually wrote. [1] [5] [6]

Nor should a modern child-protection principle be presented as a quotation of seventh-century law. The historically established finding is that major classical readings accommodated minors within the divorce waiting-period category. The ethical assessment of that accommodation is a subsequent, separately stated conclusion.

06 / HISTORICAL AND MODERN STANDARDS

Regulation is not a demonstration of ethical adequacy

UNICEF identifies marriage before age 18 as child marriage and documents serious threats to children's autonomy, education, health and welfare. [11]

A historical legal text's accommodation of a practice does not by itself establish that the practice meets present child-protection standards. The central ethical questions concern meaningful consent, welfare, protection from harm and the interests of the child.

Criticism of the legal tradition should not be turned into assumptions about individual Muslims or contemporary communities, many of which reject child marriage.

08 / CONCLUSION

The claim that the verse excludes minors fails the primary-source test

Quran 65:4 and major classical commentaries do not support the assertion that the non-menstruating divorce category refers exclusively to adult women. Ibn Kathir and al-Jalalayn explicitly include young girls, and Quran 33:49 makes the waiting-period distinction legally consequential. [1] [2] [5] [6]

This does not prove that the Quran commands marrying children, identifies a specific age or describes every Muslim's legal position. But it does defeat the claim that the classical tradition categorically excluded minors from the provision.

Verdict: An adults-only explanation of Quran 65:4 fails against the documented classical interpretation; the resulting legal implications raise substantial child-protection concerns.
