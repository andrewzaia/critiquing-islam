01 / CENTRAL TEXTUAL QUESTION

Why does a divorce law address those who have not menstruated?

DOCUMENTED TEXT

Quran 65:4 assigns a waiting period to those who have not menstruated, without specifying a minimum age or an adults-only exception. The wording and related provisions are the primary evidence; later commentaries can only document subsequent readings. [1] [7]

This is a serious matter because it concerns the legal treatment of marriage and divorce. The question is what the Quran regulates, what its related provisions imply and whether a claim that the rule applies exclusively to adults is supported by the Quranic text.

02 / QURANIC TEXT

Three waiting-period categories in the verse

Quran 65:4 prescribes a three-month period for women past menstruation and for those who have not menstruated, with a separate rule for pregnancy. Quran 2:228 sets the general menstrual-cycle framework. [1] [3] [7]

The Arabic does not state a numerical age or list every possible reason for absent menstruation. The category may encompass adults without menstrual periods as well as those who have not yet begun menstruation. The verse contains no express adults-only restriction, but it also does not state that every person in the category is underage. [7]

03 / WORDING AND TRANSLATION

The Arabic does not supply an adult-only exception

The operative clause in Quran 65:4 is wa-allai lam yahidna, commonly translated as those who have not menstruated. The Arabic describes the absence of an event, not an age threshold or a diagnosis. The three-month waiting period is supplied by the preceding coordinated clause. The Quranic Arabic Corpus presents multiple translations, some of which add explanatory words in brackets. Such additions should not be confused with words present in the verse. [1] [7]

It is plausible that more than one physiological circumstance could fit this description. That fact does not make a restrictive adult-only meaning explicit. The opposing claim would require a contextual or linguistic reason why people who have not yet experienced menstruation must be excluded from the ordinary category, rather than simply preferring a different application.

The Quran's references to nisa, women, do not specify a numerical age in this verse. Nor does 65:4 itself declare an age of consent, command an early marriage or provide an account of particular spouses. Those distinct claims cannot be smuggled into either side of the textual argument.

04 / HISTORICAL RECEPTION

Later interpretations are evidence of reception, not the rule itself

Ibn Kathir specifically applies the waiting period to girls too young to have begun menstruating, and al-Jalalayn also explains the category in terms of young age. [5] [6]

The commentaries document that a particular application was later recognised. They do not add an age limit to Quran 65:4, prove a specific historical event or settle whether the divine text is ethically sufficient. Their evidentiary role is descriptive, not authoritative. [1]

05 / COMBINED LEGAL READING

The no-contact exception makes the implications more significant

Quran 33:49 expressly exempts a defined category of marriages from the waiting period, while 65:4 states waiting periods for several other categories. These are Quranic rules requiring comparison without appeal to the authority of a later commentator. [2] [8] [9]

Read together, the two verses raise a serious question about why an explicitly defined waiting-period category has no specified minimum age. This is a legal inference from the verses, not an account of an actual marriage or an instruction to marry at a particular age.

This is a legal inference from two texts and their interpretation, not a narrated account of a specific marriage, and the verses do not order people to enter such marriages.

06 / QURANIC CROSS-REFERENCE

Quran 4:6 does not supply the missing age restriction

Quran 4:6 discusses marriageable age and sound judgement when returning an orphan's property. That is relevant to maturity but its immediate legal rule concerns property management. The verse does not expressly set a single numerical marriage age, nor does it insert an express adults-only limitation into 65:4. [4]

An adult with absent menstruation may be within the wording of 65:4, but that does not show the text excludes younger people. Later readings documented by Ibn Kathir and al-Jalalayn are not the basis for this textual finding. Quran 4:19 addresses respectful treatment, not a numerical age for the waiting period. [1] [5] [6] [10]

The evidentiary question is what the verse itself restricts. Adult medical explanations are relevant possibilities, not an express limitation of the category. Equally, no particular historical marriage, minimum age or universal application follows from the verse by itself. [1] [5] [6]

A modern child-protection principle must be distinguished from the Quran's ancient legal language. The ethical assessment is separate from the narrower textual determination that the age limitation is not stated in 65:4.

07 / TEXTUAL INFERENCE AND ITS LIMITS

What the two divorce provisions establish together

First, Quran 65:4 sets a waiting period for a category described by lack of menstruation. Second, Quran 33:49 exempts a category of marriages from a waiting period. The presence of the first rule alongside the second raises a serious legal question about the scope of marriages contemplated by the text; it does not establish the age or circumstances of any individual marriage. [1] [2]

An attempt to resolve the concern by saying the first clause applies only to adults must identify the adults-only restriction in the Quran or justify it with external linguistic evidence. The clause itself does not contain that qualification. Conversely, a critique claiming that the Quran directly orders marriage involving children would go beyond what this waiting-period verse says.

Quran 4:6 concerns testing orphans and handing over their property after the relevant age and demonstration of judgement. It merits examination as a possible contextual age-related provision, but is not itself an explicit cross-reference defining the scope of 65:4. The responsible conclusion is the narrower textual one: an age threshold needed to exclude younger cases has not been supplied by the cited wording. [4]

08 / HISTORICAL AND MODERN STANDARDS

Regulation is not a demonstration of ethical adequacy

UNICEF identifies marriage before age 18 as child marriage and documents serious threats to children's autonomy, education, health and welfare. [11]

A historical legal text's accommodation of a practice does not by itself establish that the practice meets present child-protection standards. The central ethical questions concern meaningful consent, welfare, protection from harm and the interests of the child.

Criticism of the legal tradition should not be turned into assumptions about individual Muslims or contemporary communities, many of which reject child marriage.

09 / THE EXACT ARABIC: THE RELATIVE CLAUSE DOES NOT STIPULATE ADULTHOOD

The exact Arabic: the relative clause does not stipulate adulthood

Quran 65:4 contains wa-allai lam yahidna, and those who have not menstruated. The annotated Arabic Corpus identifies the relative pronoun allai, the negative particle lam and the jussive third-person feminine plural verb yahidna from the root ḥ-y-ḍ. The phrase describes the absence of menstruation and is not accompanied by a word for an adult, a numerical age or an explicit medical diagnosis. This is the central textual observation. [12]

Its immediate counterpart in the verse is wa-allai ya'isna min al-mahid, those who have despaired of menstruation, ordinarily rendered as women past menstruation. The intervening three-month rule is then extended to those who have not menstruated. A third category deals with pregnancy and ends the waiting period at delivery. The grammar recognises multiple conditions rather than giving one universal waiting period. [1] [12]

It would be incorrect to claim the phrase explicitly names children; it does not contain an age noun. It would be equally incorrect to insist that it explicitly names only mature women. An age-restrictive explanation is an inference requiring another source, whereas the inclusive absence-of-menstruation wording is directly visible in Arabic. The article therefore needs to distinguish literal grammar, medically possible referents and the jurisprudential uses later made of this wording.

10 / IN IRTABTUM: THE DOUBT CLAUSE IS NOT A CHILD-PROTECTION EXCEPTION

In irtabtum: the doubt clause is not a child-protection exception

The clause in irtabtum, commonly rendered if you doubt or if you are uncertain, occurs after the category of women who no longer expect menstruation. Its exact scope was disputed by early interpreters. Al-Tabari records explanations that the uncertainty concerned bleeding or its classification, and concludes that the addressees were uncertain about the appropriate legal waiting period. This is a later interpretive witness, not a missing Quranic sentence. [21]

The clause does not say that a marriage is invalid unless the spouses are adults, that the non-menstruating category consists only of adults with a medical condition, or that a waiting period applies solely above a specified age. Some modern defences use the doubt language as a reason to limit the verse to adult amenorrhea, but that requires showing why the syntax excludes a person who has never menstruated by reason of youth. [12]

Al-Qurtubi's later discussion records separate interpretations about legal uncertainty, menopause and irregular bleeding. He also interprets the non-menstruating category as including the young. His discussion illustrates how different ambiguity questions were distinguished in the legal tradition. This is informative reception evidence; the direct grammatical result remains the absence of an adults-only boundary. [22]

11 / THE COMPARISON WITH QURAN 33:49 IS A CONDITIONAL LEGAL INFERENCE

The comparison with Quran 33:49 is a conditional legal inference

Quran 33:49 says that if believing women are married and then divorced min qabli an tamassuhunna, before the husband has touched them, no waiting period is to be counted. The Arabic Corpus parses tamassuhunna as the second-person plural verb of touching. In the marriage context this is commonly interpreted as a euphemism for consummation, as the parallel translations show. That context matters more than the isolated dictionary gloss touch. [2] [8] [13]

Combined with 65:4, this creates a significant inference. A category of divorced spouses who have not menstruated receives a waiting period, while divorces before marital contact are exempted. Under the ordinary interpretation of the legal relationship, the category for which the waiting period actually applies consequently presupposes that the no-contact exemption is not operative. [1] [2]

The inference is not an eyewitness narrative of a particular person's marriage. Nor does it prove a specific age for every individual included in the wording. But it makes it difficult to dismiss the passage merely as an abstract precaution unrelated to marital contact. The inference becomes substantially more troubling if the non-menstruating class includes persons who are prepubescent, as the historical commentators expressly recognised. That combination must be analysed rather than softened by a translation preference.

12 / QURAN 2:236-237 CONFIRMS DIVORCE CAN PRECEDE CONSUMMATION

Quran 2:236-237 confirms divorce can precede consummation

Quran 2:236 addresses divorce before marital contact where a payment has not been specified and directs a fair provision. Quran 2:237 provides a related rule for marriages dissolved before touching where a marriage-portion was already set, normally half the agreed portion, subject to stated forms of waiver. These are distinct legal scenarios within the Quran's own marriage and divorce framework. [14] [15]

They show an important distinction between the making of a marriage contract and marital consummation. A person can be described as married and divorced even though consummation has not occurred. That prevents the careless conclusion that every Quranic divorce mention is evidence of sexual activity. It also means one cannot answer the 65:4 question merely by saying that a contract may have been concluded before the spouses lived together. The relevant waiting-period exemption must still be explained. [13] [14]

A serious argument would identify which class is being regulated, whether the marriage involved marital contact under the applicable rule, and how the waiting-period category is assigned. No age is supplied by 2:236 or 2:237 either. These verses clarify the legal system without independently deciding the morality, prevalence or historical facts of marriages involving minors.

13 / QURAN 2:228 AND 65:1 ESTABLISH A BROADER WAITING-PERIOD SYSTEM

Quran 2:228 and 65:1 establish a broader waiting-period system

Quran 2:228 generally tells divorced women to wait for three quru', a term classically disputed between menstrual periods and intervals of purity. Quran 65:1 directs the parties to calculate the waiting period and regulates continued residence during it. Quran 65:4 then states how to count a waiting period for cases in which ordinary menstrual cycles do not provide the calculation. The texts form a system of divorce regulation rather than independent declarations of marriage eligibility. [3] [16] [17]

The functional reason for an iddah can include clarification about pregnancy, possibilities of reconciliation in some cases and social ordering of the dissolution process. These purposes are explanatory inferences from the surrounding law; they are not a licence to invent absent age conditions. The existence of a waiting period and the permission to enter a marriage are legally connected but distinct propositions.

One consequence is particularly important. If a married person in a specified class is exempt from the waiting period before marital contact, the presence of that class in the waiting-period legislation raises a question about circumstances after contact. The primary law does not itself add an age floor to resolve the concern. A critic may identify the implication without asserting that the text commands every person in that category to marry or undergo consummation.

14 / QURAN 4:6 INTRODUCES MARRIAGEABLE AGE AND JUDGEMENT, BUT IN PROPERTY LAW

Quran 4:6 introduces marriageable age and judgement, but in property law

Quran 4:6 instructs guardians to test orphans hatta idha balaghu al-nikaha, until they reach the age of marriage, and then, if they perceive rushdan, sound judgement, to deliver the orphans' property. The Arabic Corpus parses al-nikaha as an accusative noun connected with reaching marriageability and rushdan as a separate noun for responsible judgement. These are not words a critical study can ignore when examining Quranic concepts of maturity. [4] [18]

A strong adult-consent interpretation uses 4:6 to argue that marriage presupposes maturity and demonstrated judgement. This reading has a meaningful linguistic basis; it is not necessarily an apologetic invention. The challenge is the grammatical and legal target of the verse. The specific command under discussion is to test orphans and hand over their assets once criteria are met. It does not expressly say that any marriage contract concluded below that threshold is void or that the separate divorce category in 65:4 has an adult-only exception. [18]

A legal system can restrict an individual's independent power to manage money while allowing a guardian to act on that person's behalf in certain other transactions. That feature is explicitly documented in later jurisprudence, including discussions surveyed by Hallaq. Whether one regards those later outcomes as ethically acceptable is a separate issue. The key point is that 4:6's property-release test is not, by itself, a demonstrable repeal of the 65:4 wording. [25]

15 / QURAN 24:58-59 EXPLICITLY RECOGNISES A DISTINCTION AT PUBERTY

Quran 24:58-59 explicitly recognises a distinction at puberty

Quran 24:58 regulates when children who have not reached puberty must ask permission before entering private rooms, identifying particular times. Quran 24:59 changes the household privacy rule after children balagha al-atfal minkum al-huluma, reach puberty or maturity. This supplies positive evidence that the Quran can explicitly mark the boundary between prepubescent children and those who have reached a later stage when a context calls for the distinction. [19] [20]

Such a distinction is significant because 65:4 does not use the comparable language of having reached puberty or marriageable age when describing those who have not menstruated. An apologist may argue that the term women supplies the distinction implicitly; the immediate comparison shows that the Quran has other available ways of spelling out developmental categories. An ordinary label for female persons is not as exact as a clause identifying puberty or its absence. [12]

Neither 24:58 nor 24:59 is a marriage statute. They concern household access and privacy. They therefore cannot be transformed into an express permission or prohibition of marriage contracts merely because the same concepts of developmental stage occur. Their evidentiary role is narrower but important: puberty is not an unknown conceptual category that the Quran could not have mentioned had it intended that limitation.

16 / ADULT AMENORRHEA IS A REAL ALTERNATIVE, NOT AN EXHAUSTIVE EXPLANATION

Adult amenorrhea is a real alternative, not an exhaustive explanation

Some women and girls may not menstruate for reasons other than being too young to have begun menstruation. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists distinguishes primary amenorrhea, where menstruation has not begun by an expected age, from secondary amenorrhea, where previously established menstruation stops. It describes multiple medical causes, including developmental and endocrine conditions, and recommends evaluation when periods have not begun by age 15. [27]

This modern medical information shows why it is too strong to say that the words those who have not menstruated must logically describe every person in the category as a child. A mature person with primary amenorrhea can satisfy the absence-of-menstruation description. That possibility is a genuine objection to an overconfident exclusively prepubescent translation and should be stated openly.

It does not establish the reverse claim that prepubescent individuals are excluded. A broad description can include multiple causes of absence. The clinical diagnosis is also a modern classification, not a term explicitly supplied by Quran 65:4. A source-first investigation should not use ACOG's medical account to retrofit a compulsory adult-only qualification into an ancient legal clause. The more modest finding is that adult and younger cases are possible referents, while only additional legal constraints could restrict the application.

17 / AL-TABARI GIVES EXPLICIT EVIDENCE OF A YOUNGER-PERSON READING

Al-Tabari gives explicit evidence of a younger-person reading

Al-Tabari's commentary on Quran 65:4 transmits reports about the circumstance of the verse, including an attributed question mentioning the young, the old and pregnant women. He also presents discussion of the doubt clause and distinguishes uncertainty about the legal rule from uncertainty about irregular bleeding. Crucially, his own exposition interprets those who have not menstruated as younger girls who had not yet menstruated because of youth in the context of divorce after marital contact. [21]

This is a direct, textually inspectable historical interpretation, not a modern critic projecting an age category onto the verse. Yet the commentary was composed centuries after the attributed revelation and includes traditions with their own transmission histories. It does not independently verify that a particular young girl's marriage was consummated in seventh-century Arabia. It demonstrates that such a legal reading was part of an early influential Sunni exegetical tradition. [21]

Al-Tabari's argument also matters because it does not erase all alternative circumstances: his discussion includes women with uncertain or irregular bleeding and different opinions concerning the doubt clause. The accurate critical claim is not that nobody ever offered any other reading. It is that prepubescence was expressly recognised within the category, and the verse supplies no age limit clearly ruling it out.

18 / AL-QURTUBI DOCUMENTS DETAILED JURISTIC APPLICATION AND UNCERTAINTY

Al-Qurtubi documents detailed juristic application and uncertainty

Al-Qurtubi's commentary on Quran 65:4 sets out several legal questions rather than a single modern English gloss. It discusses the waiting period for those whose periods have ceased, for people with irregular bleeding and for those who have not menstruated. His discussion explicitly includes al-saghira, the young girl, in the category for whom the waiting period is reckoned in months. He also records debates over how to count when bleeding returns. [22]

The commentary is later than al-Tabari and must be treated as an additional reception witness, not an independent eyewitness to the original revelation. Its interest lies in the developed legal problems it presupposes: judges and jurists had to decide how to count waiting periods, what counted as menstruation and how marital situations affected those obligations. The source does not create the primary grammar, but it demonstrates that the adult-only reading was not the unanimous historical application of the verse. [21] [22]

Both commentators should be handled with consistent source criticism. When they support a difficult critique, their statements show what they argued and transmitted; when they offer qualifications, those qualifications deserve the same attention. Neither commentator has the power to place an age limit in the Quran or remove one that is explicitly there. The verdict ultimately rests on the primary texts, informed by their attested interpretive history.

19 / GUARDIANSHIP AND CONTRACTS: LATER LAW GOES BEYOND A WAITING-PERIOD VERSE

Guardianship and contracts: later law goes beyond a waiting-period verse

Kecia Ali's scholarly survey of classical marriage law explains why a marriage contract, a person's legal competence to contract and a spouse's capacity to exercise marital rights must be distinguished. Early jurists analysed marriage as a legal contract, with differences between schools in the role of the guardian, the conditions for validity and whose consent mattered. Those developed rules were not copied verbatim from a single Quranic waiting-period verse. [24]

Ali's earlier study of consent and forced marriage states that all four classical Sunni schools recognised a father's power to contract marriages for minor children, while there were disagreements about other guardians and the legal choices available upon maturity. Wael Hallaq separately explains that legal capacity in contracts depends on age and sound judgement, and that guardians could act for persons unable to contract independently. These later doctrines show how jurists combined the Quran with other legal concepts and sources. [23] [25]

Do not use those summaries to assert that every Muslim family entered such contracts, or that all schools endorsed identical rules governing residence and consummation. The evidence supports a historical claim about what influential jurists permitted or argued, not a census of practice or an account of a particular marriage. It also matters for legal inference: acknowledging that some contracts could exist before maturity prevents an oversimplified equation of contract date, cohabitation, sexual activity and informed consent.

20 / HISTORICAL PRACTICE NEEDS COURT RECORDS, DATES AND LOCAL CONTEXT

Historical practice needs court records, dates and local context

Judith Tucker's study of marriage in Nablus between 1720 and 1856 uses surviving Islamic court records to document variation across urban communities in child marriage, dowry, divorce and remarriage. These records are historically distinct from a Quranic verse, and their value is that they describe identifiable cases rather than only ideals asserted by jurists. Tucker also shows that differences in social setting affected how particular marriage arrangements functioned. [26]

Such evidence should not be projected indiscriminately across fourteen centuries. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Palestinian court registers cannot establish the exact prevalence of early marriage in seventh-century Arabia, nor can a general legal permission establish that every family used it. The date of an alleged Quranic event, the composition date of a tafsir, the recording of a court case and a modern scholar's publication are separate chronological anchors.

One can responsibly conclude that marriages involving minors formed part of at least some documented Islamic legal and historical settings. The stronger quantitative claims, such as a precise rate in all Muslim societies or the age of a specific disputed historical figure, require independent sources that this particular waiting-period verse cannot supply. That distinction preserves criticism of permissive legal structures while avoiding exaggerated allegations unsupported by the documents.

21 / PROTECTIVE QURANIC LANGUAGE DOES NOT WRITE A MINIMUM AGE INTO 65:4

Protective Quranic language does not write a minimum age into 65:4

Quran 4:19 prohibits a particular coercive inheritance practice involving women and commands fair treatment. Quran 4:127 addresses questions concerning women and orphaned girls and the obligation to maintain justice. These are meaningful ethical and legal countertexts. They show that the Quran contains language concerning women's treatment and the welfare of vulnerable persons rather than only rules about divorce. [10] [28]

A proposed harmonisation can combine those concerns with Quran 4:6's maturity-and-judgement language to argue that an ethically sound marriage should involve capable consent and genuine welfare. This is a serious contemporary protective approach. Yet the positive value of those principles must not be confused with the narrower textual claim that 65:4 itself excludes a premenstruating minor through a specified age boundary. [4] [18]

Nor does the historical existence of a guardian's contractual power demonstrate that a minor possessed meaningful independent consent. Ali's analysis distinguishes how later schools treated guardianship and consent and identifies important differences for adult women as well as minors. The ethical evaluation must separate formal authority to arrange a contract from the protected person's informed, voluntary agreement, which may be absent where a guardian acts without the child's choice. [23]

The resulting issue is not whether every Quranic protection is fictional; they are not. It is whether a universal moral and legal framework provides an explicit, enforceable safeguard against marriages involving children in the particular waiting-period provision. No such numerical safeguard appears in 65:4, and related provisions leave significant interpretive work to later communities and jurists.

22 / BIBLICAL THEOLOGICAL COMPARISON AND PRESENT CHILD PROTECTION

Biblical theological comparison and present child protection

The Biblical-Christian framework gives independent normative reasons to protect children and reject exploitative treatment. Mark 10:13-16 portrays Jesus receiving and valuing children, while Matthew 18:6 warns against leading vulnerable believers into serious harm. These are theological and moral teachings, not direct ancient statutes establishing a modern minimum marriage age. Claiming they specify eighteen years or expressly amend Quran 65:4 would be as unsound as inventing an adults-only phrase in the Quran. [29] [30]

Modern child-protection policy provides a more specific evaluative standard. UNICEF defines child marriage as a formal marriage or informal union involving someone under 18 and documents risks to education, autonomy, health and well-being. The threshold is a contemporary rights-based standard, not a number found in the Quran or the cited Gospel passages. Keeping those categories separate strengthens rather than weakens the ethical argument. [11]

Medieval and early modern Christian societies also had marriage customs that fail present-day child-protection standards. A Biblical comparison should therefore scrutinise both what foundational texts teach and what institutions actually practised. Historical Christian failings cannot erase weaknesses in another legal framework, and alleged Islamic deficiencies do not establish that Christian history was free of them.

Ethical verdict: whatever an ancient verse contemplated, modern legal protections should prioritise children's welfare, genuine consent and freedom from coercion. The current investigation critiques textual and legal claims; it provides no support for attributing harmful practices to Muslims generally.

23 / THE STRONGEST ALTERNATIVES, FALSIFICATION AND FOUR DISTINCT VERDICTS

The strongest alternatives, falsification and four distinct verdicts

Three readings deserve a direct comparison. Reading A: 65:4 refers exclusively to prepubescent girls. This matches a documented later interpretation but overstates the grammar because adult primary amenorrhea is a genuine possibility. Reading B: 65:4 refers exclusively to adults whose periods are absent for medical reasons. This can explain some cases but does not establish an age exclusion within the phrase, and it sits uneasily with the explicit younger-person readings in al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi. Reading C: the category is broad and was applied to both younger persons and some medically atypical adults. This best respects the open wording while acknowledging that the exact applications and legal consequences have to be argued separately. [12] [21] [22] [27]

Compare the provisions precisely. Quran 33:49 removes the waiting period for marriages ended before touching. Quran 65:4 attaches a period to non-menstruation, while Quran 4:6 addresses maturity and judgement in a property context. From these statements it is reasonable to infer that the law contemplated divorce cases involving non-menstruating spouses for whom the pre-contact exception was not operative. That inference does not become a numerical age decree or independent evidence that a specific marriage happened. [2] [4] [12]

Falsification criteria: an explicit premodern Quranic textual witness or compelling Arabic-contextual evidence requiring that the 65:4 class exclude every prepubescent person would change the adult-only assessment. A specific Quranic rule invalidating all marriages before maturity, shown to govern the divorce provisions, would materially change the legal inference. Conversely, evidence of particular historical marriages would have to establish its own provenance and dating rather than rely solely on the verse.

Text-first verdict: the Arabic supplies a waiting-period category without an adult-only qualification. Legal inference: read with 33:49, the text can encompass cases in which a marriage involving a person who has not menstruated was consummated. Historical verdict: influential commentaries and juristic traditions included younger persons, while the actual prevalence and individual cases cannot be derived from the verse alone. Biblical and ethical verdict: the project's Christian moral premises and independently documented child-protection evidence support protective limits that the specified Quranic clauses do not explicitly provide.

25 / EVIDENCE VERDICT

An adult-only limitation is not written into Quran 65:4

Quran 65:4 assigns a waiting period to those who have not menstruated without stating a minimum age or an adults-only limitation. The Arabic and translation evidence support that textual observation. Quran 33:49 supplies a related exemption from waiting periods in a different category; Quran 4:6 raises a distinct question about legal maturity and property. None inserts an explicit numerical age into 65:4. [1] [2] [4] [7]

Later commentators' interpretations document a historical reading, but do not decide the meaning by authority. Neither a blanket adults-only reading nor a claim that the verse commands marriage at any particular age follows without additional argument. The lack of an expressed protective age limit is the substantive textual concern, which can then be evaluated separately under contemporary child-protection standards. [5] [6] [11]

Text-first verdict: Quran 65:4 contains no stated minimum age or adults-only exception. Legal inference: together with the pre-contact exemption in 33:49, it can encompass divorce cases after marital contact involving a person who has not menstruated. Historical verdict: al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi and later legal traditions document interpretations including younger people without proving the circumstances of any individual marriage. Ethical verdict: law without a specified age safeguard warrants assessment against evidence about consent and child welfare. [12] [13] [21] [22] [11]

Verdict: An adult-only restriction for the non-menstruating divorce category is not present in the text of Quran 65:4. Its application and ethical implications warrant serious scrutiny. Neither the authority of later commentary nor a modern interpretive preference supplies the missing words.
