Prior literary circulation is demonstrable; copying and independently witnessed resurrection are separate propositions.

01 / THE PRECISE HISTORICAL AND SUPERNATURAL PROPOSITION

The precise historical and supernatural proposition

Quran 2:259 presents a traveller passing a ruined settlement, questioning how God will restore it, and undergoing a divine demonstration: God causes the person to die for a hundred years and then raises him. His food remains unspoiled, his donkey appears in the demonstration, and bones are assembled and covered with flesh. The question is whether this is independently authenticated history, an inherited narrative arranged around resurrection theology, or both. [1] [2]

The narrative is particularly suited to historical comparison because its distinctive elements are not limited to a universal idea such as life after death. An inexplicably extended passage of time, the protagonist's incorrect estimate of that interval, a ruined city and magically preserved provisions form a recognisable literary cluster. It is this cluster, rather than a superficial resemblance to any resurrection miracle, that the evidence must explain. [1] [11] [15]

The central burden rests with the claim that the particular event actually occurred and is known through revelation. Demonstrating that related stories circulated earlier supports an ordinary transmission explanation. It does not prove direct plagiarism; equally, the Quran's presentation cannot validate its historical claim merely by asserting divine agency. The study therefore separates text-first, historical and Biblical theological verdicts. [13] [15] [28]

02 / ARABIC WORDING: GOD CAUSED THE TRAVELLER TO DIE

Arabic wording: God caused the traveller to die

The Quranic clause fa-amatahu Allahu mi'ata 'amin thumma ba'athahu explicitly makes God the agent of the person's death and subsequent raising. Here amatahu is not simply a report that the man took a long nap. The hundred-year interval is expressed directly, with 'am for year. A translation that changes the event to sleep in order to harmonise it with an earlier story is not reporting the primary text faithfully. [1] [3]

The demonstrative purpose arises from the traveller's question about revival of a ruined town. His incorrect answer, a day or part of a day, marks the gap between perceived and actual elapsed time. The unspoiled food and the assembly of bones reveal the discrepancy and serve as physical proofs within the narrative, rather than a mere meditation on a spiritual idea. [1] [2]

One must distinguish the text's confident assertion from historical validation. There is no named informant, datable contemporary record or geographical identification in this passage. The Arabic establishes what the Quran claims. Historical sources must be examined independently to establish whether anyone witnessed the described event before the Quran's telling. [1] [15]

03 / THE PROTAGONIST AND CITY ARE BOTH UNNAMED

The protagonist and city are both unnamed

The verse begins with an expression meaning one who passed by a settlement. It supplies neither the man's personal name nor the city's proper name. The conventional identification of the location as Jerusalem and the person as Ezra or Jeremiah comes through later interpretation and comparison, not an explicit clause in Quran 2:259. The difference is important because importing such details creates an apparent textual match that the Arabic itself has not made. [1] [2] [15]

Quran 9:30 contains the name Uzayr in a separate allegation about Jewish religious claims. It does not say that Uzayr is the unnamed man of 2:259. The two passages can be interpreted together, but this remains an exegetical move requiring a separate argument. An attribution to Ezra should therefore never appear as though it were present in the wording of 2:259 itself. [10]

The city likewise remains unspecified. Ruined Jerusalem is a plausible comparison because the earlier Abimelech story is set around its destruction. But the Quran does not expressly mention Babylon, Nebuchadnezzar, Jeremiah or the rebuilding of the Temple in the verse. A claimed exact correspondence should be reduced to its actual shared components. [1] [11] [29]

04 / PRESERVED FOOD AND DRINK ARE MORE DISTINCTIVE THAN LONG SLEEP ALONE

Preserved food and drink are more distinctive than long sleep alone

The command to look at food and drink that have not suffered time's decay is among the strongest points of comparison with earlier narrative material. Food's preservation is set against the extraordinary lapse of many decades. The food is a material indication that the elapsed time was not experienced normally; it does not merely illustrate divine blessing in general. [1] [2]

In 4 Baruch the object is specifically a basket of figs gathered by Abimelech before Jerusalem's catastrophe. When he awakens they are fresh and milky despite decades having passed. The Quran names food and drink rather than figs. Consequently, the correspondence lies in preserved provisions and delayed recognition, not in an identical list of objects. [11] [12] [29]

A distinct provenance question follows. Do the common features reflect a single narrative retold in different forms, or independently produced variants of a widespread long-sleep motif? The detailed combination of devastated city, time discrepancy and unspoiled food favours a literary relationship more than mere thematic coincidence. It does not identify an exact document used by the Quran's author. [11] [15] [21]

05 / THE DONKEY IS NAMED BUT THE BONES REQUIRE CAREFUL GRAMMAR

The donkey is named but the bones require careful grammar

The phrase wa-unzur ila himarika directly commands the man to look at his donkey. The Quranic Arabic Corpus parses himarika as a masculine noun with a second-person possessive suffix. A donkey is therefore not a later commentator's invention. It belongs to the verse's original narrative description. [1] [4]

The next instruction concerns al-'izam, the bones, being raised and clothed with flesh. Most translations reasonably understand these as the donkey's bones because the animal has just been mentioned. But the original wording does not reinsert the noun donkey immediately before bones. Critical writing should identify this as a contextual inference rather than claim the verse expressly states the owner of every bone. [2] [4]

The distinction becomes important when comparing the Honi story. In Ta'anit 23a Honi's donkey survives long enough to sire successive generations, not to be reconstructed from a skeleton. A critic who equates those different animal episodes has abandoned the same exact-wording standard applied to Islamic apologetics. [18] [1]

06 / QIRA'AT ATTEST A MEANINGFUL VERBAL DISTINCTION, NOT A DIFFERENT STORY

Qira'at attest a meaningful verbal distinction, not a different story

The Quran.com reading comparison records two forms in the clause about bones: nunshizuhā, describing the assembling or raising of bones, and nunshiruhā, describing their revival. These are related but not identical words. They deserve acknowledgment because the precise verbal process is part of the argument about a physical demonstration. [5]

Neither reading supplies a historical witness, identifies Ezra, changes the interval from one hundred years or removes the donkey. They therefore refine the reported action rather than create an independent story. A claim that this variation proves the entire narrative was transmitted without any differences would be wrong, as would a claim that it radically changes the whole scene. [5] [1]

This article records the comparison without changing the project's Quran reading corpus, API or implementation. The appropriate historical question remains external attestation of the event, not the assumption that a variant reading is either automatically insignificant or automatically fatal to every preservation argument. [5] [2]

07 / QURAN 2:258-260 MAKES THE RESURRECTION PURPOSE EXPLICIT

Quran 2:258-260 makes the resurrection purpose explicit

The preceding Quran 2:258 recounts Abraham disputing with a ruler about God's capacity to give life and cause death. The following 2:260 has Abraham ask to be shown how God gives life to the dead, followed by a demonstration involving birds. Quran 2:259 therefore sits in a deliberately arranged sequence concerning divine power over death. [6] [7]

That context helps establish why the narrative is included. The unnamed person is not presented merely for antiquarian biography. The event is made to illustrate divine ability to restore what appears irrecoverable. The arrangement also warns against mistaking a theological lesson for an independently preserved eyewitness transcript. A passage may plainly make a moral point while still asserting that a miracle happened. [1] [6] [7]

The narrative arrangement does not give permission to convert the account into purely allegorical sleep. It says God caused actual death, then resurrection, and shows bones being covered with flesh. The literary context explains the presentation's purpose; it does not remove its explicit historical or supernatural proposition. [1] [2]

08 / JEREMIAH'S NARRATIVE SUPPLIES THE OLDER RUINED-CITY SETTING

Jeremiah's narrative supplies the older ruined-city setting

Jeremiah 25:11-12 and 29:10 describe a period associated with Babylonian domination and the hoped-for return of the exiles. These are the canonical Biblical foundations for later narrative settings in which Jerusalem is ruined and faithful individuals survive its catastrophe. They do not introduce the Quranic unnamed traveller or describe a century-long resurrection. [24] [25]

Jeremiah 38:7-13 narrates an Ethiopian court official, Ebed-Melech, intervening to rescue the imprisoned Jeremiah from a cistern. The same official is addressed in Jeremiah 39:15-18 with a promise that he will survive the city's coming disaster. This is the specific Biblical character around whom 4 Baruch develops a much more detailed protective miracle. [22] [23]

The key historical distinction is between a person mentioned in a Biblical document and the later plot attached to him. The existence of Ebed-Melech within Jeremiah cannot be used as a second Biblical attestation that he slept for decades with preserved figs. The original passages make no such claim. [22] [23] [29]

09 / 4 BARUCH TURNS THAT PROMISE INTO A 66-YEAR SLEEP

4 Baruch turns that promise into a 66-year sleep

In 4 Baruch, or the Paraleipomena Jeremiou, Jeremiah sends Abimelech out to collect figs shortly before Jerusalem's catastrophe. Abimelech falls asleep in the shade of a tree and wakes only after sixty-six years, unaware that the exile has taken place. The episode is a protective literary elaboration of the Biblical figure associated with Jeremiah, not an event described in Jeremiah's canonical chapters. [11] [12] [29]

Two details align unusually closely with Quran 2:259: the traveller believes only a short time has passed, and perishable provisions remain intact despite decades having elapsed. The exact interval is different, and 4 Baruch has Abimelech asleep rather than dead. Historical criticism must hold both observations together, without minimising the parallels or rewriting the differences. [1] [11] [13]

The old man's response in 4 Baruch uses the season of the figs and the changed state of the city to correct Abimelech's understanding. The Quran instead presents a direct divine explanation and an additional demonstration with bones. Such narrative reorganisation is compatible with transmission and adaptation, but requires a specified evidence chain before a historian can attribute it to one identifiable translator or storyteller. [11] [15]

10 / THE GREEK WORDING CONFIRMS THE ORIGINAL SLEEP DISTINCTION

The Greek wording confirms the original sleep distinction

The Greek 4 Baruch 5:2 uses hypnosen and the language of sleep over sixty-six years. This is not an assertion that God killed Abimelech and then returned him to life. In contrast, the Quran explicitly uses the verb for causing death. That lexical difference is an objective textual distinction and should not be smoothed away for the sake of a stronger borrowing allegation. [12] [1]

Chapter 5 describes the figs as still fresh, while 6:8 links their preservation to God's capacity to preserve human life. The Greek witness makes the shared pattern of astonishing preservation and theological inference visible without relying on a modern Muslim commentator to tell us what the earlier narrative supposedly meant. [11] [12]

The surviving Greek text has its own history of copying and editorial reconstruction. The presence of ancient narrative motifs does not make a medieval Greek manuscript a contemporary witness to the Babylonian exile. A critical edition supports reconstruction of a literary work; it does not authenticate that the sleeping incident occurred. [13] [14]

11 / FRESH FIGS BECOME A SIGN OF DIVINE PRESERVATION IN 4 BARUCH

Fresh figs become a sign of divine preservation in 4 Baruch

In 4 Baruch 6:8-10 Baruch explicitly reflects on figs that remained fresh for the extraordinary interval and links the phenomenon to God's preservation of a person. The preserved food therefore plays a theological and evidential role within its own story, not simply a decorative role in the narrative. That makes the comparison with Quran 2:259 stronger than a shared coincidence of fruit. [11]

The parallel is especially close at the level of argumentative structure: elapsed decades are established, ordinary decay is suspended, and the physical anomaly becomes evidence for God's power. The Quran's version substitutes a full century and connects the demonstration with the reassembly of bones. The two narratives have common components but different final staging. [1] [11] [15]

Similar theology cannot independently confirm the alleged preservation of figs, food or a dead person's body. Both authors affirm their own miraculous settings; neither text is an eyewitness report made at the time of the described ancient event. To call them independent experimental confirmations would misunderstand what literary texts can establish. [13] [14]

12 / 4 BARUCH'S COMPOSITION IS EARLIER THAN ISLAM, NOT ITS EXTANT COPIES

4 Baruch's composition is earlier than Islam, not its extant copies

Jens Herzer's critical study argues that the core of 4 Baruch emerged in Jewish circles in the late first or early second century, with subsequent Christian development. Scholars disagree on precise dating and layers, and a Christian ending cannot simply be projected back onto every Jewish version. The composition is earlier than Islam on the usual critical dating. [13]

The manuscript history is materially different. Kraft and Purintun list many Greek witnesses of the long form, including tenth- and eleventh-century copies, with later manuscript families and translations. Those copies are not second-century autographs. Confusing a reconstructed composition date with the date of a physically surviving codex would seriously distort the evidential claim. [14]

Consequently, there are two warranted statements: the work is generally assigned a pre-Islamic origin, and our cited full textual witnesses survive through much later copying. Neither warrants asserting that the original author's hand has been preserved. The strength of the historical case depends on critical reconstruction and the wider transmission record, not on a fabricated early manuscript. [13] [14]

13 / THE 66- AND 70-YEAR VERSIONS MUST NOT BE COLLAPSED

The 66- and 70-year versions must not be collapsed

The long Greek form of 4 Baruch recounts sixty-six years. An alternative shorter textual form and related versions preserve different numbers, including a seventy-year reading. Herzer discusses variants, and Kraft and Purintun document recensional differences. The disagreement is not a reason to silently replace the Greek number with the Quran's hundred years. [11] [13] [14]

Robert Hoyland notes that a Coptic-related Jeremiah tradition gives a seventy-year interval, illustrating the textual and literary reshaping of the story. The changed number cannot be used to invent an otherwise unknown Arabic manuscript reading precisely one hundred. No such witness has been identified here. The point remains a common long-period miracle, not identity of numerical claims. [15] [16]

The relevant chronology is sometimes confused: the Babylonian exile conventionally raises a seventy-year theme, Abimelech's Greek story says sixty-six, Honi sleeps seventy, and the Quran says one hundred. A single table can preserve these distinct figures, while a phrase such as 'they all slept a hundred years' would be demonstrably inaccurate. [1] [11] [17] [24]

14 / HONI IN THE BABYLONIAN TALMUD SLEEPS SEVENTY YEARS

Honi in the Babylonian Talmud sleeps seventy years

Babylonian Talmud Ta'anit 23a:15-16 tells how Honi the Circle-Drawer asks about a carob tree that will take seventy years to bear fruit. He then eats and falls asleep, concealed in a rock formation, awakening seventy years later. He recognises the interval through the carob tree and the identity of the later fruit gatherer. [17] [18]

The same account mentions Honi's donkey, whose descendants have multiplied in the intervening period. This is an important connection because Quran 2:259 also names a donkey. The events, however, are not identical. Honi's animal reproduces normally while he sleeps, whereas the Quran directs attention to bones being reassembled after the man's resurrection. [18] [4]

Honi does not discover preserved food still fresh after seventy years, and his story is not described as his death and resurrection. The strongest cumulative literary comparison is therefore 4 Baruch's food-and-ruined-city episode alongside Honi's long interval and donkey, not an invented single ancient account containing every detail of the Quranic verse. [17] [18] [11]

15 / PALESTINIAN AND BABYLONIAN HONI TRADITIONS ARE NOT IDENTICAL

Palestinian and Babylonian Honi traditions are not identical

The Honi long-sleep story also appears in a Palestinian rabbinic setting, discussed in connection with Jerusalem Talmud Ta'anit 3:9. The text should be treated as a distinct literary witness with its own transmission history, not automatically collapsed into the Bavli paragraph about a donkey. Differences across versions can illuminate narrative development. [20] [21]

Moshe Simon-Shoshan's peer-reviewed study connects the Palestinian Honi narrative to an older network of Jewish literature involving Abimelech, the destruction of Jerusalem and restoration after exile. This is a scholarly argument about dependence and shared memory. It strengthens the case that these motifs circulated within Jewish literary culture before the Quran. [21]

The individual accounts do not establish how or when a particular Arab audience encountered them. A proposed chain from Palestinian material through Christian storytelling into the Quran remains an inference unless supported by specific linguistic, manuscript or historical intermediaries. The presence of a network matters; it should not be mistaken for an exact line of transmission. [15] [20] [21]

16 / THE TALMUD'S ENDING CHANGES ITS NARRATIVE PURPOSE

The Talmud's ending changes its narrative purpose

After waking, Honi finds that his home and study community no longer recognise him. Ta'anit 23a:17 concludes with his isolation and death, turning the supernatural lapse into a reflection on the importance of human relationships. It is not simply a proof that God can rebuild a donkey's skeleton. [19]

The Quran places the focus on divine power to raise the dead and reassemble bones. 4 Baruch uses the preservation of figs to represent survival through catastrophe. Honi reflects on continuity across generations and the cost of losing one's social world. Similar literary machinery can serve genuinely different theological purposes in different communities. [1] [11] [19]

This difference makes a crudely mechanical equation between texts less credible. The sustainable finding is that motifs of extraordinary sleep, dislocated time, surviving food and associated animals were available for adaptation. The independent historicity of each narrated miracle is not established by the others. [15] [21]

17 / THE CITY'S DESTRUCTION IS A COMPELLING LINK BUT NOT AN EXPLICIT IDENTIFICATION

The city's destruction is a compelling link but not an explicit identification

In 4 Baruch the setting is Jerusalem around its fall and the Babylonian exile. The Quran uses a generic ruined settlement without naming Jerusalem. That distinction matters because the literary comparison gains strength from the other shared elements, while a verbatim place-name match cannot honestly be claimed. [1] [11]

Jeremiah's seventy-year framework helps explain why later Jewish and Christian storytellers might present an individual as carried across a period of national desolation. The return of life to a destroyed place can become a metaphor for the restoration of Israel. However, the Quran's hundred years do not map directly onto Jeremiah's seventy-year figure. [24] [25]

The most economical historical interpretation is that such narratives travelled through a milieu familiar with scriptural exile and restoration themes. This is a plausible explanation supported by narrative recurrence, not an archaeological discovery of the traveller or a dated record of his donkey's burial. [13] [21]

18 / EZEKIEL'S BONES ARE A SEPARATE BIBLICAL IMAGE

Ezekiel's bones are a separate Biblical image

Ezekiel 37:1-14 depicts a valley of dry bones receiving sinews, flesh and breath, followed by a divine explanation linking the vision to the restoration of Israel. The reassembly and clothing of bones in Quran 2:259 therefore also resembles a prior Biblical prophetic image. But Ezekiel does not narrate resurrection of a donkey belonging to an unnamed traveller. [26] [1]

The shared bone-reassembly motif could reflect direct or indirect familiarity with Ezekiel's vision, or a broader repertoire of resurrection imagery. A direct borrowing claim would need an identifiable sequence of unusual vocabulary and narrative elements. Generic anatomical restoration language alone cannot prove one text copied another. [26] [15]

Conversely, a defence that describes the Quran's bone scene as having no precedent in pre-Islamic scriptural imagery is inaccurate. Ezekiel supplies a clear earlier example of bones being joined and covered as an enactment of divine restoration. The specific question is how that prior image was redeployed, not whether the fundamental imagery was unprecedented. [26]

19 / THE SEVEN SLEEPERS FORM A RELATED BUT SEPARATE COMPARISON

The Seven Sleepers form a related but separate comparison

Quran 18:19 has the Companions of the Cave estimating that they remained for a day or part of a day after an extended interval. This echoes the traveller's short estimate in Quran 2:259. Quran 18:25 separately speaks of three hundred years plus nine. These are not alternative dates for the same man or the same event. [8] [9]

The Seven Sleepers account is already treated in CI-039. It concerns young persecuted believers and a cave, not a single man inspecting his food beside a ruined settlement. A family resemblance between long-sleep tales is historically useful; combining the two Quranic narratives into one artificially expands the proof. [29] [30]

Within the Quran itself, the repetition shows that distorted perception of time is a recurring narrative method. Historical critics can assess whether a circulating literary motif shaped multiple retellings without claiming the same protagonist appears wherever the words 'a day or part of a day' occur. [1] [8] [15]

20 / WHAT ROBERT HOYLAND'S COMPARISON ACTUALLY ESTABLISHES

What Robert Hoyland's comparison actually establishes

Robert Hoyland's 2017 study, The Language of the Qur'an and a Near Eastern Rip van Winkle, treats the Abimelech narrative as a strong explanation of Quran 2:259. The argument rests on the fresh food after a long interval, the ruined-city setting and the role of the episode in demonstrating divine preservation. This is a scholarly comparative argument, not direct archaeological verification. [15]

Hoyland also considers possible Christian and Arabic-speaking transmission settings for the narrative. The suggestion has historical plausibility because Jewish and Christian materials circulated across the Near East, but the precise person, medium and language that conveyed this particular version to a Quranic audience have not been demonstrated. Citing Hoyland as proof of a named transmitter would overstate his evidence. [15] [16]

The study also notes the relation to the Honi tradition and textual variation in Jeremiah stories. That makes a broad intertextual account stronger than the claim that one entire paragraph was copied word for word. This research therefore adopts the documented priority of a narrative complex while leaving the intermediary a specified open question. [15] [16] [21]

21 / A CHRONOLOGY OF COMPOSITION, COPIES AND ALLEGED EVENTS

A chronology of composition, copies and alleged events

The alleged Biblical destruction of Jerusalem and the narrated adventures of Abimelech belong to an ancient setting. The literary composition of 4 Baruch is normally assigned to the early centuries AD, and its full surviving Greek manuscript tradition is later. Rabbinic Honi materials were transmitted through Palestinian and Babylonian compilations and their copying histories. The Quran then presents its distinct version during the emergence of Islam. [13] [14] [20] [21]

A second-century proposed composition date is not the same evidential category as a tenth-century surviving copy. Similarly, a Talmudic source testifies to what late-antique rabbinic editors transmitted, not directly to a first-century BCE holy man's miraculous sleep. These distinctions are not excuses for downplaying parallels; they are the grounds for evaluating their true evidential weight. [13] [14] [18]

Separate analysis also prevents false independence. Honi and Abimelech may share an older storytelling network, so two variant tales cannot simply be counted as two unrelated eyewitnesses supporting a literal miracle. Their value is evidence of the history and variation of the story, not additional experimental proof of divine intervention. [21]

23 / THE SERIOUS ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS AND THEIR BURDENS

The serious alternative explanations and their burdens

One explanation is ordinary literary transmission and creative retelling in the mixed Jewish-Christian environment of late antiquity. It accommodates the earlier composition of 4 Baruch, the preserved provisions, the ruined city and related Honi motifs. It does not require a claim that Muhammad read a Greek manuscript. A stronger conclusion about direct borrowing needs a demonstrable dated intermediary and distinctive verbal or structural sequence. [11] [13] [15]

A second is independent retention of an older historical experience through several traditions. The possibility is not logically excluded by textual differences, but no contemporary witness is supplied for a century-long death and revival, a sixty-six-year sleep or a donkey's reconstructed bones. Historical confidence cannot be raised simply by multiplying related literary witnesses from the same story world. [14] [21]

A third is the Islamic revelation claim: a later text gives the true divine account, which differs from imperfect previous retellings. That is a theological assertion rather than a historically verified mechanism. To establish it independently would require evidence for revelation not derived solely from the Quran's own claim, or independent material evidence of the event. Neither has been identified by the sources examined. [1] [15] [28]

24 / THE BIBLICAL THEOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT HAS A DISTINCT SCOPE

The Biblical theological assessment has a distinct scope

The Biblical writings actually establish the story of Jeremiah, Ebed-Melech and threatened national catastrophe. They do not report that Ebed-Melech slept for sixty-six years, or that an unnamed man died for a century and was shown the restoration of his donkey. The expansion belongs to the history of later religious literature and must be judged on its own evidential basis. [22] [23] [24]

John 11:38-44 presents Jesus raising Lazarus, and 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 reports a first-century Christian proclamation and attributed witnesses concerning Jesus' resurrection. These Biblical texts speak to central Christian resurrection claims, but do not confirm the Quranic traveller's experience. Each specific supernatural event requires its own source assessment; one miracle narrative cannot prove another. [27] [28]

The project's Biblical theological framework does not oblige us to deny a miracle merely because Genesis or Jeremiah is silent about it. It does mean the Quran cannot acquire Biblical historical authority simply by reworking an extracanonical tale. Silence is not an explicit contradiction, but neither is an apocryphon independent confirmation of revelation. [22] [23] [27]

25 / TEXT-FIRST, HISTORICAL AND BIBLICAL VERDICTS

Text-first, historical and Biblical verdicts

Text-first verdict: Quran 2:259 unambiguously states that God caused an unnamed traveller to die for one hundred years and revived him, leaving food unspoiled and displaying the raising of bones. The text mentions a donkey but does not name Ezra, Jeremiah or Jerusalem. The qira'at differ in the verb for the bones. These are positive textual findings, not commentary substituted for Arabic. [1] [2] [5] [10]

Historical verdict: A substantial earlier literary complex is demonstrated, with 4 Baruch's remarkably close preserved-food and ruined-city narrative and Honi's separate extended sleep and donkey episode. The Quran's specific death-and-resurrection claim lacks independent historical corroboration in the inspected sources. Ordinary transmission is a strong explanatory inference; deliberate invention or direct copying by a named individual remains unproven. [11] [13] [15] [18] [21]

Biblical verdict: The canonical texts do not recognise the Quranic traveller as a separately documented Biblical resurrection. Falsification criteria: A securely dated independent witness to a historically identifiable century-long death and return, or a dated pre-Islamic intermediary preserving the Quran's exact constellation of distinctive details, would revise relevant parts of this assessment. The former would affect historicity; the latter would clarify literary dependence, not automatically prove the miracle. [14] [22] [27] [28]
