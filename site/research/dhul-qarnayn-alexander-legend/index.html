Dhul-Qarnayn and the Alexander legend

A passage-level comparison of the eastern and western journeys, the iron gate and the late antique Alexander tradition.

01 / THE LITERARY QUESTION

Dhul-Qarnayn's travels follow a distinctive late-antique narrative

HISTORICAL FINDING

Quran 18:83-102 and the Syriac Alexander Legend share an unusually specific sequence: an empowered world-traveller, the far western and eastern limits, an iron barrier against Gog and Magog and its eventual apocalyptic fate. [1] [4] [6]

This is a substantial question of narrative transmission, not simply a claim that two texts mention the same hero. The strength of the case lies in order, combined motifs and late-antique cultural context.

02 / PRIMARY SCRIPTURE

Quran 18 describes three stages and a constructed barrier

The narrative describes Dhul-Qarnayn receiving means and power, travelling west to the place of sunset, east toward sunrise, and finally to a mountain pass where inhabitants plead for protection from Gog and Magog. He constructs a barrier using iron and molten copper, and says it will fall when God's promise arrives. [1] [2] [3]

The Quran does not give the figure the name Alexander. His identification with Alexander is an interpretive and comparative question; it must not be presented as an explicit line in 18:83-102.

03 / EARLIER LITERARY TRADITION

The Neṣḥānā is not simply the generic Alexander Romance

The Syriac Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs is a late-antique Christian reworking of Alexander traditions. Its ruler travels to cosmic borders and constructs an iron gate against destructive peoples associated with Gog and Magog. Budge's edition prints the work separately from the longer Syriac Alexander Romance. [4] [5]

The distinction matters because a loose citation to any Alexander legend obscures which passages actually match the Quran. The direct comparison is with the Neṣḥānā and related Syriac traditions, rather than with every classical biography of the Macedonian ruler.

04 / PARALLEL ONE

The shared journey to the sun's limits is unusually specific

Quran 18:86 speaks of the sun appearing to set in a dark or muddy spring or body of water, and the next journey reaches a people exposed to its rising. The Syriac legend similarly explores the far reaches of the inhabited world in relation to the sun's course. [2] [4]

Scholars have offered different cosmological interpretations. The Quranic phrase describing what the traveller found at sunset need not mean that the author is claiming the physical Sun fits inside a terrestrial spring. The stronger comparison concerns the distinctive journey sequence and the literary cosmic geography, not a fabricated scientific error.

05 / PARALLEL TWO

Both narratives feature a protective northern barrier against Gog and Magog

The Quran's mountain-pass episode involves iron, heated work and molten copper, explicitly protecting people against Gog and Magog. The Syriac Alexander tradition also makes the construction of an immense northern barrier a defining heroic act. [3] [4] [9]

The Gog and Magog motif itself appears in Ezekiel 38-39, so it was available long before either narrative. Mere mention of these names is not proof of direct copying. The more discriminating evidence is their combination with Alexander-like world journeys, the iron gate and a final end-times release. [12]

06 / ICONOGRAPHY AND IDENTITY

The title fits an existing horned-Alexander tradition

Dhul-Qarnayn is commonly understood as a designation involving two horns. Alexander was represented in some ancient iconography with the horns of Zeus-Ammon, and the Syriac Christian legend gave this imagery a new biblical and apocalyptic interpretation. Tesei's study traces that adaptation through Jewish Danielic imagery. [10] [11]

Some Muslim interpretations identify Dhul-Qarnayn differently, including with Cyrus or another righteous king. These alternatives do not make the narrative parallels vanish, but a historically precise identification requires more than assuming the literal Quranic name is Alexander.

07 / THE CHRONOLOGY PROBLEM

Scholars disagree on whether the legend dates to Justinian or Heraclius

Older scholarship often placed the Syriac legend around the reign of Heraclius in the early seventh century. Tommaso Tesei argues for a sixth-century Justinianic core, with the political setting and redaction reassessed. Both positions are discussed in his Oxford monograph. [7] [8]

This does not licence a facile assertion that the earliest physical manuscript copy was written before every verse of the Quran. There is a difference between a reconstructed date of composition, surviving manuscript witnesses and the wider circulation of stories. The evidence supports a close late-antique literary relationship; precise direction and mechanism of transfer require explicit argument.

08 / MODERN SOURCE CRITICISM

Van Bladel and Tesei provide independent scholarly arguments

Kevin van Bladel's 2008 published study argues that the Quranic episode is a retelling of the Syriac Alexander legend. Tesei's more recent monograph examines the tradition's composition, the details of the gate and the political setting without treating the underlying legend as a literal historical biography. [6] [7] [9]

These are scholarly assessments, not primary ancient eyewitnesses. Their value lies in explicit comparisons of texts and chronology. Readers should be able to compare the ancient passages themselves instead of being asked to accept a modern scholar by reputation.

09 / LEGEND VERSUS BIOGRAPHY

Alexander's supernatural gate is not established historical engineering

Alexander the Great lived in the fourth century BCE. The Syriac material portrays him in a later Christian apocalyptic world populated by Gog and Magog and a barrier awaiting a divine end-time. That narrative is not a contemporary documentary description of the historical Macedonian king's construction project. [7] [9]

If the Quran's character is not Alexander, this specific biographical objection no longer directly applies. But the literary motifs still require an account of why the Quranic version matches so much of the existing Alexander story.

10 / STRONGEST ALTERNATIVE

Shared oral traditions could explain the parallels without a copied manuscript

The similarities do not demonstrate that Muhammad personally read a Syriac document or translated a Greek text. Storytelling, oral exchange and cultural reuse can carry narrative patterns across languages. A shared earlier oral or written source is another possible explanation.

Yet calling the parallels coincidence is increasingly implausible as matching details accumulate. The relevant historical choices are a direct literary relationship, an intermediary common tradition, or independently generated stories. The last option faces the heaviest explanatory burden.

12 / CONCLUSION

Independent revelation is not established by this narrative's features

The Quran's Dhul-Qarnayn episode shares a structured cluster of unusual details with a late-antique Alexander tradition. Those parallels support a strong literary-historical relationship and undermine claims that this narrative is without identifiable analogues in pre-Islamic literature. [1] [4] [6] [7]

Verdict: The assertion that Quran 18:83-102 presents a story with no identifiable pre-Islamic literary parallels fails the evidence test. The parallels are substantial. Direct copying, a common source and the exact historical identity of Dhul-Qarnayn remain separate issues; the text alone does not prove that Alexander built the described apocalyptic barrier.
