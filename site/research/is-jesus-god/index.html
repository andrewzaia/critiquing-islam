Is Jesus God?

The New Testament, the Quran and the problem of earlier Gospel authority.

01 / THE CLAIM

The central contradiction must be stated precisely

TEXTUAL FINDING

The canonical New Testament contains explicit identifications of Jesus with divine status; the Quran expressly rejects the claim that God is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary. These portrayals cannot both be accurate descriptions of the same theological proposition without a further explanation of which text has authority and why.

The question has three distinct levels: (1) what these surviving texts actually say; (2) how early the Christian claims can be traced; and (3) whether the Quran's statements about the Gospel imply endorsement of the Christian texts that were available to its first audience.

This article makes the textual disagreement explicit. It does not pretend that the divinity of Jesus is a laboratory-verifiable historical event. Nor does it assume without argument that the Quran's Injil is identical in scope to the four canonical Gospel books.

02 / WHAT THE QURAN SAYS

The Quran rejects identifying Jesus with God

Quran 5:72 explicitly condemns those who say that God is the Messiah, son of Mary, and attributes to Jesus the instruction to worship God, his Lord and theirs. Quran 5:73 criticises a formulation describing God as a third of three, while 5:75 calls Jesus a messenger and stresses the humanity of Jesus and Mary. [15]

Quran 4:171 calls Jesus a messenger, God's word conveyed to Mary, and a spirit from Him, while rejecting claims of divine sonship and warning against saying three. Quran 19:30-36 presents the infant Jesus as God's servant and prophet, and again rejects God having a son. [16] [17]

These are not subtle differences in terminology. The Quran takes a position against a central Christian affirmation. At the same time, the Quran's criticism of deification should not be paraphrased as though orthodox Christians believe that Jesus is a second god alongside the Father. Nicene Christianity explicitly professes one God, with distinctions of persons. [27]

The Quran's titles for Jesus

Calling Jesus God's word or a spirit from Him does not, by itself, commit the Quran to John's doctrine of the Word made flesh. Their uses must be interpreted within their respective contexts, not merely equated because translations use the word Word. [26] [16]

03 / JOHN'S PROLOGUE

John 1 makes a direct claim about the Word

John 1:1 distinguishes the Word as being with God and describes the Word as God. John 1:3 places the creation of all things in relation to the Word, and 1:14 identifies the Word as having become flesh. Within the narrative, this is a claim about the identity of Jesus, not merely that he carried a message from God. [1]

The text is important because the theological assertion is explicit. A reader may dispute its historical truth or propose a different interpretation of the Greek expression, but cannot accurately summarise the passage as presenting Jesus as an ordinary messenger with no divine status.

A qualification also matters: John is a Gospel written by an early Christian author, not an audio transcript of Jesus. This demonstrates what the Johannine text affirms; identifying the exact words uttered by the historical Jesus is a separate historical question.

04 / A DIRECT ADDRESS

John 20:28 addresses Jesus as Lord and God

In John 20:24-29, the risen Jesus appears to Thomas. Thomas then addresses him as his Lord and God. Jesus' response moves to belief rather than rejecting the form of address. The context makes it difficult to dismiss Thomas' statement as a generic exclamation directed away from Jesus. [2]

This is one of the strongest passages against the claim that the New Testament never calls Jesus God. It is a direct claim within the written narrative. It is not proof that every earlier Christian community used identical terminology or that every reader must accept John's account as an independent historical fact.

John 10:27-39 also presents Jesus claiming a distinctive unity with the Father, which his opponents construe as a claim to divine status. However, the passage invites careful interpretation: oneness of purpose, shared power, and a deeper divine claim are different proposals, and the immediate context matters. [3]

05 / ISAIAH AND REVELATION

A divine title is applied to the risen Jesus

Isaiah 44:6 presents Israel's God as the First and the Last, explicitly excluding rival deities. In Revelation 1:17-18, the speaker identified as one who died and now lives describes himself as the First and the Last. In Revelation's Christian vision, this title is applied to the risen Jesus. [4] [5]

This is a significant intertextual link: a designation strongly associated with Israel's unique God is applied to Christ. It is stronger than the loose claim that every use of the word lord must mean God, since lord can also be an ordinary title of respect or authority.

Revelation is an apocalyptic visionary work. The passage is evidence for its author's very high view of Christ, not a record of Jesus giving the same declaration to a street audience in Galilee.

06 / EARLIEST CHRISTIAN WRITINGS

High claims about Jesus appear before the Gospel of John

One objection holds that belief in Jesus' divine status was invented centuries after his lifetime. The surviving documents do not support so late an origin. Paul's undisputed letters belong to the first century, well before the Quran and before the fourth-century Nicene Council.

Philippians 2:5-11 presents Christ in the form of God, describes a self-humbling into human service, and gives Jesus a cosmic exaltation in which every knee bows and every tongue confesses him as Lord. The final image closely recalls Isaiah 45:22-23, where universal acknowledgement belongs to Israel's God. Scholars debate whether Philippians preserves material preceding Paul's letter and precisely how pre-existence and divine identity are to be understood. The exalted description itself is unquestionably early. [6] [8]

In 1 Corinthians 8:4-6, Paul reaffirms one God while distinguishing the Father, from whom all things come, and the one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom all things come. This gives Jesus a role within creation itself. It does not state the full later Nicene formula, but cannot easily be reduced to a mere teacher's moral example. [7]

Chronology matters

These first-century passages do not prove that every New Testament formulation was fully developed at the start of Jesus' ministry. They do disprove the specific claim that an exalted view of Jesus first arose at a fourth-century council.

07 / MARK AND DANIEL

The earlier Gospel traditions also attribute exceptional authority

Mark 2:1-12 depicts Jesus forgiving sins and being challenged on the ground that only God can forgive sins. The narrative frames his action as more than a generic instruction to repent. The response claims authority for the Son of Man. [9]

Mark 14:61-64 describes Jesus responding to a question about the Messiah, then invoking the Son of Man and the right hand of power, imagery associated with Daniel 7:13-14. His audience in Mark perceives a blasphemous claim. [10] [11]

Neither example is a simple, unambiguous sentence where Jesus says, "I am God" in modern theological vocabulary. Claims of delegated divine authority and participation in God's rule are interpretive possibilities that must be considered. Their value is cumulative: early Christian portrayals of Jesus are not limited to the office of an ordinary prophet.

08 / CHRISTIAN TEXTUAL COMPLEXITY

What about verses where Jesus is distinct from or subordinate to God?

The New Testament also says things that a critic of Jesus' divinity can legitimately cite. In John 17:3, Jesus addresses the Father as the only true God; John 14:28 speaks of the Father as greater; and Mark 13:32 depicts the Son as lacking knowledge of the final hour. [12] [13] [14]

These passages must not be omitted. They show that Biblical Christology is not adequately summarised by stripping away every distinction between Jesus and the Father. Classical Christian theology holds that Father and Son are distinct persons and speaks of the Son's human life and self-humbling. Its interpretation is not that Jesus is the Father under another name. [27]

A non-Trinitarian reading may instead understand Jesus as subordinate, exalted or uniquely commissioned without being fully divine. That is a genuine interpretive dispute. However, invoking the countertexts does not erase the explicit language in John 1 or John 20 or the early cosmic portrayal in Philippians and 1 Corinthians.

09 / PRE-ISLAMIC WITNESSES

The chronological problem for a late correction

The first-century letters of Paul, followed by the canonical Gospels and Revelation, contain the divine and exalted claims examined above. Material Biblical witnesses help test whether these writings were circulating before Islam. Codex Vaticanus, catalogued by the Vatican Library as a fourth-century Greek Bible, preserves the Gospels and major Pauline letters, although its original surviving contents are incomplete. [30] Codex Sinaiticus, also a fourth-century Greek Bible, preserves the complete New Testament, including Revelation, with images and a scholarly transcription available for investigation. [29] [31]

These witnesses are especially relevant for the textual existence of passages such as John 1, John 20 and Philippians 2 centuries before the Quran. A case about one precise Greek reading should still be checked against the manuscript image, transcription and known variants. The mere age of a codex does not establish that every line in a modern translation is identical to its earliest composition.

For Old Testament quotations used by Christian authors, the Septuagint supplies an earlier Greek-language scriptural setting. It began as Jewish Greek translation work before Christianity and later became part of Christian Greek Biblical manuscripts. The Septuagint is a family of translation texts, not a third individual codex; its relationship to the Hebrew textual traditions and to particular New Testament allusions must be evaluated passage by passage. [32]

These catalogued manuscripts establish textual circulation well before Islam, not the supernatural truth of the recorded claims. Codex Vaticanus should not be cited as direct evidence for the original Greek text of Revelation in its surviving fourth-century portion; Sinaiticus is the relevant early manuscript witness here.

This establishes early attestation of the Christian beliefs. It does not automatically establish the theological truth of those beliefs. Nor does it show that all early Christians agreed: competing explanations of Jesus' status existed before Islam, so describing the Quran as the first or only dissenting text would be historically inaccurate.

10 / THE QURAN AND EARLIER SCRIPTURE

What exactly does the Quran affirm about the Gospel?

Quran 3:3-4 presents the Torah and Gospel as earlier revelations. Quran 5:46 describes the Gospel given to Jesus as containing guidance and light. Quran 5:47 then directs the People of the Gospel to judge by what God revealed within it. Quran 5:68 similarly challenges the People of Scripture to uphold the Torah and Gospel. [25] [19] [20]

These statements raise a concrete question: what usable revelation was available to the communities addressed by the Quran, and how could they obey an instruction to judge by it? A categorical assertion that Christians possessed no meaningful access to earlier divine guidance needs to explain that instruction.

Quran 10:94 conditionally directs consultation of people who were reading earlier scripture. This acknowledges readers of earlier writings, but does not alone guarantee the unaltered wording of every manuscript they possessed. [21]

The difficulty becomes acute when these affirmations are placed beside the Quran's direct rejection of Jesus' divinity. John's Gospel and early Christian writings were not composed after Islam. Substantial portions survive in Greek manuscripts predating Muhammad by centuries, including Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus. [30] [29] This rules out treating the central Christian texts under discussion as a later response invented after the Quran.

If the earlier revelation upheld by Quran 5:46-47 includes those surviving Johannine claims, then there is an explicit doctrinal conflict: the Quran rejects a divine identification preserved in the earlier Christian tradition. Whether the Quran intended to endorse those particular sentences, rather than only what it regards as original revelation, is the disputed bridge in the argument.

11 / THE STRONGEST ISLAMIC RESPONSE

Quran 5:48 is an essential qualification

Quran 5:48 does not merely state that the Quran confirms previous scripture. It also portrays the Quran as muhaymin over earlier scripture, often translated as guardian, overseer or criterion. A Muslim interpretation can therefore distinguish authentic original revelation from alleged later alterations and assign the Quran a corrective role. [19] The classical commentary of Ibn Kathir explicitly presents the Quran as judging claims made in previous scripture. [33]

This is a coherent theological account of how someone can affirm the original Gospel while rejecting particular propositions in the surviving New Testament. It means the alleged contradiction is not a simple formal inconsistency unless the argument establishes that the Quran also endorses the disputed content of the extant Gospels.

However, an appeal to the Quran as a correcting standard is not itself independent historical proof that a particular passage in John was added, altered or falsely attributed. Such a historical claim requires identifiable textual witnesses, dates, transmission evidence or a defensible argument about composition. An allegation of a change after the fourth century faces the evidence of the surviving codices; an allegation of an earlier change is not ruled out by their date alone. Both require a specified passage and a traceable historical argument. [29] [31]

The critical question

Which early Christian statement about Jesus' divine status is alleged to have been corrupted, by whom, when, and on what textual evidence? A later scripture's disagreement identifies the competing belief but cannot by itself reconstruct the earlier text's history.

12 / TERMINOLOGY AND TEXT

The Injil and four canonical Gospels must not be silently equated

Within Islamic theology, the Injil is a revelation given to Jesus. In the New Testament and early Christian usage, the gospel is the proclaimed message about Jesus and the saving action attributed to him; the four canonical Gospels are written narrative accounts of his life and teaching. These descriptions do not have identical definitions simply because the same English word, Gospel, is used.

A Muslim response may say that Jesus' original revelation was not identical to the later four books. That conceptual distinction is valid. But turning it into the historical assertion that Jesus possessed a now-lost written book which taught a specifically Quranic view of him requires evidence not supplied simply by the terminology of Quran 5:46. [19]

Conversely, Christian manuscripts documenting what early communities believed about Jesus do not prove that every remembered saying in the Gospels is a word-for-word transcript. The fair comparison distinguishes historical attestation, literary interpretation and doctrinal truth.

13 / WORDS, RECORDS AND PRESERVATION

Does the Quran promise preservation of Jesus' message?

The strongest version of this criticism must not rely on the false assertion that Quran 15:9 unambiguously guarantees every letter of the later Christian Bible. Quran 15:9 promises protection of the Reminder; in its Quranic context, this is generally understood to refer to the Quran. [22]

Quran 18:27 and 6:115 state that God's words cannot be changed. These are strong declarations concerning divine revelation and authority, but moving from them to a claim about the perfect scribal transmission of every extant Biblical manuscript is a further interpretive step. [23] [24]

The more securely grounded criticism combines the Quran's positive language about the Gospel in 5:46-48 and 5:68 with the pre-Islamic documentary record of Christianity. The question is no longer whether Christians possessed writings that gave Jesus divine status before Muhammad: the surviving manuscripts establish that this textual tradition existed. The historical issue is how the Quran's praise and instruction regarding the Gospel relate to that documented tradition. [19] [31] [29]

If the available Gospel was substantially authoritative, why does the Quran reject an important teaching already recorded within it? If the Quran affirmed only uncorrupted original revelation, what specific evidence identifies the rejected statements as later additions, and what access to the genuine guidance would its Christian audience have had? The appeal to Quran 5:48 explains the Islamic doctrinal position but does not, without more evidence, verify an alleged change to the earlier Greek text.

The specific Johannine claims in John 1:1-14 and 20:24-29 are not post-Islamic constructions. They belong to a Gospel transmitted long before Muhammad, with an early papyrus witness in P66 and fourth-century codices among the surviving Greek evidence. This gives the textual disagreement an identifiable historical baseline, while leaving intact the separate question of whether every Biblical reading originated with the earliest author. [29] [30] [34]

Quran 12:111 and 16:89 speak of explaining all things in detail or comprehensively, and 6:114 describes the book as explained in detail. An obvious critical question follows: why is a proposed alteration of such a central claim about Jesus not identified by passage and transmission evidence? But the terms need interpretation. Ibn Kathir understands this language broadly in the realm of guidance, belief and religious rulings, not as an explicit guarantee to document each ancient manuscript variant. [35] [36] [37] [38]

The historically grounded criticism is not that a later sacred text must contain a complete manuscript catalogue. It is that its disagreement with earlier surviving texts does not itself provide an independent demonstration that those earlier texts were fabricated. The competing claims should be evaluated by their actual textual and historical support.

15 / CONCLUSION

What does the evidence actually establish?

It is textually indisputable that canonical New Testament writings identify Jesus with divine status and that the Quran rejects identifying God with the Messiah. The high Christian claims occur in writings centuries older than the Quran, including first-century Pauline material. A claim that Jesus' divinity was only invented by the Nicene Council does not fit the surviving literary record.

The Quran's affirmation of earlier revelation creates a serious interpretive and historical question when combined with its rejection of claims found in early Christian texts. But the inference is conditional: one must establish precisely which Gospel text the Quran means to affirm, what it allows the Quran to correct, and whether a proposed corruption claim is historically supported.

The argument is strongest when it asks for evidence of a specific change, rather than declaring without qualification that the Quran promises every word of the canonical Gospels was preserved. The documents show a major disagreement over Jesus' identity; evaluating the truth of competing revelations requires historical reasoning beyond simply assuming either book's infallibility.

Verdict: An unsupported corruption claim does not overturn earlier Christian testimony. High claims about Jesus' divinity are documented in Christian texts centuries before the Quran, including early Pauline material. The Quran rejects those claims but does not independently demonstrate that their presence in the surviving Christian tradition is due to textual corruption. This is a failure of historical corroboration, not proof of a supernatural doctrine.
