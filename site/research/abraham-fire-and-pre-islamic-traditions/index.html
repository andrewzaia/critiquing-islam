An old literary witness proves the story circulated, not that an ancient miracle occurred.

01 / WHAT THE QURAN CLAIMS AND WHAT MUST BE PROVED

What the Quran claims and what must be proved

Quran 21:51-70 portrays Abraham challenging idol worship, destroying the objects of worship and receiving miraculous deliverance when his opponents seek to burn him. The account is not just a moral against idolatry: the command to the fire in 21:69 is presented as God's intervention in a physical threat. Quran 29:24 explicitly calls the rescue a sign for believers. The precise historical proposition is that the described miracle occurred, not merely that an inspiring tale circulated. [1] [5] [7]

The strongest critical question is not whether a Jewish author had previously mentioned fire. It is whether the Quran supplies independently authenticated history or repeats a recognizable cluster of already circulating religious narratives. We distinguish the chronological priority of literary accounts, possible contact between communities, factual corroboration of an alleged ancient event, and the separate truth claim of divine revelation. None is interchangeable with the others. [14] [17] [20]

An earlier story does not logically disprove a later revelation that might recount the same event. But when the only support for the miracle is competing literary traditions, a new claim of divine authority cannot verify itself. A historian must ask what independently dated witness could establish that Abraham was rescued from actual flames, rather than simply establish that communities told this story. [17] [19] [25]

02 / THE QURANIC STARTING POINT IS THE IDOLS, NOT NIMROD

The Quranic starting point is the idols, not Nimrod

The narrative opens at 21:51 with Abraham's God-given discernment, followed by his criticism of his father and people in 21:52-56. These verses place the scene within a debate about manufactured objects receiving worship. The father is not named in this passage, and the language does not identify a named king or a specific city. Any such identification must come from another source and be labelled accordingly. [1] [10]

Quran 21:57 reports Abraham's intention to devise an action against the idols. The physical attack happens while the worshippers are absent; the subsequent discovery provokes an interrogation. The plot is thus more elaborate than a simple report that Abraham was faithful and persecuted. It combines a confrontation with inherited religion, a staged challenge to an idol's power and a threatened punishment. [2] [3]

The test for literary relationship is the sequence of actions, not just the word fire. A similar scene featuring idolatrous relatives, an idol demolished to expose its impotence and a miraculous rescue is more probative of a shared narrative environment than a generic similarity between two accounts of divine protection. It still cannot identify the exact transmission path without an intermediary witness. [3] [21] [25]

03 / THE CHIEF IDOL AND THE QUESTION IT CANNOT ANSWER

The chief idol and the question it cannot answer

In Quran 21:58-63 Abraham breaks the idols except their largest and directs the questioners to ask it what happened. The conditional phrase about the idols being able to speak is central to the argument. The statement attributed to the largest idol operates as a rhetorical demonstration of the idols' inability to act or speak, not as a historical allegation that a statue could really commit vandalism. [3]

Genesis Rabbah 38:13 contains a strikingly close theatrical device. In its idol-shop story Abraham tells his father Terah that the largest idol smashed the others during a dispute over offerings. Terah objects that the idols possess no understanding, and Abraham turns Terah's objection against the act of worship. The cast, setting and immediate dialogue are not identical to the Quran, but the argumentative structure is conspicuously similar. [21] [25]

This is stronger evidence than merely noting that both texts oppose idolatry. It is also narrower than proof of copying. The fifth-century rabbinic compilation preserves a pre-Islamic form of the story, but a specific Arabic intermediary, Muhammad's direct access to the Hebrew work and dependence on this precise recension have not been demonstrated here. [21] [22] [25]

04 / THE COMMAND TO BURN ABRAHAM IS UNMISTAKABLE

The command to burn Abraham is unmistakable

Quran 21:68 gives the imperative harriquhu, burn him. It is linked to a demand that the community support its gods. The threat is a response to Abraham's challenge and is reported as the enemies' own proposal, not a metaphor invented by a modern translator. The surrounding plot culminates in God's intervention and the opponents' failure. [4] [6]

Unlike a plain statement that Abraham escaped the land of idol worship, the command in Quran 21:69 addresses fire itself. The word nar denotes fire; the verse is not simply describing Abraham's internal confidence or his spiritual survival of adversity. A figurative interpretation would need an independent linguistic and contextual justification strong enough to explain the command to the fire and the rescue in 29:24. [5] [7]

The earlier historical evidence is literary rather than eyewitness. Pseudo-Philo narrates actual exposure to a furnace and an unharmed Abraham. Genesis Rabbah describes Nimrod ordering an ordeal and a rescue. Their wording and plot mechanics differ from the Quran, but both establish that a miraculous physical fire deliverance was already available as Jewish narrative material. [17] [21]

05 / THE ARABIC COMMAND FOR COOLNESS AND SAFETY

The Arabic command for coolness and safety

The decisive phrase in Quran 21:69 is ya naru kuni bardan wa-salaman ala Ibrahima: fire is ordered to become coolness and safety for Abraham. The Corpus identifies the imperative kuni and the nouns bardan and salaman. Whatever mechanism a reader proposes, the narrated divine agency is explicit. This is not a later hadith supplying a miracle omitted by the Quran. [5]

Verse 21:70 then presents the adversaries as defeated in their plotting. Verse 29:24 states that God saved Abraham from fire. The combination means the Quranic claim is more specific than a general declaration that God assists prophets: it asserts a threatened burning followed by rescue and presents that event as evidence of divine power. [6] [7]

However, neither verse names an eyewitness who left a contemporary record, gives the year of the episode, or links the event to an archaeological context that would allow a historian to authenticate it. These omissions do not disprove the event. They do mean that the Quran's confident narration cannot function as independent verification of its own supernatural content. [5] [7] [17]

06 / QURAN 29 AND 37 CONFIRM THE FIRE BUT NOT EVERY DETAIL

Quran 29 and 37 confirm the fire but not every detail

Quran 29:24 reports the answer of Abraham's people as a proposal to kill or burn him, followed by divine rescue. This version compresses the idol episode rather than supplying new witnesses. It matters because its language unambiguously locates the attack among his people without identifying their political leader. Multiple passages within one scripture are not independent external attestations. [7]

Quran 37:97 says that Abraham's opponents should build a bunyan, a constructed structure, and cast him into al-jahim, the blazing fire. Some English versions interpret the structure as a furnace. The Arabic itself speaks of a building or structure; the furnace characterisation is supported by narrative context, not a distinct lexical word for an industrial kiln. [8]

Comparing the two passages demonstrates both consistency and selective detail. One supplies an explicit rescue, another an elaborate preparation for burning, and Quran 21:69 supplies the divine command. The combined Quranic presentation supports the fact that the text teaches a fire miracle. It does not automatically corroborate a later detailed legend about an individual tyrant, a particular furnace or numerous spectators. [4] [5] [7] [8]

07 / NIMROD IS NOT IDENTIFIED AS THE PERSECUTOR IN THE QURAN

Nimrod is not identified as the persecutor in the Quran

The identification of Nimrod as Abraham's opponent belongs to earlier Jewish interpretive literature and later reception of the narrative, not to the Quranic fire verses themselves. Quran 2:258 mentions a man who disputes with Abraham over divine power, but does not name him or state that he ordered a furnace. It would be methodologically wrong to quote the name Nimrod as though 2:258 or 21:68 used it. [9] [21]

Quran 6:74 names Azar in an address to Abraham's father, whereas Genesis identifies Terah. That is a distinct textual problem already examined in CI-036; neither the identity dispute nor a proposed reconciliation supplies independent witnesses to the fire. The fire critique should not depend on resolving every element of Abraham's family tree. [10] [11]

Genesis Rabbah 38:13 does assign the encounter to Nimrod, and that source is relevant evidence for what the Jewish tradition had become before Islam. Pseudo-Philo, however, names a different antagonist, Jectan or Joktan, in a Tower of Babel setting. The existence of these two versions is a reason to examine literary development, not to pretend that every ancient source tells precisely the same story. [17] [21] [25]

08 / GENESIS SAYS UR OF THE CHALDEANS, NOT A FURNACE MIRACLE

Genesis says Ur of the Chaldeans, not a furnace miracle

Genesis 11:26-32 identifies Terah, Abram and Haran, records Haran's death during his father's lifetime and narrates the departure from Ur of the Chaldeans. It does not tell a story in which Nimrod burns Abraham, nor does it report that Abraham survives a furnace. The distinction matters because the historical debate is about material added by later narratives, not a direct contradiction between two explicit accounts of the same fire. [11] [26]

Joshua 24:2-3 acknowledges that Abraham's ancestral family worshipped other gods. This provides a Biblical basis for portraying his early faith as a break with an idolatrous environment. It still does not mention the dramatic destruction of statues, an idol-shop exchange or a rescue from burning. The scriptural background creates an interpretive opening without supplying the later scene as eyewitness history. [12]

Nehemiah 9:7-8 describes Abraham's selection and departure from Ur. Some subsequent interpreters read the word Ur through a fire-related wordplay, but the canonical text itself does not specify a violent ordeal. To argue that Genesis secretly contains the whole furnace narrative requires more than a resemblance in the written consonants of a place name. [13] [26] [27]

09 / JUBILEES ALREADY STAGES ABRAHAM'S REJECTION OF IDOLS

Jubilees already stages Abraham's rejection of idols

Book of Jubilees 12:1-8 presents a conversation in which Abraham challenges Terah's devotion to lifeless manufactured gods. His father acknowledges the difficulty of opposing the surrounding people and warns him to remain quiet for safety. This text is a Jewish expansion of the Abraham narrative that substantially predates the Quran. It supplies a recoverable context for anti-idol dialogue without establishing the authenticity of every speech assigned to ancient characters. [14]

Jubilees is significant because the Quran's idol polemic is not isolated in the literature of late antiquity. Earlier Jewish authors had already built episodes from the simpler Biblical observations that Abraham's family served other gods and that God called him out of that environment. The inherited tradition could grow through theological storytelling without an independently preserved record from Abraham's own era. [12] [14]

The existence of a narrative in Jubilees and in Genesis Rabbah should not be counted as two independent witnesses to the supposed actions of Abraham. These are literary interpreters connected to earlier scriptural material, and the latter text can reflect continuing elaboration of themes already present in the former. The historian must map that relationship before multiplying attestations. [14] [21] [25]

10 / IN JUBILEES, ABRAHAM STARTS THE FIRE AND HARAN DIES

In Jubilees, Abraham starts the fire and Haran dies

Jubilees 12:12-14 provides the first critical reversal. Abraham burns the house containing his people's idols. His brother Haran rushes in to rescue those idols, is burned and dies. Abraham does not need to be saved from the flames in that episode. The agents and consequences therefore differ from the Quran's version, where the opponents seek to burn Abraham and God rescues him. [15] [7]

This difference is not an insignificant spelling variant. The early Jewish work and the Quran tell materially different episodes concerning fire, destruction of idols and Abraham's family. It is possible that both drew on related storytelling traditions and chose different developments. But it is unjustified to merge them into one seamless historical account without additional testimony. [15] [17]

The Haran motif also supplies an explanation for how Genesis 11:28 could inspire later narrative expansion. The Bible says Haran died in Ur before his father. A story of fire gives an imaginative answer to why he died, while another tradition asks why Abraham was brought out of Ur. This literary rationale may explain transmission; it does not prove the narrated fire occurred. [11] [15] [27]

11 / WHAT THE DEAD SEA SCROLLS DO AND DO NOT ESTABLISH

What the Dead Sea Scrolls do and do not establish

The Israel Antiquities Authority lists 4Q216 as a Hebrew Jubilees manuscript written in a Hasmonean-period script. This is valuable material evidence that a form of Jubilees circulated long before Islam. It also prevents an argument that Jubilees itself must be a medieval composition invented after the Quran. [16]

That material witness must not be overclaimed. The existence of an early fragment of a literary work does not establish that the exact wording of Jubilees 12:12-14 is physically preserved on that fragment. Specific line-level manuscript attestation requires a separate collation. Here the scroll establishes a pre-Islamic textual tradition, while later manuscript transmission supplies the cited passage. [15] [16]

A manuscript can also attest what a community read without verifying what the story says happened centuries earlier. The evidential categories are distinct: physical dating supports circulation, textual comparison supports literary priority, and independent historical corroboration would be required to turn the miracle into demonstrable history. Confusing these levels is as serious when defending a Biblical apocryphon as when defending the Quran. [16] [19]

12 / PSEUDO-PHILO HAS ABRAHAM SURVIVING A FURNACE

Pseudo-Philo has Abraham surviving a furnace

Biblical Antiquities 6:16-18, commonly attributed to Pseudo-Philo, describes Abraham being placed in a furnace with bricks and surviving unharmed. A divine earthquake accompanies the event, and fire consumes many others around the furnace. Unlike Jubilees 12, this version already contains the decisive motif of Abraham exposed to a lethal fire and miraculously delivered. It is a much closer parallel to Quran 21:68-70. [17]

The literary location is nevertheless important. This episode is embedded in an expanded account of the Tower of Babel, not in the same fully developed idol-shop scene found in Genesis Rabbah. The narrative opponent is named Jectan, usually identified with biblical Joktan, rather than Nimrod. The Quran's immediate enemies are collectively described without a named leader. Similarities and differences must both appear in the evidence matrix. [17] [25]

This is a key correction to simplistic critiques that cite only a fifth-century rabbinic midrash. The furnace-rescue story demonstrably belongs to a tradition whose composition scholars place much earlier than Genesis Rabbah. An early story can be historically imaginative, but its early date makes it impossible to claim that such a fire deliverance first emerged with the Quran. [17] [18] [20]

13 / COMPOSITION IS NOT THE SAME AS THE SURVIVING MANUSCRIPT

Composition is not the same as the surviving manuscript

Scholarly introductions generally place Biblical Antiquities around the first or second century AD, with debate around the period immediately before or after AD 70. The exact authorship and original language remain questions of critical scholarship. The name Pseudo-Philo is a modern convenience: the ancient author was not demonstrably Philo of Alexandria. A precise claimed autograph date would exceed the evidence. [18] [19] [20]

The accessible complete textual witnesses are much later Latin manuscripts, with Cambridge's overview dating them from the eleventh to fifteenth centuries. An earlier Hebrew original and Greek intermediary are scholarly reconstructions of transmission, not surviving copies in the author's hand. We must not write as though the modern online English translation is itself a first-century papyrus. [18] [19]

The rigorous historical conclusion is still substantial. Critical scholarship places the literary work centuries before Islam, and the furnace passage is part of its transmitted narrative. The late physical copies qualify the kind of evidence available, but do not warrant treating the work as a newly invented Islamic-era text. Conversely, the work's old composition date does not turn the miracle into independently authenticated Bronze Age history. [17] [19] [20]

14 / GENESIS RABBAH TIES THE IDOL SHOP TO THE FURNACE

Genesis Rabbah ties the idol shop to the furnace

Genesis Rabbah 38:13 adds a famous narrative in which Terah places Abraham in charge of an idol shop. Abraham breaks the smaller statues, puts the stick with the largest one and then challenges Terah to believe the claim that the chief idol acted. The logical reversal closely tracks the rhetorical role of the large idol in Quran 21:63. [3] [21]

The sequence continues when Terah brings Abraham to Nimrod. The ruler proposes worship of fire, and Abraham counters with a chain of comparisons to water, clouds, wind and humanity. Nimrod then decides to throw him into the fire. The presentation is a theological and literary argument against idolatry as much as a purported biography. It must be examined as both narrative and exegetical performance. [21] [25]

Scholarship generally dates the composition and redaction of Genesis Rabbah to roughly the fifth century AD in Roman Palestine, though its traditions and textual layers need not all have originated simultaneously. Thus its narrative is earlier than the Quran as a written collection, while the exact date of this particular anecdote remains less precisely recoverable. The document is evidence of Jewish literary circulation, not an eyewitness deposition. [22] [25]

15 / HARAN AND NIMROD BELONG TO THE MIDRASHIC DEVELOPMENT

Haran and Nimrod belong to the midrashic development

In Genesis Rabbah, Abraham survives the ordeal and Haran then declares loyalty to his brother. Haran is cast into the fire and dies, linking the tale to Genesis 11:28. This arrangement differs from Jubilees, where Haran dies while trying to rescue idols from a fire that Abraham started. It also differs from Pseudo-Philo, in which the immediate antagonist is Jectan. These distinctions matter for any proposed lineage of stories. [11] [15] [17] [21]

An argument that the Quran repeated an already elaborated Jewish tale is supported by a cluster of similar features, including the rhetorical idol challenge and the miraculous fire deliverance. Yet the Quran does not recite the midrashic dialogue with Nimrod, nor Haran's subsequent death. Thus we can establish a shared late-antique narrative repertoire more securely than a verbatim copying relationship. [3] [5] [21]

A defender might propose that the versions select different true events. That is logically possible. It would require a positive source identifying multiple fires, multiple persecutors or sequential episodes rather than merely reconciling contradictions by invention. Without such evidence, history cannot authenticate a complete harmonised biography from divergent literary witnesses. [15] [17] [21]

16 / JEROME RECORDS THE FIRE TRADITION BEFORE ISLAM

Jerome records the fire tradition before Islam

Jerome's Hebrew Questions on Genesis 11:28 refers to a Jewish tradition in which Abraham was cast into fire after rejecting idolatrous worship and was delivered by divine aid. This Christian author's report is valuable because it indicates that the story circulated across Jewish-Christian interpretive settings before the Quran. Jerome does not supply a contemporary witness to Abraham. [24]

Jerome connects the tradition with the language of Ur of the Chaldeans. His testimony also exposes the role of scriptural interpretation: readers could hear the place name as an occasion to speak of fire and use the narrative to make sense of Abraham's departure. The later Christian record must be studied as evidence for the reception of a Jewish tradition, not as proof that Jerome had independently verified its ancient origin. [24] [27]

Because Jerome's description differs in particulars from Genesis Rabbah and Pseudo-Philo, it is not sound to count every mention as an independent eyewitness or as a single unaltered historical account. Together they establish a durable pre-Islamic narrative tradition, with adaptation over time. The degree of direct literary dependence among all recensions is more difficult to demonstrate. [17] [21] [24]

17 / UR AND FIRE: A WORDPLAY IS NOT AN ARCHAEOLOGICAL DISCOVERY

Ur and fire: a wordplay is not an archaeological discovery

Genesis 11:28 names Ur of the Chaldeans as a place associated with Haran's death. Comparative Hebrew and Greek witnesses show the geographical setting of the Biblical line. Some ancient and medieval readers associated the written form Ur with a term for fire and read God's removal of Abraham from Ur as rescue from a furnace. This is interpretation of a place name, not a newly discovered primary statement in Genesis about a fire miracle. [11] [26] [27]

Scholarly work on the Glossa Hebraica traces this explanatory wordplay and its later Jewish and Christian reception. The parallel with fire can help explain why additional episodes developed around Genesis 11:28 and 15:7. It does not show that the historical city was literally named after an ordeal involving Abraham, nor does it supply a date for the miracle. [13] [27]

The competing accounts are easier to understand when the literal Biblical text, the interpretive pun and the expanded drama are held apart. Critics who treat a possible etymological prompt as proof that an author deliberately fabricated a story go beyond the evidence; defenders who treat the pun as hidden confirmation of a miracle make the symmetrical mistake. [26] [27]

18 / DANIEL 3 SUPPLIES A BIBLICAL MODEL FOR FIRE DELIVERANCE

Daniel 3 supplies a Biblical model for fire deliverance

Daniel 3:19-27 narrates three Jewish men surviving punishment in a furnace under Nebuchadnezzar. Unlike Abraham's ordeal, the scene is actually narrated in the canonical Biblical book. The common motifs include a hostile ruler, conflict over worship, lethal heat, divine preservation and amazement that fire fails to harm God's servants. This is a potential literary model for the later Abraham stories. [23]

Pseudo-Philo's episode has a furnace and destruction of nearby enemies. Genesis Rabbah features a persecuting ruler and Abraham unharmed. Similar motifs could have been adapted from Daniel's established Biblical story into an Abrahamic setting. That is a serious literary hypothesis, but proving a particular author's conscious use of Daniel requires more than a shared miracle type. [17] [21] [23] [25]

From a Biblical perspective, the presence of a fire deliverance in Daniel does not authorise the historian to transplant the event to Abraham. Christian acceptance of Daniel as scripture may justify a theological view of that Biblical story, but the canonical text does not establish that Abraham underwent a matching ordeal. Related supernatural motifs are not a substitute for evidence about different historical individuals. [11] [23] [28]

19 / THE WITNESSES ARE EARLIER, BUT THEY ARE NOT INDEPENDENT OF ONE ANOTHER

The witnesses are earlier, but they are not independent of one another

The chronological sequence matters: Genesis presents Ur and an idolatrous ancestry; Jubilees expands Abraham's rejection of idols and Haran's death in a fire; Pseudo-Philo recounts Abraham surviving a furnace; Jerome records a Jewish fire tradition; Genesis Rabbah joins the idol-shop argument to Nimrod's ordeal; the Quran later tells its own fire rescue narrative. These milestones show a developed pre-Islamic repertoire, not a single unchanged chain of quotation. [11] [14] [15] [17] [21] [24]

The dates are also different kinds of dates. A composition date for Jubilees is not an extant autograph; a Hasmonean manuscript of Jubilees does not necessarily carry the exact chapter under discussion; the reconstructed first- or second-century date of Pseudo-Philo is different from its eleventh-century Latin witnesses; a fifth-century midrashic redaction is different from the date of every tradition it incorporates. [16] [18] [19] [22]

Several of these works interpret a shared Biblical figure and related passages. Their existence provides independent evidence of literary circulation across periods, but not multiple independent eyewitness attestations of the purported miracle. The strongest sustainable historical claim is that the Quran tells a story that already had a long, demonstrable Jewish literary history. [20] [25]

21 / THREE EXPLANATIONS AND THE EVIDENCE EACH WOULD REQUIRE

Three explanations and the evidence each would require

The first explanation is ordinary transmission and reshaping of Jewish narrative material in the religious world of late antiquity. It fits the documented chronology, the shared chief-idol argument and the earlier fire deliverance motif. Its strongest form does not need to assert that Muhammad personally read Pseudo-Philo or Genesis Rabbah. The missing evidence is a securely located and dated intermediary that establishes a specific route into the Quranic version. [3] [17] [21] [25]

The second explanation is a genuine historical rescue remembered in multiple forms. The texts might preserve an older core, but they differ on the killer, victim and fate of Haran, while no independent contemporary record is cited. Showing that several religious communities told a story is not equivalent to demonstrating that the event occurred. A plausible older oral tradition needs evidence of its age and independence, not an assumption. [15] [17] [21]

The third explanation is direct divine revelation of the true account in the Quran. That is a theological assertion rather than a historical conclusion established by these sources. To move it beyond circular validation, its proponent would need independent grounds for the Quran's revelatory authority or independently corroborating material concerning the actual ancient incident. Neither the Quran's own claim nor the older midrash can do that work by itself. [5] [7] [20]

22 / THE BIBLICAL VERDICT IS NOT THE SAME AS THE HISTORICAL VERDICT

The Biblical verdict is not the same as the historical verdict

The Bible is the theological benchmark of this project. Genesis, Joshua and Acts testify to Abraham's family background, divine calling and migration. Daniel narrates a deliverance from a furnace involving three different individuals. The canonical text does not positively narrate the Quran's Abrahamic fire miracle. A Biblical-Christian critique is therefore entitled to reject the proposition that this later event has become established Biblical history. [11] [12] [23] [28]

It would be an error to say that Genesis explicitly asserts that Abraham was never thrown into fire. Absence of an episode from a selective narrative does not prove it impossible. The decisive distinction is between an episode that Biblical scripture actually affirms and an extra-Biblical tradition that must be separately substantiated. A critic gains nothing by manufacturing a contradiction from silence. [11] [28]

Nor does the failure to authenticate the fire rescue imply that every claim about Abraham or every divine rescue in the Bible is false. Such a global inference would ignore the independent evidence standards used in this investigation. The specific historical burden rests on identifying a source close enough to the alleged event to distinguish a real miracle from later imaginative religious retelling. [17] [23] [25]

23 / TEXT-FIRST, HISTORICAL AND BIBLICAL VERDICTS

Text-first, historical and Biblical verdicts

Text-first verdict: The Quran directly reports Abraham's attack on idols, a threat to burn him and a divine rescue in which fire is commanded to become coolness and safety. It does not name Nimrod as the fire persecutor, does not narrate Haran's death in this event, and does not supply independent eyewitness testimony. This is an explicit supernatural assertion, not merely an allegory introduced by interpreters. [3] [4] [5] [7] [8] [9]

Historical verdict: Pre-Islamic Jewish texts demonstrably preserve the chief-idol argumentative device and Abraham's deliverance from a furnace. Jubilees, Pseudo-Philo, Jerome and Genesis Rabbah document several stages and forms of that tradition. None proves an actual ancient miracle. The claim that the Quran offers independently authenticated historical disclosure therefore fails the available evidence test; a specific copying path or deliberate invention by Muhammad is not demonstrated. [15] [17] [18] [21] [24]

Biblical verdict: The canonical Biblical writings do not establish the claimed Abrahamic fire miracle. The story cannot acquire Biblical authority simply through repetition in the Quran. Falsification criteria: A securely dated independent ancient record of Abraham surviving a fire, a provable early link to the event outside later literary retellings, or a dated linguistic intermediary tracing the precise Quranic version would alter parts of this assessment. No such witness is identified by the cited evidence. [11] [23] [25] [28]
