The Satanic Verses: revelation, retraction and early testimony

Quran 53:19-23 and 22:52 compared with al-Tabari's earliest attributed accounts, Bukhari's prostration reports and the Biblical tests for prophecy.

01 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The claim under examination

The Satanic Verses episode is a report that Muhammad, while reciting chapter 53, uttered words approving the intercession of al-Lat, al-Uzza and Manat, and that the utterance was later withdrawn as Satan's insertion. The allegation is not that the currently received Quran praises those deities. It is about an alleged earlier public utterance and subsequent correction. [1] [11]

The historical question is whether that episode actually occurred. The textual question is whether the extant Quran acknowledges a possible Satanic intrusion into prophetic recitation. The Biblical question is whether a revelation claim could meet the earlier test of fidelity to God's message if such an episode occurred. These require different kinds of proof. [11] [15]

02 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Quran 53:19-23, the surviving wording

The received text names al-Lat, al-Uzza and Manat in verses 19-20, questions the attribution of female beings to God in verses 21-22, then dismisses them as names lacking divine authorisation in verse 23. A reading of the present passage alone therefore yields rejection of those deities, not divine endorsement of their intercession. [1] [2]

The controversial approval formula is not part of the text of verses 19-23 as presented by the standard Quran. It would be an error to quote it as though it were present canonical scripture. An investigation of alleged withdrawal must compare the reported formula with that actually transmitted text and identify the witness for each. [2]

03 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The alleged gharaniq wording

In al-Tabari's historical report attached to 22:52, an attributed narrative says Satan placed the formula tilka al-gharaniq al-ula, wa-inna shafa'atahunna la-turtaja on Muhammad's tongue. It describes the deities as exalted beings whose intercession was hoped for. The Arabic form of the first noun and the closing verbal wording show variation across versions. [11] [15]

A formula quoted in a tenth-century compilation, even when traced through earlier transmitters, is evidence for a tradition. It is not itself a seventh-century audio record or a surviving manuscript page from the alleged original recitation. Its evidentiary status must be declared whenever its words are analysed. [15]

04 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Quran 22:52 and its own admission

The Quran says no messenger or prophet was sent before Muhammad without Satan casting something into his tamanna or umniyya, whereupon God cancels or removes what Satan casts and confirms his signs. This statement is part of the surviving Quran rather than a later biographical embellishment. The text identifies an interference and a divine correction. [3] [4]

On its own, however, the verse does not name the three goddesses, describe public prostration or say Muhammad personally spoke the controversial sentence. Those elements depend on external reports. The reasonable critical question is why the text speaks so explicitly about Satanic interference while not identifying the precise episode, if any, that occasioned the statement. [4]

05 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The verb tamanna and umniyya

The Arabic wording idha tamanna alqa al-shaytanu fi umniyyatihi has traditionally been understood in more than one way: when a messenger recited, or when he wished or desired. The root can involve wishing or recitation in usage discussed by interpreters. One cannot resolve this semantic question simply by choosing a preferred English translation. [3] [11]

The context is important: what Satan casts is removed, then God's ayat are established. An interpretation focused on recitation fits the narrative reports, while a reading focused on a prophet's desire permits a broader account of temptation. The existence of these possibilities does not prove either a particular historical interpolation or its impossibility. [11]

The Quran elsewhere instructs reciters to seek refuge in God from Satan before reading scripture. That injunction is compatible with a threat being recognised and resisted; it does not specify whether a prophet could inadvertently voice intrusive words in public. Quran 16:98-100 therefore supplies additional context, but no independent record of this episode. The semantic analysis should begin with the actual Arabic verb and nominal form, not with a later translation selected to settle a historical debate. [3] [24]

06 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The significance of nasakha

Quran 22:52 uses fa-yansakhu Allahu ma yulqi al-shaytan, followed by thumma yuhkimu Allahu ayatihi. The first expression can communicate cancelling or removing what Satan casts; the second establishes or makes firm God's signs. The sequence, not a simple denial of interference, is the text's stated response. [3] [4]

A theological reconciliation can say that the final revelation was protected because the interference was eventually removed. But that is different from demonstrating that no false wording was ever publicly heard as revelation. The gap between initial perception and later correction is precisely what a historical investigation must examine. [4]

07 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Why 22:53-54 matters

The following verses say the Satanic insertion becomes a test for people with diseased or hardened hearts, and that people given knowledge may recognise the truth of God's revelation. This framing treats the interference as something with consequences for an audience rather than merely a private temptation invisible to everyone. [4] [5]

It is not legitimate to convert the test language into proof that the gharaniq story happened exactly as later narrated. Still, the continuation makes a purely generic account of fleeting, consequence-free temptation less persuasive unless the alternative can explain what is being made a public test and how God's signs are being confirmed. [5]

08 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Quran 17:73-75 and attempted compromise

These verses say that opponents nearly tempted Muhammad to attribute something other than what had been revealed, and that he might have been taken as a close friend if he had done so. The passage also says he would have faced severe punishment without divine strengthening. It reports pressure and divine protection, but does not confess that he fabricated revelation. [6] [7]

The story told by al-Tabari links the alleged praise of pagan deities to a desire for accommodation with Meccan opponents. That is a possible intertextual connection, not a proof that the words in 17:73-75 were composed in reaction to that exact incident. Cause and sequence require separate evidence. [7]

09 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Quran 69:44-47 and accountability

The Quran presents a severe hypothetical warning against Muhammad attributing fabricated speech to God. Those verses are often cited as a safeguard, but a threatened consequence does not by itself establish that a particular historical mistake never occurred. A warning and an alleged temptation can coexist as claims within the same religious text. [8]

The more precise issue is whether a publicly heard praise of pagan intercessors, if uttered under mistaken inspiration, would count as ascribing false speech to God. That question goes beyond grammar to the account's theology. It cannot be answered by calling the alleged incident either impossible or certain without first evaluating the record.

10 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Al-Tabari's direct Arabic testimony

In his commentary on Quran 22:52, al-Tabari explicitly introduces an explanation that Satan placed on Muhammad's tongue material not revealed by God. He then preserves multiple attributed narratives, including one from Muhammad ibn Kab al-Qurazi and another transmitted through Ibn Ishaq. These are concrete literary witnesses to the existence of the story. [11] [12]

The writer died in 923 CE, centuries after the events he narrates. His preservation of chains and variant versions is valuable historical evidence about the tradition's circulation, but his compilation is not an independent eyewitness account. One must inspect both the narrative wording and its transmission rather than count every chain as a new observer. [12]

11 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Ibn Ishaq and Ibn Hisham distinction

An attributed Ibn Ishaq version survives within al-Tabari's chain, while the familiar Ibn Hisham redaction of Ibn Ishaq's biography omits the episode. It is historically misleading to say simply that Ibn Ishaq universally included it, or that its absence from Ibn Hisham proves Ibn Ishaq never transmitted it. [11] [15]

The surviving witnesses are mediated and editorially different. Comparing them can reveal how an awkward report was received and transmitted, but cannot recover a lost autograph without further evidence. This issue belongs to the history of early Muslim memory, not to an imagined uninterrupted quotation of a first-generation diary. [15]

12 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Reported Meccan prostration

Al-Tabari's account links the disputed formula to a communal prostration at the end of Surat al-Najm and says some Meccans believed their deities had received acknowledgement. It also relates rumours affecting emigrants who were in Abyssinia. These details form a coherent narrative explanation of an unexpected public reaction. [11] [13]

Narrative coherence is not enough to authenticate the story. The timing of reported migrations, why different groups prostrated and which versions name particular participants require a controlled comparison of reports. The act of prostration has another conceivable explanation: the chapter ends by calling its audience to bow to God. [13] [14]

13 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

What Bukhari actually attests

Sahih al-Bukhari 1070 and 1071 transmit reports that Muhammad recited Surat al-Najm and that others, including pagans according to one version, prostrated. These reports do not reproduce the gharaniq approval words. Their relevance is therefore to the shared-prostration tradition, not direct authentication of a Satanic sentence. [13] [14]

Treating the Bukhari passages as though they contain the controversial interpolation would be an avoidable factual error. Conversely, ignoring their public-prostration motif would omit a potentially relevant point of comparison. Repeated retellings about prostration must also be tested for dependence rather than automatically multiplied into independent witnesses. [14]

14 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The earliest report dates and attribution

Modern scholarship traces versions of the story to named early Muslim transmitters, including figures assigned to the first two Islamic centuries. Shahab Ahmed's substantial source study argues that the episode was accepted within important early Muslim milieus before later rejection became dominant. This establishes a contested history of reception. [15] [16]

Dates assigned to attributed narrators are not the same as dates of surviving documents. A report copied in a later collection might preserve earlier material, but that conclusion requires examining the chain, textual family and possible editorial shaping. Attributing a story to a Companion does not automatically make it contemporary testimony. [16]

15 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Shahab Ahmed's argument

Before Orthodoxy, published in 2017, identifies many versions of the incident and analyses their chains and wording. Ahmed contests the assumption that Muslim denial was always universal and treats acceptance of the incident as part of early Muslim memory. This is serious specialised research and cannot simply be waved away as hostile polemic. [15] [16]

His reconstruction remains a scholarly argument about early transmission and interpretation, not a recovered independent recording of the Meccan event. It is possible to accept that early communities transmitted and believed the episode while suspending judgement about its precise historical occurrence. That distinction is central to this investigation's verdict. [16]

16 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Scholarly counterarguments and limits

A sceptical explanation notes that later writers may have constructed the story to account for Quran 22:52, explain pagan prostration or dramatise divine correction. Specific chains may be interrupted, dependent or vulnerable to later attribution. These concerns cannot be dismissed merely because the narrative would be damaging to theological claims. [11] [13]

On the other hand, the incident's awkwardness and circulation in numerous early-attributed versions are facts that require explanation rather than categorical denial. An embarrassing report is not automatically true: stories can develop for exegetical or polemical reasons. Their earliest independent textual attestations and transmission relationships remain the decisive questions. [13] [15]

17 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Patricia Crone and the structure of sura 53

Patricia Crone's 2015 study Problems in sura 53 examines difficulties in the composition and speaker of the chapter and tentatively proposes an earlier oracular source for part of it. The study is relevant to textual and literary analysis, but its abstract does not demonstrate that the Satanic Verses incident happened as reported. [17]

It would be inaccurate to present Crone's distinct thesis as a proof of al-Tabari's narrative. Criticism of a chapter's structure, an argument for pre-existing material and evidence of the gharaniq report are overlapping research questions with different burdens. A sound article keeps the scholarly proposals in their correct categories.

18 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Why the received Quran rejects the episode's wording

Verses 53:21-23 mock the attribution of daughters to God and describe the named deities as lacking divine authority. Quran 53:26 also limits even angelic intercession to divine permission. These are not affirmations that al-Lat, al-Uzza and Manat possess effective intercession; the received passage repudiates such an idea. [1] [9]

The contrast is decisive for identifying the nature of the controversy. The allegation is about an earlier, removed utterance, not a contradiction between the surviving anti-idolatry verses and an extant pro-idolatry verse. Those who claim a current Quranic variant preserves the praise must produce a specific manuscript or authorised reading. [9] [23]

The surrounding Quran also rejects unauthorised intercessors in 39:43-45 and insists on exclusive worship in chapter 109. Those are additional controls against projecting the alleged praise formula into the received corpus. The dispute is over a purported temporary lapse, not over whether present Islamic theology permits worship of these deities. Identifying this boundary makes the critique more precise and prevents a defender from rebutting a claim that the investigation has not actually made. [1] [22] [23]

19 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Is a temporary correction sufficient?

One defence says that any Satanic insertion was short-lived and immediately corrected by God, preserving the final text. This directly addresses final-text integrity, but leaves open the epistemic question faced by an original audience: what reliable external criterion distinguished a false prophetic utterance before the later correction arrived? [3] [11]

If the alleged words were proclaimed as revelation, then later removal would not erase the earlier lapse in discernment. If the words were merely overheard from another speaker or never spoken, the force of that objection changes. The competing historical possibilities must be assessed, not conflated. [11]

20 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Biblical tests of alleged revelation

Deuteronomy 13:1-5 tests even a wonder-working religious claimant against fidelity to the true God. Jeremiah 23 criticises those speaking visions from their own hearts rather than God's mouth. Galatians 1:8 rejects even an angel announcing a rival gospel; 1 John 4 urges testing spirits. These texts establish an earlier Biblical standard for evaluating revelation claims. [18] [19]

A Biblical-Christian critique may therefore argue that a prophet's alleged endorsement of pagan intercessors would violate that standard. This is a conditional theological assessment. It should not be disguised as independent proof that the alleged Meccan utterance occurred, and the earlier Biblical texts should not be misquoted as direct comments on Muhammad. [19] [20] [21]

21 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Evidence comparison

The surviving Quran names the three deities only to reject them and speaks in 22:52 of Satan casting something subsequently removed. Al-Tabari preserves narratives that make the interference a specific public utterance. Bukhari preserves the prostration motif without the incriminating formula. Modern historians evaluate these distinct pieces of evidence. [1] [3]

Each source bears a different weight: primary scripture establishes the canonical wording; later narrative collections establish that accounts circulated; a historical critic must reconstruct when and how those accounts arose. No number of late retellings automatically becomes an eyewitness witness to the alleged event. [3] [11] [13]

The evidence matrix is asymmetric. Quran 53 has very high evidentiary value for the wording now transmitted, but almost none for whether unrecorded words were spoken before it reached that form. Quran 22:52 is direct evidence for a doctrinal description of Satanic interference, though it leaves the episode unspecified. Al-Tabari is strong evidence for an attributed account and weak direct evidence for the reported event. Bukhari attests a prostration story, not the interpolation. Ahmed evaluates transmission history rather than supplying a seventh-century witness. [1] [3] [11] [13] [15]

22 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The strongest alternative explanations

One proposal is that the episode substantially happened and later theology suppressed its acceptance. Another is that Quran 22:52 and an otherwise unexplained communal prostration encouraged an exegetical legend. A third proposes some genuine early memory that was reshaped in transmission. None is demonstrated solely by internal coherence. [3] [11]

The first explanation needs reliable transmission to the event and ideally independent contemporary evidence. The second needs an account of the motif's formation and variants. The third requires identification of older core material within later elaboration. A claim of Satanic intervention is a supernatural addition beyond the existence of a human utterance. [11] [15] [16]

23 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Text-first and historical verdicts

Text-first verdict: the current Quran does not contain praise of the three pagan goddesses, but Quran 22:52 explicitly discusses Satan's interference and God's subsequent cancellation. The canonical passages create a meaningful theological problem about how claims of revelation can be distinguished when false material is alleged. [1] [3]

Historical verdict: early Muslim literary tradition contains substantial, directly attested versions of the Satanic Verses story. Whether Muhammad actually recited the disputed formula remains unproved by independent eyewitness evidence. The site's critique should not inflate a historically debated report into certainty, nor describe it as a modern anti-Islamic invention. [3] [11] [15]

The evidentiary verdict does not depend on declaring al-Tabari a fraud, the received Quran an approval of idolatry or Bukhari a direct Satanic-Verses witness. None is accurate. A strong critique can instead ask how an audience could discriminate genuine revelation from an allegedly inspired false utterance, and why early transmitters retained a story subsequently rejected as theologically intolerable. Those questions have clear textual and historiographical foundations even when the event's historicity remains disputed. [3] [11] [15]

24 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Biblical verdict and falsification

Biblical verdict: if the episode occurred as al-Tabari's account alleges, publicly praising pagan intercession as revelation would fail the earlier Biblical tests of prophetic truth. Independently of that conditional, the history of early acceptance and later rejection presents a significant challenge to claims of an uncontested memory of perfect discernment. [1] [3]

Falsification criteria: a demonstrably early manuscript containing the disputed formula as Quranic text would substantially change the textual claim. An independent, securely dated account of the Meccan event could strengthen its historicity. Evidence of common-source dependence among all purported witnesses would weaken arguments based on their apparent multiplicity. [3] [18] [20]

25 / PRIMARY SOURCES AND SCHOLARSHIP

References and transmission controls

Quranic passages attest the received text. Al-Tabari and Bukhari attest attributed traditions. Later scholarship assesses those reports, but is not a contemporary witness to the event.
