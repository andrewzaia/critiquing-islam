The crucifixion: who died?

Quran 4:157 and earlier Christian and Roman records do not make the same claim about Jesus' death.

01 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The sources do not describe the same event in the same way

KEY TEXTUAL FINDING

The dominant traditional reading of Quran 4:157 denies that Jesus was crucified; the earliest surviving Christian writings affirm that he died. This is a real disagreement between textual traditions. Deciding what happened historically requires assessing their dates, genres and evidentiary value.

Quran 4:157 attributes the disputed claim to particular speakers. The text must therefore be examined for what it denies about those speakers, and separately for what it says about the historical event. Those are related but distinct questions.

02 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

What exactly does Quran 4:157 deny?

The verse reports the claim that certain speakers killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, and then rejects that claim, saying they did not kill or crucify him. Quran 4:158 continues by stating that God raised him. [1]

In context, the disputed boast belongs to the speakers criticised in the preceding passage. That is not the same thing as saying the Christian Gospel text makes the boast. This distinction is a legitimate textual observation, and it should not be conflated with the separate question of Jesus' actual death.

There has been a long dominant Muslim interpretation denying Jesus' crucifixion. Scholarly discussion also records minority readings that restrict the denial to the speakers' agency, certainty or claims rather than denying the event in every sense. [5] [6] Those minority arguments exist but do not erase the explicit negative formulation of 4:157.

03 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The death of Jesus is already central to Paul's testimony

In 1 Corinthians 15:3-4, Paul identifies Jesus' death, burial and subsequent resurrection as the content of a tradition he had received and handed on. [2] This letter belongs to the first century, several centuries before the Quran.

Mark 15 narrates Jesus' crucifixion and death in detail. [3] These are Christian texts, so their theological convictions must be recognised, but their agreement on the crucifixion also establishes that it was not a late Christian belief invented after Islam appeared.

The claim that Jesus died by crucifixion and the claim that he rose from the dead are distinct historical propositions. Evidence for the early belief in the former does not itself establish the latter.

04 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Tacitus separately reports an execution under Pilate

The Roman historian Tacitus, writing in the early second century, reports that Christus suffered the extreme penalty while Tiberius ruled and Pontius Pilate held authority in Judaea. [4]

His statement is later than Paul's but earlier than Islam. It is a non-Christian literary source, adding an important line of attestation. Historians must still consider what Tacitus knew independently and what information might have reached him indirectly; the passage is not a surviving first-hand Roman execution record.

05 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Distinguishing a denial of agency from a denial of crucifixion

One interpretive possibility argues that 4:157 denies the boast of particular speakers who were not the Roman authorities who executed Jesus. The objection is that the verse also contains a denial of crucifixion, not merely a denial of authorship of the act. That is why the traditional non-crucifixion reading has been so influential. [5]

Other scholars have proposed contextual readings that avoid an unconditional denial of Jesus' death. [6] Those readings merit examination where linguistic evidence supports them, but cannot be asserted as the plain, historically uncontested meaning of the passage.

What does not resolve the question

Saying simply that a later revelation overrides an earlier account is a theological commitment, not independent historical evidence. Conversely, the existence of an earlier text is not an automatic guarantee that every detail in it is historically accurate.

06 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

What 4:157 actually says

Quran 4:157 denies that the speakers under criticism killed or crucified Jesus, using the phrase shubbiha lahum about what appeared to them. Quran 4:158 says God raised him. The verse does not name any replacement victim. Some translations specify another person through interpretation; others simply describe the appearance. [1] [7]

Claims that Judas, a disciple, Simon of Cyrene or a pursuer was crucified in Jesus' place require material beyond the words of the Quran. The distinction between a scriptural denial and a later reconstructed event is central to the historical test.

07 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Competing accounts of the supposed substitute

Islamic exegetical accounts vary. Ma'arif al-Quran describes both a disciple volunteering to resemble Jesus and a pursuer being made to resemble him. It acknowledges that the Quran does not determine which reported scenario happened. Ibn Kathir also provides a substitution explanation. [8] [9]

These writings document the development of interpretations, not contemporary Roman evidence of a mistaken identity. Disagreement over the alleged substitute does not logically disprove the possibility of a switch, but it leaves the hypothesis without a stable, historically documented identity for the victim.

Necessary distinction

The Quran denies the event as traditionally understood. The detailed story of an imposter is an interpretation, not an independently verified ancient execution report.

08 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Crucifixion was proclaimed within decades

Paul's mid-first-century letters establish how early the crucifixion was central to Christian teaching. He describes a received tradition that Jesus died and was buried in 1 Corinthians 15:3-4, preaches the crucified Messiah in 1 Corinthians 1:18-24, and refers to Jesus Christ crucified in 1 Corinthians 2:2. Galatians 3:1 and 6:14 and Philippians 2:8 likewise refer to the cross. [2] [10] [11] [12] [13]

Paul was not an eyewitness to the execution, so this is evidence for early transmission of a belief rather than direct observation. But that is historically consequential. A narrative invented hundreds of years after the event cannot account for explicit crucifixion claims in letters already being circulated only decades later.

09 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Four Gospels, but not four independent eyewitness statements

Mark 15 identifies Jesus as the person crucified under Roman authority. Matthew 27, Luke 23 and John 19 also recount Jesus being executed, not an unidentified substitute. [3] [14] [15] [16]

Matthew and Luke depend substantially on Mark for material, so the sources should not be counted as four fully independent historical depositions. The independence of John's Passion tradition is debated. Nevertheless the Christian literary evidence consistently identifies the crucified individual as Jesus, and the earliest Pauline references precede these Gospel narratives.

Simon of Cyrene appears in Mark as a person compelled to carry the cross, not the person executed instead of Jesus. A different role for Simon would require separate evidence.

10 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

External sources and the problem of Josephus

Tacitus, writing in the early second century, reports that Christus was executed during Tiberius' reign under Pontius Pilate. This is a later non-Christian witness, but it is not an extant Roman court record and his underlying informants cannot be established beyond doubt. [4]

Josephus' Antiquities 18.63-64 contains a reference to Pilate condemning Jesus to the cross, but some wording appears to have been altered by Christians and scholars disagree on the original scope of the passage. His generally accepted mention of James as brother of Jesus called Christ in Antiquities 20.200 does not itself narrate the crucifixion. [17] [18]

A responsible argument does not inflate these sources into first-hand eyewitnesses. They offer supplementary historical context, while the main early evidence remains in the first-century Christian material.

11 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Early Christians disputed apparent suffering

Ignatius of Antioch, in the early second century, insists that Jesus was truly crucified under Pilate. His letters to the Smyrnaeans and Trallians reject beliefs that Christ merely appeared to suffer. [19] [20]

These letters demonstrate both that Jesus' real physical crucifixion was firmly taught and that alternative apparent-suffering ideas already existed. They are not direct eyewitness accounts. The existence of an early controversy does not establish that the alternative account has equal evidentiary support.

12 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

A substitution story really did predate Islam

Irenaeus, writing in the late second century, attributes to a Basilidean teaching the claim that Simon of Cyrene was made to look like Jesus and crucified instead, while Jesus escaped. [21]

This is a crucial qualification: the idea of substitution did not first appear in Islam. But Irenaeus is hostilely reporting another group's belief, not witnessing the first-century execution. His text establishes an early rival explanation, not a documented alternate victim. Similarity to later Islamic substitution accounts cannot alone prove direct literary borrowing.

13 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Pre-Islamic codices preserve the Gospel crucifixion narrative

Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus are fourth-century Greek biblical manuscripts, copied centuries before the Quran. Their surviving Gospels contain narratives of Jesus being crucified. Sinaiticus preserves the complete New Testament. [22] [23]

These manuscripts cannot prove every account is historically accurate or recover all original first-century wording. They do establish that the Christian crucifixion narrative was available in writing centuries before Muhammad and was not invented to answer a later Quranic denial.

17 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Read both Arabic negations in Quran 4:157 without importing a substitute

Quran 4:157 says وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ, literally that they did not kill him and did not crucify him. The Arabic verbs qataluhu and salabuhu refer to killing and crucifying, and the plural verbal subjects refer back to the group whose boast is under criticism. The Quranic Arabic Corpus confirms both negative clauses and their morphology. [1] [30]

The words cannot simply be rewritten as "the Romans killed him but the Jews did not" without acknowledging that this restricts the negation to the boasters' agency. That reading is grammatically possible because the plural subject does identify particular speakers; it is not equivalent to the traditional claim that nobody crucified Jesus. The next clause, however, makes the passage more than a bare allocation of responsibility. [1] [5] [6]

A fair test therefore separates the textual observation, which is secure, from the two rival historical claims: no execution occurred, or an execution occurred but not by the people claiming credit for it. The first conflicts with multiple much earlier sources; the second can avoid that conflict, but must be defended from the Quranic context rather than introduced as a foregone conclusion. [30] [31]

18 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The passive appearance clause does not name a victim

The phrase وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمْ uses a passive form-II perfect verb, shubbiha, together with lahum, to them. The Quranic Arabic Corpus identifies the passive and the recipient pronoun. The words do not explicitly say that Judas, Simon of Cyrene or a volunteer was physically transformed into Jesus. [30] [31]

The text then condemns disputants who lack knowledge and follow conjecture. Its closing وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ يَقِينًا repeats the denial with yaqinan. The grammar can be discussed in relation to certainty, but it does not independently identify who died or provide a Roman execution record. Quran 4:158 declares that God raised Jesus, which is an additional theological assertion rather than evidence from the scene. [1] [31]

Treating the passive as a complete mechanism of a miraculous body swap supplies information absent from its Arabic. To defend a swap historically would require a dateable witness, a named victim, an account of identification and an explanation of why the early communities consistently named Jesus. Later exegetical alternatives do not satisfy those requirements merely by repeating a possible story. [8] [9]

19 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Quran 4:159 and other Jesus passages cannot be omitted

Quran 4:159 follows the denial and refers to the People of the Book believing in him before his death. The antecedent of the possessive pronoun and the chronology of the belief have been interpreted differently. The verse does not directly provide a first-century witness to the execution, but excluding it from any literary analysis of 4:157-158 makes the immediate context incomplete. [43]

Quran 3:55 uses wording concerning God taking Jesus and raising him; 5:117 refers to Jesus being taken, and 19:33 refers to death and raising. These passages generate real interpretation debates about death, ascension and future events. None can be treated as though it straightforwardly identifies the supposed person crucified in Jesus' place. [26] [28] [29]

A model in which Jesus was crucified by Roman authorities but the speakers of Quran 4:157 were wrong to claim they killed him can be made consistent with early historical evidence. Whether it best explains the Quran's rhetoric is an independent Arabic interpretive question. A model of no historical crucifixion faces the much stronger chronological objections documented below. [1] [6] [43]

20 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Greek of 1 Corinthians 15 establishes a received death tradition

In 1 Corinthians 15:3 Paul uses παρέδωκα (I handed on) and παρέλαβον (I received), then states Χριστὸς ἀπέθανεν, Christ died. The accompanying verse says he was buried. The Greek formulation explicitly identifies an earlier tradition Paul claims to have received, not a belief first devised by medieval Gospel copyists. [2] [32]

The letter is commonly dated to the middle of the first century, several decades after the event. The precise date when every clause of the formula was first used is disputed and cannot be proved down to an individual year. But Paul's own first-century letter is already compelling evidence that the death and burial narrative belonged to the earliest recoverable Christian generation. [2] [10] [38]

This does not establish the supernatural resurrection narrated in the following clauses. The death, burial and alleged resurrection are distinguishable assertions. Nor does receiving a tradition make Paul an eyewitness to the execution. The strength of the historical argument comes from the early attestation combined with later and non-Christian material, rather than treating a received creed as a contemporary autopsy. [32] [38]

21 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Paul knew earlier Jerusalem leaders, but did not personally see the execution

In Galatians 1:18-19 Paul says that he visited Cephas and met James, identified as the Lord's brother. This is an important first-person testimony that Paul had contact with figures from the earlier Jerusalem movement, strengthening the relevance of his received traditions. [33]

The passage does not transcribe a conversation in which Cephas or James individually described standing at the cross. To infer that every detail of Paul's death account was independently cross-examined by those men goes beyond the text. What it does show is access to early participants rather than dependence on a single Christian text composed centuries later. [2] [33]

A theory that the whole crucifixion story emerged as a seventh-century innovation or a late medieval interpolation cannot accommodate Paul's first-century testimony. A theory of mistaken identification within the first century remains logically possible but must explain the absence of a comparably early independent substitute tradition. [1] [33]

22 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Papyrus 46 is an early physical witness to Paul, not his autograph

Papyrus 46, an early Greek codex of Pauline letters catalogued by the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts, is usually dated around 200 CE, with a palaeographic range in the late second or early third century. Its surviving Pauline material provides a manuscript witness well before Islam and before the fourth-century complete biblical codices. [34]

The manuscript's date is not the date Paul's letter was composed, and the original first-century autograph does not survive. Its historical use is to establish that the earlier written tradition was present before Islam and that its wording can be examined against later Greek manuscripts. Manuscript attestation is not independent eyewitness testimony to the original death. [34] [22] [23]

For the crucifixion in a narrative Gospel, the Codex Sinaiticus Project provides direct images and transcription of Mark 15 in a fourth-century codex. Its pages depict Pilate handing Jesus over and Jesus being crucified; the testimony is materially earlier than Quran 4:157, even though the fourth-century manuscript is later than the Gospel's first-century composition. [35]

23 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Tacitus gives Roman corroboration while preserving real source limits

Annals 15.44 places the execution of Christus under Tiberius during Pontius Pilate's government. Tacitus is a Roman writer describing Christians with hostility, not a Christian evangelist seeking to establish resurrection theology. The Latin passage is therefore a significant distinct attestation of the execution tradition around the early second century. [4] [44]

Its limits must be kept visible. Tacitus does not name his original informant or provide an extant Roman court document from the trial. His use of the administrative title procurator rather than the earlier prefect is noted in the Dickinson College commentary. That anachronistic title does not make his whole report fictitious, but it prevents treating the wording as direct verbatim archival minutes. [4]

The appropriate judgement is independent literary corroboration of a Roman execution tradition, not proof that Tacitus personally saw Jesus executed. The explanation that the Romans executed Jesus fits both his report and the Gospel narratives without a speculative substitution mechanism. [37] [38]

24 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Pilate's inscription corroborates the governor, not a trial transcript

A Latin dedication found at Caesarea Maritima names Pontius Pilate as prefect of Judaea. Its physical inscription provides independent material evidence that the named Roman official existed and occupied the office relevant to the historical setting. It also clarifies why Tacitus' later title differs from the governor's contemporary designation. [39]

The inscription does not mention Jesus, a trial, a prisoner exchange or an execution. It would be unsound to state that the stone proves the Gospel scene word for word, or that it establishes a substitute victim. It confirms an important part of the political and administrative context. [39] [4]

Helen Bond's study of Pilate in history and interpretation underscores that the primary texts give different depictions of the governor's temperament and actions. A historically supported execution under Pilate need not require accepting every reported dialogue or scene of his handwashing. [37]

25 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

A first-century crucifixion victim establishes Roman practice, not Jesus' body

The remains of a man commonly called Yehohanan, recovered from a first-century Jerusalem ossuary, include a heel bone penetrated by a nail. The Israel Antiquities Authority collection record associates the find with Roman-period crucifixion. The archaeological context independently demonstrates that this kind of execution occurred in Judaea. [40]

This skeleton is not identified as Jesus, and no responsible source-critical argument should suggest otherwise. The object is evidence of a historical execution method and archaeological context; it neither confirms nor denies the Gospel's narrative of a particular victim. [40]

The broader historical evaluation combines the known Roman technique, an attested governor, first-century Christian writing and a later hostile Roman report. The converging picture makes Jesus' execution significantly more probable than a body-swap story without a comparable contemporary textual or physical witness. [37] [38] [40]

26 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Ignatius explicitly rejects the appearance-only explanation

Ignatius' Letter to the Smyrnaeans 1-2, in its Greek textual tradition, asserts that Jesus was truly affixed to a cross under Pilate. It also rejects the view that the suffering occurred only in appearance. The original Greek can be compared with the transmitted English translation rather than accepting a modern paraphrase at face value. [19] [36]

Ignatius is a Christian writer defending his community against rival understandings of Jesus' body. He is not an eyewitness to the events under Pilate. But his early-second-century insistence confirms that real suffering and rival appearance-only views were already subjects of Christian dispute well before the Quran. [19] [20]

It is important not to equate every docetic interpretation with a substitution narrative. Saying Jesus only appeared to suffer can differ substantially from saying another named man was physically executed while Jesus watched. The distinctions matter when assessing whether a historical alternative has a specific independent pedigree. [36] [42]

27 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Basilidean substitution and the Gospel of Peter are not confirmations of a switch

Irenaeus, in Against Heresies 1.24.4, attributes to followers of Basilides a story in which Simon of Cyrene was transfigured and crucified instead of Jesus. This is an actual pre-Islamic attestation of the substitute idea. It corrects any careless assertion that no substitution story existed before the Quran. [21]

Yet the attribution was written by a critic of Basilides in the late second century. It is evidence for a reported rival teaching, not a first-century identification of Simon's body or a contemporary Roman execution certificate. There is no identified independent eyewitness record corroborating the swap. Its similarity to a later Islamic explanation also does not prove that the Quran borrowed through a particular identifiable intermediary. [21]

The non-canonical Gospel of Peter, probably from the second century, relocates responsibility for Jesus' execution in striking ways, including stronger blame of Herod. But as the Cambridge scholarly edition notes, it still recounts Jesus' trial, death and resurrection, rather than furnishing an eyewitness record that Simon of Cyrene was crucified instead. Earlier sectarian plurality should not be confused with equal historical evidence. [42]

28 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Roman responsibility must not become collective blame against Jews

Crucifixion under Pilate was a Roman execution. The Quran's wording concerns the boast of a particular group, while different Christian writings offer different representations of priestly elites, crowds and Roman authorities. A discussion of the historicity of Jesus' death must not convert those accounts into collective guilt assigned to Jewish people. [1] [3] [4]

J. Christopher Edwards' study of ancient Christian representations of Jesus' execution documents how Christian authors at times shifted responsibility from Roman executioners towards Jewish groups. That history warrants explicit source criticism of blame narratives, not avoidance of the independently supported fact of a Roman crucifixion. [41]

The appropriate test asks which political authority conducted the execution, how early each source is, and which details are later theological elaborations. It does not justify attributing guilt to a contemporary population on the basis of its religion or ancestry. [37] [41]

29 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

An actual alternative execution history requires positive early evidence

A substitution claim could be strengthened by a first-century account identifying a different executed person, an independent record of a custody or identity switch, or a demonstrably early textual stream preserving the alternative. No such evidence has been established in the surviving records examined here. Later named-substitute traditions are possible scenarios, not demonstrated reconstructions. [21] [35]

An agency-only reading of Quran 4:157 does not require any body swap and can be historically compatible with execution by Roman soldiers. The necessary test then becomes textual: can the narrower understanding naturally account for both the negative clauses and the appearance language? The strongest traditional reading still faces the earlier death evidence; the narrower reading deserves a real linguistic test rather than dismissal as mere apologetics. [30] [31]

A divine rescue is not an event ordinary historical sources could rule out as metaphysically impossible. But absent an independent witness, the claim cannot outweigh the earlier Christian and Roman convergence on a more ordinary execution. The conclusion concerns the best-supported historical proposition, not proof that all miracles are logically impossible. [37] [38]

The physical copies of early Christian texts establish that the Christian account was already in circulation before Islam. The conclusion is about historical probability of execution, not a historical proof that resurrection occurred, nor a claim that every Gospel detail is beyond criticism. Separating those propositions makes the critique more defensible. [32] [37]

31 / TEXT AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Which conclusions survive source criticism?

It is historically demonstrable that Christian testimony to Jesus' death predates the Quran by centuries. Paul, the Gospel of Mark and Tacitus offer distinct records of the early death tradition, while Quran 4:157 became the basis for a dominant Islamic denial of crucifixion.

These sources are not identical in date or purpose, and the evidence is not mathematically conclusive. Nevertheless, a historical explanation denying the crucifixion must account for the earlier, converging testimony rather than simply assume it is wrong because a later text disagrees.

Historical verdict, on the balance of probabilities: Jesus' execution under Pontius Pilate is much better supported by chronologically earlier sources than any claim of a substituted victim. Paul's first-century received death tradition, the Gospel Passion narratives, second-century Roman notice and pre-Islamic manuscripts converge on Jesus as the crucified person. The Quran's negative formulation lacks a named replacement or independently documented switch. A narrow reading denying only the specific boasters' agency remains a possible reconciliation, but it is not evidence for the traditional historical assertion that nobody crucified Jesus. [2] [4] [30] [34]

Verdict: The traditional non-crucifixion claim fails the historical evidence test. Jesus' execution by crucifixion under Pilate is supported by substantially earlier and converging sources; the alleged substitute has no comparable positive historical attestation. The Quran itself names no imposter.

EVIDENCE CLASSIFICATIONVery strong historical attestation; substitution not establishedEarly sources support the crucifixion; the traditional Quranic reading denies it.
