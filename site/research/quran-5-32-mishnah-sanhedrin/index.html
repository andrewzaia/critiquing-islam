01 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

A distinctive parallel, not merely the same moral principle

THE QUESTION UNDER REVIEW

Quran 5:32 equates killing one person with killing all humanity and preserving one person with preserving all humanity. Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 presents a closely corresponding maxim immediately after its discussion of Cain and Abel. Does this evidence support an earlier Jewish source, and what would be needed to prove a particular act of copying or an independent divine decree? [1] [2] [7]

The shared ethical analogy is only part of the evidence. Both traditions link it to the first murder, the Mishnah interprets Abel's blood and potential descendants, and the Quran makes the maxim a divine ordinance upon the Children of Israel. Those contextual features give this comparison considerably more specificity than a general statement that life is valuable. [6] [7] [9]

This study distinguishes a demonstrable prior textual tradition from the historical route by which a later author or community received it. The former can be evidenced by extant texts and their chronology; the latter requires an identifiable transmission pathway. A source-critical argument should not claim the second merely because the first is compelling. [14] [15] [16]

02 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Quran's Arabic makes a claim about earlier legislation

Quran 5:32 begins min ajli dhalika katabna ala bani Isra'il. The causal phrase follows the first murder; the first-person verb states that a prescription was made upon the Children of Israel. This is not simply a universal ethical proverb. It is framed as the report of an earlier divine act, and its historical verifiability must be evaluated as such. The Quranic Arabic Corpus parses the relevant words rather than relying on an English paraphrase. [2] [4]

The analogy speaks of taking a nafs, a life or soul, in relation to al-nas jami'an, all humanity. The sentence also contains the conditions concerning a life for a life and corruption in the land. These are part of the original verse, not additions invented by later legal commentators. It would be a selective quotation to print the maxim without acknowledging the qualifiers. [4] [5]

The Children of Israel are the stated recipients of the decree, but the language of all humanity is used for the consequence of killing or preserving a person. One phrase does not erase the other. A reader must avoid turning an assertion about the group given a rule into a claim that only that group's members count as human lives. [2]

03 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Cain and Abel are already the Quranic setting

Quran 5:27-31 narrates the two sons of Adam offering sacrifices, one threatening murder, the other refusing to attack, the killing, and a crow scratching the earth as the killer learns what to do with the body. Verse 32 then opens with a causal transition to the decree. The moral maxim is deliberately attached to the earliest homicide narrative rather than placed in an unrelated legal chapter. [1]

The Quranic passage does not itself give the brothers the names Cain and Abel. Those are the names used in the Biblical narrative to which it is conventionally compared. Importing those names for convenience is reasonable when declared, but it must not become an assertion that they are literally present in the Arabic verses. [1] [23]

The crow episode differs from Genesis. Its existence does not show that the shared one-life analogy arose independently, nor does the analogy prove that every detail derives from the same rabbinic work. Different motifs can have different transmission histories. Pregill's specialised study is important precisely because the intertextual setting extends beyond one selected sentence. [14] [23]

04 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Genesis contains the murder, not the full one-life maxim

Genesis 4:8-10 tells of Cain murdering Abel and of Abel's blood crying from the ground. The Hebrew phrase for the blood is a plural form. It offers an interpretative opening for later Jewish discussion of consequences extending beyond the murdered individual. The Genesis narrative itself does not explicitly say that preserving one person is equivalent to preserving the entire world. [6] [23]

This distinction is decisive for identifying the type of source at issue. Genesis is an earlier narrative of homicide; Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 articulates the one-life, whole-world interpretation; Quran 5:32 preserves a related moral scale after the same narrative. It would be inaccurate to identify the full maxim as a verbatim quotation from Genesis, and equally inaccurate to pretend its rabbinic formulation began with the Quran. [6] [7] [9]

Genesis' account of Cain's punishment and protection is also not a detailed Israelite penal code. The Quran's subsequent attribution of a rule to the Children of Israel is therefore a different literary move whose historical status must be argued separately. [2] [23]

05 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The earlier maxim belongs to a capital-case witness warning

Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 addresses witnesses in capital trials. They are warned against rumor, speculation and indirect testimony. The text contrasts monetary loss, where restitution can be paid, with the irreversible consequences of a wrongful death. A falsely convicted person loses not only an existing life but the possible descendants of that life. [7] [8]

The passage then invokes Cain and Abel before drawing the analogy that destroying a single life is like destroying a whole world, and preserving a single life like preserving a whole world. This is an internally structured argument: the moral comparison grows out of the judiciary's responsibility to protect people from fatal errors. [7] [21]

Thus the earlier Jewish teaching is not merely a vague slogan. It has a procedural setting and an explanation linking one death to a lost future. The Quran preserves a similar conclusion while presenting it as an earlier divine decree. That shift of authority and function must be recognised in the comparison. [2] [7]

06 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Mishnah's argument explains the plural 'bloods'

In the Mishnah's explanation, Genesis' plural bloods can refer to Abel's own blood together with the blood of his unborn descendants. The potential lives destroyed by his killing explain how one murder can be treated as the destruction of a whole world. Another explanation associates the plural wording with blood scattered in multiple places. Both are interpretative readings of the Biblical phrase, not separate contemporary reports from the murder scene. [6] [7]

Lineage is the earlier explanation, not a replacement translation. The Mishnah links the plural Hebrew demei in Genesis 4:10 to Abel's blood and the blood of his potential descendants. This supports an explanation in terms of an extinguished family line, potentially extending across generations. However, Quran 5:32 says al-nas jami'an, literally all people or all humanity, not one generation or one's descendants. The earlier interpretative rationale and the Quran's actual wording must be kept distinct. The fact that a murder prevents possible descendants is also not a complete explanation of the saving-one-life half of the maxim, which operates as a moral analogy. [2] [6] [7]

The Quran's account does not reproduce this Hebrew grammatical argument. It states the analogy following the brothers' story without explaining the plural bloods. That difference is relevant to the form of transmission: a later text can adopt the conclusion of an earlier exposition without reproducing the derivation in full. [1] [2] [21]

The alignment of murder narrative, unusual world-sized value of one life and sequential positioning is strong evidence of engagement with a common interpretative tradition. Differences in language and legal context weaken a claim of verbatim copying but do not make the correspondence disappear. [7] [9] [14]

07 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Adam rationale complicates simplistic ethnic claims

Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 also argues that humanity began from one person. Among the purposes given are respect for individual lives and resistance to claims that one person's ancestor outranks another's. This larger context speaks to a shared human origin and should be read alongside the life-maxim when evaluating the passage's moral scope. [7] [21]

Some textual versions contain the qualification 'of Israel'; others do not. Even if a restrictive wording appears in a particular witness, it does not justify erasing the universalising Adam argument in the surrounding text. Conversely, a universal Adam rationale cannot by itself settle which precise version of the disputed sentence is earliest. [11] [12]

The Quran identifies Israel as the recipient of the alleged decree while evaluating killing through an all-humanity analogy. That is an observable combination of a particular addressee and a universal moral metaphor, not a contradiction resolved by selecting only half the verse. [2] [7]

08 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Mishnah's compilation belongs centuries before Islam

The Mishnah is generally associated with the editorial activity of Judah ha-Nasi near the beginning of the third century CE. Sefaria places the work around 190-230 CE. This is well before the seventh-century Quran and establishes a substantial chronological case for the Jewish formulation's literary priority. [10]

There is an important qualification. A work's formation and the age of its surviving complete copies are different things. The prominent Kaufmann and Parma manuscripts were copied centuries after the Mishnah's compilation, and its traditions passed through regional study, copying and commentary. Text-critical analysis must therefore account for variation instead of claiming that every letter in a late copy was present in the earliest editorial form. [13] [20]

The late date of a particular manuscript, however, is not proof that the teaching was created at that late date. To overturn the earlier composition history of the rabbinic maxim would require positive evidence of interpolation or later invention, not simply an observation that an autograph no longer survives. [10] [15]

09 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Corpus Coranicum records the primary intertext

Corpus Coranicum places the Hebrew Mishnah passage in direct conversation with Quran 5:32 and publishes an intertextual entry for the relationship. Its documentation is useful because it makes the Hebrew and Arabic material available for source criticism without requiring trust in a polemicist's selective English quotation. [9]

The research project also distinguishes texts from the world of the Quran from proven manuscript exemplars that a Quranic author personally handled. That is the methodological standard to apply: the texts show an earlier tradition relevant to the Quran's wording, not a recovered photograph of the exact manuscript or oral conversation through which the later formulation arose. [9] [16]

The direct source-level conclusion is that the earlier rabbinic maxim and its Cain-Abel setting have a powerful literary relationship to the Quranic passage. The reconstruction of where, when and by whom the wording was transmitted into the Quran's environment remains a separate historical question. [14] [15]

10 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Alternative Mishnah numbering must not be mistaken for new sources

The passage normally appears as Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 in Sefaria and many contemporary English references. Aaron Koller's 2025 peer-reviewed study gives the maxim as Sanhedrin 4:12, reflecting an alternative division or scholarly numbering convention. Researchers must match the words and editorial system rather than assume those numbers prove the existence of two independent ancient works. [7] [15]

For accessibility, this study uses the commonly linked 4:5 while noting the alternative 4:12. An apparent disagreement in scholarly numbering is not an objection to the underlying comparison. It is a bibliographic issue best resolved by explicit reference to the edition used. [9] [15]

11 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The wording 'of Israel' is genuinely contested

Some printed Mishnah and talmudic witnesses qualify the object of saving or destroying as a soul 'of Israel'. Other important witnesses present a more general formulation without those words. The German textual commentary and Hebrew manuscript discussion at Sefaria document this difference. We cannot responsibly declare one version original simply because it would sharpen a contrast with the Quran. [11] [12]

The Kaufmann and Parma traditions are central to this question, but their surviving manuscripts are medieval and have their own histories. Philological work shows meaningful differences between the transmission lines, a reason for controlled collation rather than uncritical preference. A thorough reconstruction needs the relevant original-language witnesses, not just one English translation. [13] [20]

Accordingly, the often-repeated assertion that the Quran uniquely universalised an originally Israelite-only maxim is not proved by quoting the restricted form. Equally, one cannot simply decree that no Jewish manuscript ever contained the restriction. Both overstatements fail the evidence standard. [2] [7] [11] [12]

12 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Related ideas do not amount to an exact literal translation

The Mishnah speaks of preserving or destroying a world, while the Quran uses the comparison of one human soul to all people. The distinction between the Hebrew world metaphor and Arabic all-humanity wording matters. Their ideas are recognisably related, but the passages are not identical sentences in different scripts. The Mishnah's judicial warning and the Quran's divine-decree framing also differ. [2] [4] [7] [9]

English translations create additional variation by choosing among terms such as preserve, sustain, save, revive or give life. The overlap must survive inspection of the original texts, not rely on conveniently matching modern glosses. Here the parallel survives at the conceptual and narrative levels without being an exact verbal reproduction. [5] [21]

It would therefore be inaccurate to say there is no borrowing relationship because the wording differs, just as it would be inaccurate to claim a perfect word-for-word reproduction. The most defensible description is a distinctive reworking of an earlier attested Jewish moral teaching. [9] [14]

13 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The Quran inserts legal exceptions not present in the maxim

Quran 5:32 excludes the taking of a life under the terms of another life and corruption in the land. These qualifications belong to the text itself. They are not necessarily equivalent to the Mishnah's rules of capital procedure, which have a different framing and do not present the same pair of conditions within this maxim. [2] [4] [7]

The exceptions do not establish that every possible accusation of corruption permits execution; the scope and procedure require interpretation. Nevertheless, a modern reader who quotes only the statement about saving humanity has omitted real legal language. Other Quranic passages discuss retaliation and protection of life, including 2:178, 5:45 and 17:33. They show that the Quran's life ethic forms part of a broader penal scheme. [17] [18] [19]

Quran 4:92-93 additionally distinguishes accidental killing from deliberate homicide. Those distinctions make it misleading to treat verse 5:32 as a complete standalone criminal code or an entirely exceptionless universal prohibition. [22]

14 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Verse 5:33 immediately turns to severe public-order penalties

Quran 5:33 sets out punishments for those presented as waging war against God and his messenger and causing corruption on earth, including execution, crucifixion, cross-amputation or exile. Verse 34 introduces a qualification for people who repent before apprehension. This immediate transition is relevant to any claim that the preceding verse offers a categorical nonviolent principle detached from legal sanctions. [3]

The existence of penalties does not by itself contradict a rule against wrongful killing. A meaningful argument instead asks how the term corruption is defined, who may establish guilt and which legal safeguards govern the application of punishment. The passages have to be read together without collapsing their categories. [2] [3] [17]

The resulting text-first observation is narrow but strong: the Quranic maxim is legally qualified and sits beside coercive penalties. Any public quotation that hides this surrounding language gives an incomplete account of what the Quran says. [3] [5]

15 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Shared tradition does not identify the precise intermediary

A written Mishnah on Muhammad's desk is not a prerequisite for a historical connection. Jewish teachings could circulate in oral instruction, debate and storytelling, and later audiences could restate them in Arabic. That is a possible route for memorable explanatory motifs. Yet a possible route should not be falsely advertised as a witnessed conversation or recovered document. [14] [16]

The analogy's prior attestation and the shared Cain-Abel setting remain evidence even without a named intermediary. The question is not whether every element is identical, but whether the combination is better accounted for by independent invention or engagement with an earlier available tradition. Pregill's and Koller's studies treat the wider Quranic and rabbinic context as relevant rather than arguing from a single detached slogan. [14] [15]

Claims that the Quran was taught this verse by a particular rabbi, or borrowed from a specific surviving codex, do not meet an independent proof requirement on the sources assembled here. The strong link is at the level of transmitted intellectual tradition. [9] [16]

16 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Modern research assesses both connection and literary adaptation

Michael Pregill's 2022 article analyses the Quranic sons-of-Adam narrative as part of a larger collection of rabbinic resonances. His abstract treats the single-life maxim as a conspicuous precursor while resisting a simplistic derivation of every detail, particularly the crow episode, from one midrash. This supports a stronger comparison because it tests the entire sequence and not only the attractive ethical phrase. [14]

Aaron Koller's 2025 peer-reviewed study examines Quranic citations or allusions to Mishnah material and treats 5:32 as evidence relevant to rabbinic teaching in seventh-century Arabia. A historian may find that argument persuasive, but a scholar's view is an interpretation of primary evidence, not an additional ancient eyewitness. [15]

Pregill's 2023 work argues that different Quranic engagements with Biblical and Jewish materials may require different models of reception. That caution is particularly useful here: evidence of a connected tradition is strong, while claiming a recoverable single mechanism of borrowing without source-specific evidence would outrun the record. [16]

17 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

The claim that the maxim was uniquely Quranic fails

The ethical equivalence of one life and an entire world is documented in the earlier Mishnah tradition alongside the Cain-Abel rationale. Therefore a claim that this distinctive image was invented or first articulated by Quran 5:32 does not meet a basic chronological test. The earlier source is not a hypothetical lost text; its received wording and legal setting can be inspected directly. [7] [10] [15]

This is not the same as disproving divine revelation in the abstract. A believer can assert that a later revelation confirms an earlier insight. But that theological hypothesis does not restore historical originality, prove a newly given command or demonstrate that the Quran's specific reformulation was independently revealed. Claims of uniqueness and claims of inspiration require different evidence. [2] [7]

The legitimate comparison instead asks what was retained and what changed: common first-murder narrative, shared life/world valuation, different literary purpose, and the Quran's explicit exceptions and reference to an earlier decree upon Israel. Such changes are analytically meaningful without demonstrating how they came about. [1] [2] [14]

19 / TEXTUAL AND HISTORICAL EVIDENCE

Text-first and historical verdicts

Text-first verdict: Quran 5:32 and Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 preserve a distinctive correspondence: killing or preserving one life is assessed on the scale of all humanity or an entire world, directly following Cain and Abel. The Quran presents the maxim as a divine decree addressed to Israel and incorporates explicit exceptions; the Mishnah presents it as an inference in a capital-witness warning. These are observable similarities and differences, not competing modern opinions. [2] [7] [9]

Historical verdict: The maxim is already documented in Jewish rabbinic tradition centuries before the Quran. A claim that the Quran first introduced this imagery is not supported by the textual record. Quranic engagement with an earlier Jewish formulation is a strong inference. The precise path of that engagement remains historically unverified on the available evidence. [10] [14] [15] [16]

Further evidence required: A controlled collation of the debated 'of Israel' reading, a securely dated Arabic or Aramaic intermediary or an independent record of a precise seventh-century transmission would sharpen the historical model. The Quran's theological attribution and the Mishnah's rabbinic authority each require their own evidential assessment; neither is validated by untested assertion. [11] [12] [13] [20]
